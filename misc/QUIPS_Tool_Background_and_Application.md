# **Comprehensive Evaluative Analysis of the Quality In Prognosis Studies Tool for Risk of Bias Assessment in Clinical Research**

The evolution of evidence-based clinical practice relies heavily on the synthesis of high-quality prognostic research. Prognostic factors—variables that predict the likely course and outcome of a disease—inform therapeutic strategies, patient counseling, and the stratification of individuals into risk-based management protocols.1 However, the utility of such research is often compromised by significant methodological variability and the presence of systematic errors, or biases, that distort the reported associations between predictors and outcomes.1 To address the historical lack of a standardized critical appraisal framework, the Quality In Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool was developed as a domain-based instrument for assessing the risk of bias in studies of prognostic factors.1 This report provides an exhaustive examination of the QUIPS framework, its development, the mechanics of its six core domains, its scoring criteria, and its application in contemporary meta-epidemiological contexts.

## **The Genesis and Methodological Evolution of the QUIPS Instrument**

The identification of a need for a specialized tool in prognosis research emerged from a growing realization among methodologists that instruments designed for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or diagnostic accuracy studies were insufficient for the unique longitudinal and observational nature of prognostic research.1 Preliminary research conducted prior to 2006 identified six fundamental areas of concern that frequently undermined the validity of prognostic findings: participation, attrition, prognostic factor measurement, confounding measurement, outcome measurement, and statistical analysis.1 These areas formed the conceptual blueprint for what would become the QUIPS tool.

The formalization of the QUIPS tool was an iterative process spearheaded by a working group of fourteen experts, including epidemiologists, statisticians, and clinicians.1 Utilizing a modified Delphi approach and nominal group techniques, this group collaborated to refine prompting items and establish a structured rubric for risk of bias assessment.1 The development focused initially on studies of low back pain, providing a concrete clinical context for testing the tool's utility.1 Through a series of facilitated workshops in 2006 and 2007, participants reached a consensus on the specific wording of prompting items designed to guide ratings of high, moderate, or low risk of bias.1

To validate the tool’s broader applicability, it was shared with eighty-three research teams across various medical disciplines, including rheumatology, cardiovascular disease, and kidney disease.1 Feedback was collected via a structured survey, with forty-three teams reporting on their experiences.1 The majority of these reviewers (74%) found reaching a consensus on risk judgments to be straightforward, with a median completion time of twenty minutes per study.1 Interrater agreement, measured by the $\\kappa$ statistic, varied across teams but showed a median of 0.75, suggesting substantial reliability when used by trained assessors.1

## **Architectural Foundations: The Six Domains of Potential Bias**

The QUIPS tool operates through a domain-based assessment rather than a summated quality score. This architectural choice reflects modern epidemiological understanding that different sources of bias can affect study results in divergent ways, and a single number cannot capture the nuance of internal validity.1 Each domain is designed to prompt the assessor to consider specific mechanisms by which systematic error might occur.

### **Domain 1: Study Participation and Selection Bias**

The Study Participation domain addresses the degree to which the study sample represents the target population of interest. Selection bias occurs when the relationship between the prognostic factor and the outcome differs between those who participated in the study and those who were eligible but did not participate.1 This is a frequent concern in studies conducted at tertiary referral centers, where patients may have more severe disease or different comorbidities than the general population.7

Assessors are prompted to evaluate the description of the source population, the sampling frame, recruitment methods, and the application of inclusion and exclusion criteria.1 A low risk of bias is generally assigned when the study recruits a consecutive or random sample of eligible patients who represent the broader population in terms of key baseline characteristics.1 Conversely, a high risk of bias is inferred if the participation rate is notably low or if the sample is highly selective, such as using a "convenience sample" that excludes patients with certain common comorbidities, thereby distorting the real-world association between the predictor and the outcome.1

| Participation Criteria | Signaling Question Focus |
| :---- | :---- |
| Population Source | Is the source population clearly defined by geography, time, and setting? 8 |
| Recruitment Method | Were participants recruited consecutively or randomly to prevent "cherry-picking"? 2 |
| Eligibility Criteria | Are inclusion/exclusion criteria explicitly stated and applied uniformly? 1 |
| Participation Rate | Was the proportion of eligible individuals who agreed to participate adequate (e.g., $\\ge 80\\%$)? 2 |

### **Domain 2: Study Attrition and Informative Censoring**

Study attrition evaluates the risk that loss to follow-up is associated with either the prognostic factor or the outcome, a phenomenon known as informative censoring.1 If individuals who drop out of a study differ systematically from those who remain, the reported effect size will be biased.1 For example, if patients with a specific genetic marker are more likely to experience severe side effects and thus withdraw from a study, the remaining sample may understate the true association between that marker and the long-term outcome.

In this domain, the tool asks reviewers to consider the total follow-up rate, the reasons for dropout, and a comparison of the characteristics of those lost to follow-up versus those who completed the study.1 Low risk of bias is suggested when follow-up is nearly complete or when dropouts occur "at random"—meaning their absence is unrelated to the study variables.1 High risk is determined if important systematic differences exist between completers and non-completers that likely distort the primary association.1

### **Domain 3: Prognostic Factor Measurement and Misclassification**

The Prognostic Factor Measurement domain focuses on the validity and reliability of the predictor assessment. Misclassification of the prognostic factor—whether due to measurement error, recall bias, or inconsistent application of definitions—can lead to biased estimates of association.1 For instance, if a biomarker is measured using different assays across centers without standardization, the resulting "noise" can obscure the true signal or create a false association.

Key judgments in this domain involve whether a clear, pre-defined definition of the prognostic factor was used, whether the measurement was performed similarly for all participants, and whether measurement properties (validity/reliability) are supported by evidence.1 Ideally, measurements should be objective and, where possible, performed by assessors blinded to other patient characteristics or future outcomes to prevent expectation bias.2

### **Domain 4: Outcome Measurement and Detection Bias**

Outcome Measurement bias occurs when the method or intensity of outcome assessment varies according to the baseline level of the prognostic factor.1 This is a form of detection bias. A classic example is the "protopathic" bias or surveillance bias, where patients with a certain risk factor are monitored more frequently, leading to a higher rate of outcome detection compared to those not monitored as closely.1

The QUIPS tool prompts the assessor to look for clear outcome definitions, evidence of valid and reliable measurement protocols, and the consistency of follow-up duration and assessment methods across all study participants.1 A low risk of bias is achieved when the outcome is measured uniformly, ideally using standardized tools like imaging or histology that are less prone to subjective interpretation.2 Blinding of outcome assessors to the prognostic factor status is a critical safeguarding mechanism evaluated in this domain.1

### **Domain 5: Study Confounding and Multivariable Adjustment**

Confounding is a primary threat to the internal validity of any observational prognostic study. It arises when a third variable is associated with both the prognostic factor and the outcome, potentially creating a spurious association or masking a real one.1 Domain 5 assesses whether the researchers accounted for these factors through study design (e.g., matching or stratification) or through statistical analysis (e.g., multivariable adjustment).1

To judge risk in this domain, the assessor must first identify the "important" confounders for the specific clinical question—often based on existing literature or a conceptual model.1 The tool then evaluates whether these variables were measured accurately and whether they were included in the final analytic model.1 High risk of bias is commonly found in studies that report only univariable associations or fail to adjust for fundamental variables like age and disease severity.2

| Confounding Assessment | Evaluative Requirement |
| :---- | :---- |
| Selection of Covariates | Were all important potential confounders identified a priori? 1 |
| Measurement Quality | Were confounders measured using valid and reliable methods? 8 |
| Adjustment Strategy | Did the researchers use multivariable analysis to account for confounders? 1 |
| Handling of Missing Data | Were appropriate methods (e.g., multiple imputation) used for missing covariate data? 1 |

### **Domain 6: Statistical Analysis and Reporting**

The final domain addresses the integrity of the data analysis and the transparency of reporting. This domain targets "p-hacking," selective reporting of significant results, and improper model-building techniques.1 A major concern in prognosis research is the "data-dependent" selection of variables, where researchers test dozens of potential predictors and only report those that reach statistical significance, which leads to over-optimistic results.3

Assessors evaluate whether the statistical model was appropriate for the data type (e.g., Cox proportional hazards for time-to-event data), whether continuous variables were handled correctly (e.g., avoiding arbitrary cut-points), and whether all pre-specified outcomes were reported.1 High risk of bias is often assigned when there is clear evidence of selective reporting or when the sample size is insufficient for the number of variables tested, leading to model overfitting.1

## **Operationalizing the QUIPS Tool in the Systematic Review Process**

The transition from understanding the domains to applying them in a systematic review requires a standardized workflow. The developers of QUIPS advocate for a consensus-based process involving at least two independent reviewers with training in epidemiology and the clinical subject area.1

### **The Role of Signaling Questions and Prompting Items**

The core of the tool is a set of prompting items for each domain. These items are not binary "yes/no" indicators of quality but are signaling questions meant to inform a professional judgment.1 Reviewers typically rate each item as "Yes," "Partial," "No," or "Unsure".5 This granular rating helps the reviewer visualize the strengths and weaknesses within a single domain before making the final risk judgment.1

It is important to note that not every prompting item is relevant to every study. For example, if a study has zero attrition, questions regarding the comparison of completers and non-completers are moot.1 The tool is flexible, allowing reviewers to omit irrelevant items while still reaching a valid domain judgment.1

### **The Synthesis of Risk of Bias Judgments**

After evaluating the prompting items, the reviewer assigns a final rating for the domain: High, Moderate, or Low risk of bias.1 This judgment is qualitative and requires the synthesis of information across the signaling questions.

* **Low Risk of Bias:** The evidence suggests that the relationship between the prognostic factor and outcome is unlikely to be significantly distorted by factors within this domain.1  
* **Moderate Risk of Bias:** There are some methodological flaws or missing information that suggest the relationship may be distorted, but the overall association is still likely to be informative.1  
* **High Risk of Bias:** There are significant flaws (e.g., a very low participation rate or a lack of confounding adjustment) that make the reported result highly likely to be spurious or misleading.1

The developers explicitly recommend against calculating a summated "quality score" (e.g., 5 out of 6 domains).1 Instead, the profile of bias across domains should be used to describe the study's overall reliability. In synthesis, a study is often categorized as "Low Risk" overall only if all six domains are rated as low risk, or if the specific domains most critical to the research question are low risk.1

## **Comparative Analysis: Choosing the Correct Appraisal Tool**

In the landscape of evidence synthesis, choosing the correct appraisal tool is contingent on the specific research question. A common error among researchers is misapplying QUIPS to prediction models or intervention studies.

### **QUIPS vs. PROBAST**

The most significant distinction is between prognostic *factor* studies and prognostic *model* studies. Prognostic factor studies (the target of QUIPS) focus on the independent association of a single variable with an outcome.6 Prognostic *model* studies (the target of PROBAST) aim to combine multiple variables into a single mathematical formula to predict an individual's specific probability of an event.7 While PROBAST uses a similar domain structure (Participants, Predictors, Outcome, Analysis), its criteria are specifically tuned to model performance metrics like calibration and discrimination, which are not the focus of QUIPS.7

### **QUIPS vs. ROBINS-I and RoB 2.0**

For studies investigating the efficacy of a treatment or intervention, the Cochrane risk of bias tools are the appropriate choice.7 Even when an intervention is studied for its prognostic value, it is often better appraised using ROBINS-I (for non-randomized studies) or RoB 2.0 (for randomized trials) because these tools are optimized to evaluate the complexities of treatment effects, such as performance bias and crossovers.16

| Tool | Application Focus | Domain Structure |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **QUIPS** | Prognostic factor (single predictor) identification.1 | 6 Domains: Participation, Attrition, PF Measure, Outcome Measure, Confounding, Analysis.1 |
| **PROBAST** | Multivariable prediction model development or validation.7 | 4 Domains: Participants, Predictors, Outcome, Analysis.18 |
| **ROBINS-I** | Non-randomized studies of interventions.7 | 7 Domains focusing on treatment-related biases (e.g., immortal time bias).20 |
| **QUADAS-2** | Diagnostic test accuracy studies.16 | 4 Domains: Patient Selection, Index Test, Reference Standard, Flow and Timing.24 |

## **Implementation Challenges: Interrater Reliability and Learning Phases**

The practical application of QUIPS in specialized fields like pain rehabilitation and oncology has revealed both its strengths and its limitations regarding reliability. A notable study in the field of chronic pain rehabilitation assessed the interrater agreement of two researchers across forty-three papers.14

Initial interrater agreement was found to be "minimal," with a $\\kappa$ of 0.323 in the first round.14 However, following a learning phase and consensus meetings where the raters discussed their interpretations, agreement improved to "weak/moderate" ($\\kappa \= 0.536$) in the second round.14 This finding underscores that the QUIPS tool is not a "plug-and-play" instrument. It requires a significant learning curve and a shared understanding of clinical thresholds.9 For example, reviewers must agree on what constitutes an "adequate" participation rate (e.g., $\\ge 67\\%$) and what baseline characteristics are "key" for describing a study sample (e.g., age, sex, socioeconomic status, and comorbidities).9

Despite the challenges in achieving high numerical agreement, users often report that the *process* of using QUIPS is inherently valuable. It "demands" that raters investigate and discuss critical aspects of study quality that might otherwise be overlooked in a less structured review.5 This collaborative investigation often reveals subtle biases in confounding or selective reporting that are not immediately apparent.14

## **Technical Infrastructure: Digital Tools and Visualization**

To support the adoption of QUIPS, several technical resources have been developed. These facilitate the documentation of judgments and the visual presentation of results in final reports.

### **The QUIPS Electronic Spreadsheet**

An official Excel-based tool is widely used to organize the assessment. This spreadsheet allows reviewers to input text excerpts directly from the study being appraised into "white boxes," facilitating a clear audit trail for the consensus process.8 Raters use drop-down menus in "blue cells" to rate the adequacy of reporting and "green cells" to assign the final risk of bias judgments.8

### **Visualization via Robvis**

In many contemporary systematic reviews, the results of QUIPS assessments are visualized using the "robvis" web application.25 This tool generates "traffic light" plots, which display the domain-level judgments for each individual study, and weighted bar plots, which show the distribution of risk of bias across the entire body of evidence.25 These figures are publication-quality and help readers quickly identify systemic weaknesses in a particular research field, such as a consistent high risk of bias in confounding across all included studies.25

## **Advancements and the Transition to "QUIPS-2" (2024-2025)**

In recent literature (2024-2025), the tool is frequently referred to as "QUIPS-2" or a "refined version".27 While some researchers utilize it interchangeably with the 2013 version, the "QUIPS-2" nomenclature often signifies a more modern application that integrates the original domains with more specific signaling questions for modern statistical methods, such as competing risk analysis and multiple imputation.13

For instance, systematic reviews published in 2024 and 2025 investigating sinonasal mucosal melanoma (SNMM) and intraventricular hemorrhage in preterm neonates have utilized the "QUIPS-2" framework to evaluate retrospective and prospective cohorts.28 These studies highlight that while prognostic factor and outcome measurements are generally low risk in modern research, "Study Attrition" and "Confounding" remain significant areas of "Moderate" to "High" risk, reflecting persistent challenges in the long-term follow-up of rare diseases and the complexity of controlling for multifactorial clinical drivers.28

Furthermore, new tools like QUAPAS (Quality Assessment of Prognostic Accuracy Studies) are emerging as adaptations of QUADAS-2, incorporating domains and signaling questions from QUIPS and PROBAST to specifically assess "prognostic accuracy" studies, further illustrating the expanding ecosystem of appraisal tools derived from the original QUIPS philosophy.24

## **Synthesis of Evidence: Integration with GRADE**

The final stage of the QUIPS assessment is often its integration into a GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) analysis.34 Within the GRADE framework, the risk of bias assessment at the study level (via QUIPS) informs the "Study Limitations" domain, which can lead to a downgrading of the overall certainty of the body of evidence.21

Factors that can decrease the quality of evidence in prognosis include the phase of investigation (explanatory vs. confirmatory), study limitations (high QUIPS risk), inconsistency of results, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.34 Conversely, factors like a large effect size or an exposure-response gradient can increase confidence in the evidence.34 This integration is vital for translating prognostic data into clinical practice guidelines, as it ensures that recommendations are based on a rigorous assessment of both the strength of association and the reliability of the underlying research.5

| GRADE Factor | Impact of QUIPS Result |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Study Limitations** | "High" risk in multiple QUIPS domains leads to a downgrade (e.g., from High to Moderate or Low quality).34 |
| **Inconsistency** | If studies with different QUIPS profiles yield wildly different results, certainty is downgraded.34 |
| **Effect Size** | A "Moderate" or "Large" effect size may compensate for minor methodological flaws in some QUIPS domains.34 |
| **Publication Bias** | Selective reporting identified in QUIPS Domain 6 across multiple studies suggests potential publication bias.34 |

## **Conclusions and Future Outlook**

The Quality In Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool has fundamentally transformed the methodology of evidence synthesis in clinical prognosis. By providing a transparent, domain-based structure, it has moved the field away from arbitrary quality scores toward a meaningful evaluation of internal validity. The six domains—Participation, Attrition, Prognostic Factor Measurement, Outcome Measurement, Confounding, and Statistical Analysis—map directly onto the primary sources of systematic error in observational research, offering a comprehensive framework for both researchers and reviewers.1

The widespread adoption of QUIPS by the Cochrane Prognosis Methods Group and its successful application across diverse medical fields—from back pain to pediatric oncology—demonstrate its versatility and robustness.1 While achieving high interrater reliability requires dedicated training and calibration, the tool’s strength lies in its ability to foster a deeper understanding of methodological bias among its users.9

As research methodologies continue to advance, the ongoing refinement of the tool into "QUIPS-2" ensures its relevance in the era of artificial intelligence and high-dimensional data.29 For the future of prognosis research, the continued use of QUIPS will remain a cornerstone for identifying high-quality evidence, informing clinical decision-making, and ultimately improving patient outcomes through a more precise understanding of disease trajectories. The integration of QUIPS into digital ecosystems and broader evidentiary frameworks like GRADE ensures that the legacy of the 2013 consensus continues to uphold the standards of scientific rigor in the complex domain of human health.
