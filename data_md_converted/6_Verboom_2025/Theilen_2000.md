# Electroencephalogram silence ratio for early outcome prognosis in severe head trauma

Hermann J. Theilen, MD; Maximilian Ragaller, MD; Uwe Tschö, MD; Sven A. May, MD; Gabriele Schackert, PhD; Michael D. Albrecht, PhD

Objective: To introduce the electroencephalogram silenceratio (ESR) as a variable derived from mathematically processed electroencephalogram for early outcome prognosis in patients with severe head trauma and to comparatively assess sensitivity, specificity and predictive value vs. somatosensory evoked potentials and brainstem auditory evoked potentials.

Design: Prospective, interventional study.

Setting: Intensive care unit of a university hospital.

Patients: A total of 32 adults with severe acute head trauma (Glasgow Coma Scale score ≤8).

Methods and Main Results: In all patients, electroencephalographic recording was continuously performed by frontomastoid electrode montage for 24–96 hrs after admission to the ICU. The data were subsequently computed by fast Fourier analysis and the ESR (intervals of suppression as periods >240 msecs during which the electroencephalographic voltage did not exceed 5  $\mu$ V) was displayed and recorded on a computer for further evaluation.

Somatosensory evoked potentials and brainstem auditory evoked potentials were elicited during the first 2 days after admission. Outcome evaluation was performed 6 months after trauma using the Glasgow Outcome Scale and the Rappaport Disability Rating Scale. After careful artifact exclusion, the ESR depicted the highest sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value compared with evoked potentials. Even a highly significant correlation between outcome and ESR was found (p < .0001).

Conclusion: The ESR is a valuable variable showing a high reliability with respect to outcome prediction in severe head trauma with a higher predictive value than short latency somatosensory evoked potentials. Evidence exists that the ESR provides at least partial information regarding adequate cerebral oxygen delivery. (Crit Care Med 2000; 28:3522–3529)

Key Words: head trauma; outcome; processed electroencephalogram; neuromonitoring; electroencephalogram-silence ratio; somatosensory evoked potentials; brainstem evoked potentials

In several investigations, low amplitude EEG events have been found to correlate with poor outcome after brain injury (6-8). Rae-Grant et al. The question arises whether this electrophysiological occurrence may be quantifiable and subsequently able to provide valuable information regarding early outcome prediction in patients with head trauma. Rampil et al. (9) introduced a variable called "burst suppression ratio" for quantifying the burst suppression phenomena induced by narcotic drugs. The algorithm described intervals of suppression as periods >240 msecs during which the EEG voltage did not exceed 5 µV, then calculated as percentage of a given time period. This variable might be suitable for assessing quantitatively low-amplitude events in the electroencephalogram.

The present study was performed to investigate whether the "burst suppression ratio" might be a useful variable for early outcome prognosis in patients with severe head trauma. ## **PATIENTS AND METHODS**

*Patient Population.* The prospective study was undertaken at a university intensive care unit from July 1996 to June 1998. The study group consisted of 45 patients (35 males, 10 females) with severe head injury after closed head trauma. The mean age was 35.2 6 17.9 yrs (range, 18–75 yrs). All patients presented with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score #8 after resuscitation or deteriorated to this level within 6 hrs after trauma (median, 5). Informed consent for participation in this study was obtained from next of kin.

Diagnostic cranial computed tomographic (CT) scans were obtained in all patients at hospital admission, followed by immediate surgical intervention in 24 patients. To additionally describe the brain injury pattern, the Marshall CT scan classification (10) was used.

In addition, in eight healthy awake volunteers (mean age, 26.5 6 8.5 yrs [mean 6 SD[rsqb]) and in nine patients without brain injury, metabolic disorders, or sepsis that might influence electrocortical activity substantially who were sedated by midazolam/ fentanyl for \$24 hrs (38.4 6 12.8 yrs), the ESR was measured.

*Clinical Management.* Multimodality physiologic monitoring involved electrocardiography, arterial blood pressure by peripheral arterial cannulation, arterial oxyhemoglobin saturation by transcutaneous pulse oximetry, body temperature, and mean intracranial pressure (ICP). Mechanical ventilation was controlled by repeated blood gas analysis. ICP monitoring was performed via epidural or intraventricular catheterization. Central venous catheters were placed in all patients to guide normovolemic fluid management.

After ICU admission, sedation was performed by continuous application of midazolam (1.8–10.4 mg/kg/min iv) in combination with fentanyl (3.7–11.1 ng/kg/min iv) for analgesia. Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) was held at \$70 mm Hg. In 19 patients, a continuous intravenous application of norepinephrine was therefore necessary.

If ICP values exceeded 20 mm Hg for .10 mins, the treatment of the intracranial hypertension was supplemented in the following manner in addition to the previously induced sedation: head elevation to between 20° and 30° from horizontal while maintaining an adequate CPP, administration of osmodiuretics (mannitol), and mild hyperventilation (PaCO2, 37.5 6 1.5 mm Hg).

In 14 patients, a further elevation of ICP to values .30 mm Hg was followed by decompressive craniectomy. If this therapy failed, barbiturates were applied intravenously. The algorithm fits the procedure of ICP therapy as suggested by Maas et al. (11).

*Electroencephalographic Measures.* The EEG recording was performed using a computerized two-channel EEG monitor (pEEG-Monitor, Dra¨ger, Lu¨ beck, Germany) that displays both the raw EEG signal from the frontal brain and the computed data. Electrodes were placed on the scalp with bipolar frontomastoid montage (Fp1-A1 and Fp2-A2; international 10–20-system of electrode placement) with central forehead ground. Conventional Ag-AgCl electrocardiographic electrodes were taken for EEG recording. The EEG data were obtained at a sampling rate of 128 Hz and subsequently analyzed continuously during 2-sec data segments. Analysis was performed by fast Fourier transformation and power spectral analysis as described elsewhere (12). The skin was processed to reach an impedance of ,4 kV at each electrode. Every 5 mins the impedance was controlled automatically by the system and impedance shifts were adjusted by a replacement of electrodes. Amplifier bandwidths were 1.5–30 Hz, and the amplifier gain was set at 4300. The forehead electromyogram was recorded simultaneously for artifact rejection.

The monitoring was started #12 hrs after trauma and was continued for \$24 hrs up to 96 hrs. The mean duration of monitoring was 58 6 19 hrs. Monitoring was discontinued earlier than 96 hrs when sedation was stopped and EMG effected the ESR artificially or barbiturates or propofol were injected to decrease high intracranial pressure levels.

If any influence of muscular activity or eye movement on the electroencephalogram could not be excluded, a short acting neuromuscular blocking agent was applied intravenously.

*EEG-Silence Ratio.* The EEG-silence ratio was defined in accordance to the definition of Rampil et al. (9) as intervals of suppression as periods .240 msecs during which time the EEG voltage does not exceed 5 mV. EEG silence was determined by the system if \$32 successive samples of 128 sampling points/sec were ,5 mV and subsequently averaged for a time of 60 secs. If substantial interhemispheric differences occurred, the lower value of both hemispheres was taken for further outcome evaluation.

*Monitoring of Short-Latency Somatosensory Evoked Potentials and Brainstem Auditory Evoked Potentials.* SSEP and brainstem auditory evoked potentials (BAEP) were obtained at the bedside with the Neuropack II instrument (Nihon-Kohden, Tokyo, Japan) at least during the first 2 days after ICU admission.

The SSEP were elicited by electrical stimulation of the median nerves at 5 Hz bilaterally recording at second cervical level (N13 peak) and parietal scalp (N20 peak). A total of 200 responses were averaged per trial. N20 latency and central conduction time were estimated and taken for further evaluation. The results were scored in a 3-grade-scale according to Judson et al. (13) as follows: 1, normal symmetrical; 2, increased central conduction time on one or both sides; 3, absent cortical responses over one or both hemispheres. BAEP were elicited by 10 Hz alternating click stimulation of each ear (100-dB intensity) recorded at the vertex scalp with ipsilateral mastoid reference. A total of 1,200 responses were averaged per trial. The results were scored according to Cant et al. (14) as follows: 1, normal bilaterally; 2, abnormal latencies, wave V present; 3, wave V absent on one or both sides.

*Outcome Evaluation.* Outcome was assessed 6 months after discharge from ICU by means of the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) (15) and the Rappaport Disability Rating Scale (RDRS) (Table 1), which was introduced to provide a more specific outcome evaluation with regard to neuropsychological behavior (16).

The evaluations were obtained either by immediate contact with the patients or by telephone interview with the patients or their guardians or caretakers. To standardize the estimates of the patient status, the interviews

*Exclusion Criteria.* Patients were excluded from the study if the raw electroencephalogram showed electrocerebral inactivity or a burst-suppression-pattern initially or during the first 2 days of treatment (n 5 6). In patients presenting with these patterns, a poor outcome can be expected without exception. New information regarding outcome evaluation could not be expected by use of the ESR. Although no patients screened for the study showed metabolic disorders or sepsis, which might substantially effect the ESR, such occurrences would have necessitated exclusion.

The immediate use of barbiturates, propofol, or g-hydroxybutyrate to treat intracranial hypertension led to the exclusion of seven patients from the study. The application of these drugs, even in clinically relevant dosages, is able to increase the ESR in a substantial manner, in contrast to benzodiazepines.

*Statistical Analysis.* For statistical analysis and assessment of significance, we calculated Pearson's correlation coefficients, linear, and multivariate forward, stepwise logistic regression analysis. In addition predictive accuracy was computed to clarify the association between outcome and GCS score, somatosensory evoked potentials, ESR, and age. By means of cross tables we additionally computed sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive values for ESR, SSEP and BAEP. Student's *t*-test was taken for analysis of variance. A *p* ,.05 was considered statistically significant.

#### DISCUSSION

According to the results of the present study we are able to introduce the EEG-silence ratio as a highly feasible variable for outcome prediction in severe head trauma. Because the ESR is a metric variable derived from the raw electroencephalogram, it can be interpreted even without being familiar with special EEG analysis. This may induce a wider application of the mathematically processed electroencephalogram in clinical routine.

To achieve a high reproducibility of the approach, some important methodologic principles have to be stressed. The amplitude of the EEG signal can be artificially affected by several methodologic alterations. It is well known that an increase in the electrode distance results in an augmentation of the EEG amplitude (17). Correspondingly a closer electrode montage leads to an artificial increase of the ESR as it could be observed frequently. The frontomastoid montage chosen for ESR estimation could not be changed without affecting the ESR. Another important component is the choice of the sedatives and analgesic drugs. Benzodiazepines in dosages commonly used for adequate sedation do not result in an attenuation of the EEG amplitude but were described to increase EEG activity in the β-frequency range, especially in frontal brain areas (20). For opioids, an increase in the EEG amplitude accompanied by a slowing has been observed in several studies (21, 22). These drugs do not flatten the EEG amplitude although Herkes et al. (23) described the development of a burst suppression pattern in septic patients while applying midazolam. However, this phenomenon could also be ascribed to hypoxic brain damage that might have occurred in these patients in the course of the disease. In eight healthy young persons and in nine patients without cerebral injury sedated by midazolam and fentanyl, an ESR increase was not found in the present study. In contrast, immediately after the intravenous application of barbiturates, propofol,  $\gamma$ -hydroxybutyrate, or etomidate, an artificial increase of the ESR occurred systematically in a dosedependent manner. Consequently, the accurate interpretation of the electroencephalogram after the use of these drugs is impossible until it is completely metabolized or eliminated from the body. This might imply a limitation of the method. However, the combination of benzodiazepines with opioids is the most commonly used sedative strategy in most intensive care units during the initial phase of head trauma. This fact confines this methodologic insufficiency to patients requiring extended pharmacologic therapy for the treatment of a strongly increased intracranial pressure. In our study, 16% of all patients initially considered for the study had to be excluded because of prolonged barbiturate use started immediately after admission to ICU that was caused by an initially extensive ICP. This value is comparable to currently available data. In several studies, 10% to 12% of head-injured patients manifested intracranial pressure levels suggesting the additional use of barbiturates for ICP lowering (24–27). Taking into account that the use of barbiturates or similar drugs for ICP therapy is presently not a common practice (28), ESR monitoring is impossible because of barbiturate application only in a small subset of patients.

Even the exclusion of patients presenting with sepsis or metabolic disturbances only concerns a very small group of brain traumatized patients. As reported by Piek et al. (29) septicemia and renal, hepatic, or gastrointestinal abnormalities are either late complications of head trauma patients not effecting early prognosis by use of the ESR or only a very small subgroup with inconsiderable influence on methodologic suitability. We did not exclude one patient as a result of metabolic disorders.

The exclusion of patients with burst suppression electroencephalogram or EEG inactivity was done to make higher demands on the presented method. If a native electroencephalogram is available, these patterns can be detected and interpreted without using the ESR. If not, however, the ESR is able to provide reliable information. All ESR values were measured distinctly .25% while showing a poor outcome. Including the six patients initially presenting with EEG inactivity or burst suppression electroencephalogram, a higher correlation between ESR values and outcome would be computed.

There is evidence that the increasing reduction of the EEG amplitude even as brief intermittent events characterizes a more appreciable injury of the cerebral integrity. In the EEG abnormality grades developed by Synek (30), the EEG amplitude is more attenuated in patients with a poor outcome. Even low amplitude events have been described by several authors to imply a poor prognosis (6–8). However, in all studies it has been a rare event. Rae-Grant et al. (6) detected "episodic-low-amplitude-events" in 20 of 2,730 electroencephalograms analyzed over 2 yrs. In our group, .30% of all patients displayed very low amplitude EEG events. The question arises which kind of pathophysiological background might explain the described phenomenon. An increased ESR representing a profound disruption of normal electrophysiological activity might indicate either structural or functional involvement of cortical or subcortical structures. Morphologic lesions of the brain like diffuse axonal injury, especially in the reticular formation, are able to affect both electrocortical activity and conscious behavior or arousal (31, 32). This injury pattern has even been a repeated, although not consistent, finding of magnetic resonance imaging diagnosis in our patients showing high ESR values (6 of 10 patients). Even widespread cortical contusions in frontal brain areas are able to influence the ESR substantially. However, this damage is even able to cause incorrectly high values referred to prognosis, as observed in one patient of our study. In this patient, the ESR was measured at nearly 50% despite an adequate outcome (GOS score, 5). Consequently, it must be outlined that the introduced variable, like any other electrophysiological variable, must be interpreted in the entirety of all other diagnostic findings to reach a maximum of reliability with regard to its approach.

An EEG amplitude lowering could also be the result of a functional alteration of electrophysiological activity. It has been a frequent finding that cerebral ischemia occurring early after head trauma and probably induced by cerebral arterial spasm is a significant complication and can lead to impaired cerebral blood flow, especially in association with traumatic subarachnoidal hemorrhage (33–35). It is even a well known fact that electrophysiological activity is linked to cerebral metabolism and oxygen delivery as summarized by Jordan (1). It is necessary, however, to distinguish exactly between different extents of the impaired oxygen delivery. As postulated by Speckmann and Caspers (36) and confirmed in a recent study of Goel et al. (37), during moderate hypoxia medium- and highfrequency components of the electroencephalogram increased whereas lowfrequency components declined, accompanied by a reduction of the total power. This response was explained by an altered DC potential in the cortical areas. A further deterioration of oxygen delivery leads to EEG slowing and subsequently to EEG silence. It can be hypothesized that the ESR reflecting at least partially the EEG amplitude might already be affected even by moderate hypoxia. It has been noticed frequently in our patients who an acute reduction of the CPP resulted in an ESR increase before a decline of EEG frequency (own unpublished observations). Proceeding from the assumption that an elevated ESR is able to reflect cerebral hypoxia, an improvement of oxygen delivery should have a significant effect on this variable. In fact, we could reduce the ESR considerably while increasing cerebral perfusion pressure or adjusting intravascular volume. In an accompanying (unpublished) study, we were able to decrease the ESR in 36% of all patients with initially pathologic values. All patients, even those with a primarily high ESR which could be lowered to ,20%, showed GOS scores of 4–5 6 months after injury. While observing the ESR reactivity after clamping of one internal carotid artery during carotid endarterectomy, a strong ESR increase in the ipsilateral brain hemisphere was observed as a repeated finding. Because even the blood flow velocity in the ipsilateral middle cerebral artery estimated by transcranial Doppler sonography declined markedly, we concluded that this reaction was a consequence of a pathologically relevant reduction of oxygen delivery. Declamping normalized the ESR and blood flow velocity quickly.

After decompressive craniectomy in two of six patients showing a pathologically high ESR value, a considerable decline of ESR was observed immediately after surgery, which can be ascribed to a substantial increase of the CPP thus resulting in an improvement of cerebral oxygen delivery. It cannot be completely excluded that even a change of electrical properties (such as impedance or capacitance) or the development of a breach rhythm might affect the ESR. Regarding the highly predictive information of the ESR in these patients, however, it is reasonable to assume that low amplitude episodes detected by the ESR specifically reflected brief episodes of very low activity of cortical neurons.

Every new variable introduced in clinical routine should provide improvements compared with established methods. Accordingly, the ESR as a prognostic variable must be matched with SSEP, which has been proven to be the most useful variable for outcome prediction in sedated brain trauma patients. The prognostic accuracy of SSEP in the present study was analogous to those of previous studies (13, 14, 38, 39) as well as the superiority compared with BAEP (13). Comparing this issue with the ESR, the new variable was found to be at least as feasible as SSEP. In one patient, it even contradicted the findings of SSEP. Despite initially lost cortical responses in both hemispheres, a very low ESR questioned this finding. In the course of the disease, the prognostic assessment of the ESR could be confirmed. The patient regained consciousness and 6 months after injury exhibited a GOS score of 4. SSEP reappeared 3 wks after trauma. However, as mentioned above in another case, the SSEP were superior to ESR with regard to outcome prediction. In one patient showing isolated infratentorial infarction, cortical responses were absent in both hemispheres despite a low ESR value. This limitation of the introduced approach was confirmed in two patients not included in the present study suffering from thrombosis of the basilar artery. During the first days even in these cases, the ESR showed values that have to be interpreted as not critical (unpublished observations). However, the limitation of predictive value of the ESR is limited to patients with isolated infratentorial lesions. On the other hand, the ESR can possibly be taken as a diagnostic tool; this must be confirmed in a future study.

Another advantage is the possibility of a continuous noninvasive monitoring of brain integrity. As described above, the ESR is not always a steady value but was sometimes observed to vary during the monitoring period. This was possibly induced by different levels of oxygen delivery, as discussed above. Further investigations should be performed to evaluate the suitability of this device to provide an additional tool for controlling adequate cerebral oxygenation.

As an additional convenience, the ESR is not economically burdensome. Many computerized EEG systems already integrated the burst suppression ratio, which is a synonym for the ESR. Furthermore, in the device used for the present study it is even possible to take conventional electrocardiographic electrodes for EEG monitoring, which allows a continuous evaluation of cortical activity for days without any risk of local infection. A further attractive feature is the simplicity of interpretation, because the ESR as an intelligible metric variable can be valued easily and quickly even for the inexperienced observer with regard to electroencephalography.

In conclusion, the presented method is a suitable, highly reliable, and practical alternative for outcome prediction in severe head trauma. Furthermore, several advantages have been described that demonstrate that this approach provides important information in comatose patients, thus making it an effective noninvasive monitoring variable for neurophysiological monitoring on intensive care units. Our data suggest that this variable should be included in multimodality neuromonitoring in patients with head trauma.
