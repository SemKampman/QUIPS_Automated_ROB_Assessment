# Profile of First Time Seizure in Infants with 1 to 12 Months of Age Presenting to a Tertiary Care Pediatric Hospital

Ishtiyaq Qadri<sup>1</sup> Abdus Sami Bhat<sup>1</sup> Wani Shahid Hussain<sup>1</sup> Ayaz Ahmad Kakroo<sup>1</sup>

Abstract The incidence of seizure in first year of life is very high. Considerable research on neonatal seizures has been done; however information regarding the profile of patients with first time seizure in the age group of 1 to 12 months is scarce. Our study was a prospective observational study undertaken at a tertiary pediatric care hospital in north India with objectives of finding the epidemiological, clinical, and aetiological profile of patients in the age group of 1 to 12 months presenting with first time seizure and to ascertain their electroencephalography (EEG) and imaging findings. Seizure recurrence and developmental delay was also studied during the first six months post seizure. Fifty patients were included in the study. There were 31 male patients (62%) and 19 female patients (38%). Mean age at seizure occurrence was 5.3 2.1 months. Forty one patients (82%) presented with generalized tonic seizures. Only two patients (4%) had focal seizures. Seizures in 7 patients (14%) could not be classified. Electroencephalography and brain imaging were done in 42 patients as indicated. Among these 42 patients, EEG abnormalities were noted in 15 patients (35.7%), while imaging abnormalities were noted in 10 patients (23%). Fifteen patients (35.7%) were found to have developmental delay on follow-up. 16 (38%) patients developed recurrence of seizures. Statistical analysis revealed significant association between abnormal imaging and developmental delay at six months and seizure recurrence within six months of first seizure, however the association with abnormal EEG was not statistically significant.

#### Introduction

The incidence of seizure in first year of life is very high.<sup>1</sup> There is a sharp difference between neonatal and postneonatal seizures in infants with respect to etiology, semiology, electroencephalography (EEG), and imaging findings and final prognosis.<sup>2</sup> Considerable research on neonatal seizures has been done; however, information regarding the profile of patients with first time seizure in the age group of 1 to 12 months is scarce. Our study was undertaken with objectives of finding the epidemiological, clinical, and etiological profile of patients in the age group of 1 to 12 months presenting with first time seizure to our hospital and to ascertain their EEG and imaging findings. Seizure recurrence and developmental delay was also studied during the first 6 months postseizure.

# Materials and Methods

This was an observational prospective study conducted at the GB Pant Children Hospital, Srinagar, a tertiary care hospital for children from March 2016 to December 2016. A written informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians. Study population included infants between age

group of 1 to 12 months presenting to the hospital with first time seizure. Infants with any significant perinatal history (history of neonatal jaundice needing phototherapy or exchange transfusion, birth asphyxia, hypoglycemia, or with prior history of seizures were excluded from the study. History was taken about the seizure from guardians to differentiate between actual seizure and seizure mimics and to ascertain the semiology of seizure event. Attendants were encouraged to video image the seizure if recurrent so as to be certain about the seizure occurrence. A detailed history was also sought regarding other variables relevant to the study including a thorough developmental history. A detailed examination was done under controlled conditions after patient stabilization including anthropometry, general physical and systemic examination, and developmental examination. A child psychologist was involved in developmental assessment. Development Assessment Scale for Indian Infants (DASII) was used in this study. This scale is based on Bayley Scale of Infant Development and is used to assess development of children from birth through 2 years of age. It measures motor development and mental development as motor developmental quotient (DQ) and mental DQ. The normative values of DASII are well established and this scale is being used frequently in Indian studies.3–<sup>5</sup> A DQ score of < 70 (< 2 standard deviation) in motor and/or mental DQ is taken as developmental delay. Stigmata of intrauterine infections and any neurocutaneous markers were sought. Assessment for hearing and visual impairment was also done. Patients received treatment as per standard management protocols. Relevant investigations (blood sugar, serum total calcium, serum electrolytes, and septic workup) were done during hospital stay as required. An EEG was done after 2 weeks of seizure occurrence wherever indicated. Given the large number of patients who undergo EEG on daily basis at our EEG facility, we were not in a position to do EEG as soon as possible which is most desirable; however, we still worked it out at 2 weeks for all patients so that at least recommended period of within 4 weeks is not missed. EEG was read by a neurologist and for the purposes of this study, the findings were recorded as either normal or abnormal if any of the epileptiform discharges were present. Specific epileptiform abnormalities were not studied. Brain imaging, tandem mass spectrometry, and urine gas chromatography–mass spectrometry were also advised as required according to protocols. Patients were followed up for 6 months after initial presentation with seizure and contact with primary care taker was maintained through telephonic calls and planned outpatient visits. For statistical analysis, Fisher's exact test was used for discrete variables and Student's t-test for continuous variables. Relative risk (RR) was also calculated wherever required.

The association of developmental delay with abnormal imaging was found statistically significant (p-value < 0.0005). The RR of developing developmental delay at 6 months after first seizure in patients with abnormal imaging was 6.4 times (95% confidence interval [CI] of 2.8–14.3) as compared with those with normal imaging. Only 3 out of 15 patients with abnormal EEG went on to have developmental delay, and the remaining 7 patients with developmental delay had normal EEG (RR: 0.45 with 95% CI of 0.15–1.34). Hence, the risk of developmental delay at 6 months after first seizure in patients with abnormal EEG did not differ significantly from those having normal EEG. Sixteen patients (32%) had seizure recurrence on follow-up. Out of these, nine patients had abnormal imaging, while seven had normal imaging. Out of the remaining 26 patients who did not have recurrence of seizure, only one had abnormal imaging, while 25 had normal imaging giving a RR of 4.1 with 95% CI of 2.1 to 8.1. Among the 16 patients who had seizure recurrence, only 3 patients had an abnormal EEG, and 13 had normal EEG. Out of the remaining 26 patients who did not have recurrence of seizure, 12 had abnormal EEG, and 14 had normal EEG giving a RR of 0.41 with a 95% CI of 0.41 to 1.22. Statistical analysis thus revealed a significant risk of recurrence within first 6 months of first seizure in the presence of abnormal imaging as compared with those with normal imaging; however, the risk of recurrence in the presence of abnormal EEG was not significantly different from those with normal EEG.

There were three deaths (6%) in the study group. Two deaths occurred on initial admission because of shock and multiorgan failure and one death occurred during follow-up at home because of unknown reasons.

Male patients were found to constitute 62% of total study group, indicating a higher incidence of seizures in males as compared with females in this particular age group. Other studies on pediatric seizures have documented similar result8; 82% patients presented with generalized seizures which is a higher percentage when compared with studies done on childhood epilepsies as one group. One study reported 66% patients presenting with generalized seizures in a cohort of 50 up to 3 years of age.<sup>9</sup> Generalized tonic-clonic seizures were reported as the most common seizure type (69.9%) in children in a study from Nepal.<sup>10</sup> However, our study found generalized tonic seizures as the most common seizure pattern in this particular age group. Although we tried to elicit history related to seizure semiology in detail in cases where seizures were not observed in hospital, there may be a possibility of miscommunication in a few cases.

Our study found a different etiological pattern as compared with few other studies done in this age group. Instead of acute symptomatic seizures (related to central nervous system infections, hypocalcemia, hypoglycemia, electrolyte imbalance, vaccine induced), unprovoked seizures were found to be most common (58%) (►Table 3). Also, structural central nervous system abnormalities were noted in a significant number of patients (12%). A recent study reported 51% patients in the same age group presenting as provoked seizures with hypocalcemia constituting 26% of seizures.<sup>11</sup> However, like our study, the sample size studied was small. A larger cohort of patients may be needed to arrive at a proper conclusion in this regard. Our study noted EEG abnormalities in 35.7% of patients whose EEG was done. Abnormal EEG was reported in 24.1% in children with generalized seizure in a previous study.<sup>12</sup> Imaging abnormalities were found in 23% of patients imaged in our study. This result conforms to other studies done in children although across the whole pediatric age group.<sup>13</sup>

Our study found developmental delay in 35.7% of patients and seizure recurrence in 32% of patients during a 6-month follow-up. Both developmental delay and seizure recurrence were significantly associated with abnormal brain imaging, while association with abnormal EEG was not statistically significant. These associations have not been studied previously in this age group.

## Limitations

A relatively small sample size, short follow-up period for studying seizure recurrence and developmental delay and reliance on caretaker history in many cases about seizure event instead of actual observation or video imaging were major limitations of this study. EEG could not be done sooner than 2 weeks in all patients because of logistic issues which might have underestimated the true number of abnormal EEGs.

## Conclusion

Our study found a different etiological pattern of postneonatal infant seizures that differed significantly from previous studies. The difference could be explained by a different sample population studied as compared with previous studies which studied childhood seizures as whole. Also, we recommend a larger cohort of patients to study etiology in this age group. Further, this study found brain imaging as an important tool in predicting seizure recurrence and developmental delay in postneonatal infants presenting with first seizure. Studies with large sample size are required to confirm the findings.
