## a b s t r a c t

One of the most important risk factors for seizure recurrence is the electroencephalogram (EEG) characteristics of children with a first unprovoked seizure. However, the nature of the relationship between the risk factors for subsequent epilepsy and EEG characteristics, especially the localization of EEG paroxysmal abnormalities (PAs), remains unclear. The importance of EEG characteristics, especially the localizations of PAs, as predictors for subsequent epilepsy in children with a first unprovoked seizure was investigated. The participants were recruited from University of Yamanashi Hospital between July 1, 1997 and June 30, 2010 and followed until December 31, 2014. Eligible candidates were children between 1 month and 15 years old who presented with their first unprovoked non-febrile seizure. Awake and sleep EEGs were performed on a 12- or 16-channel machine 7–20 days after a first unprovoked seizure. Agreement regarding the presence of a PA was required for inclusion of the patient in the study. EEG PAs were classified based on the presence of spikes, sharp waves, or spike-wave complexes, whether focal or generalized,that were considered abnormal for age and state. All subjects were followed for more than 4 years. Of 87 subjects, 48 (55.2%) experienced recurrence. On the other hand, of 87 subjects, 52 (59.8%) showed an EEG PA. Of 18 patients with paroxysms in the frontal region, 17 (94.4%) developed epilepsy. Patients with frontal EEG paroxysms had a significantly higher risk of developing epilepsy than those with focal paroxysms in other regions of EEG foci (p < 0.05). Moreover, compared with generalized EEG foci, the odds ratios for patients with frontal, RD, occipital, and mid-temporal EEG foci were 85.0 (95% CI: 4.5–1617.1), 9.3 (0.9–96.0), 2.5 (0.1–62.6), and 7.5 (0.5–122.7), respectively. Children with a first unprovoked seizure presenting with frontal paroxysmal EEG abnormalities may be at risk for the development of epilepsy later in life.

## **2. Materials and methods**

#### 2.1. Participants

Participants were recruited from the University of Yamanashi Hospital between July 1, 1997 and June 30, 2010 and followed until December 31, 2014. The records of each participant were reviewed to documentthe occurrence of a seizure, classify the cause, and verify residency. Eligible candidates were children between 1 month and 15 years old who presented with their first unprovoked nonfebrile seizure. All participants were required to agree to take no medication until the appearance of a second seizure. Participants with multiple seizures occurring within 24 h were included if they were nottaking AEDs. Participants with previous neonatal seizures, febrile convulsions, immediate posttraumatic seizures, or other provoked seizures who now presented with a first unprovoked seizure were also included. Exclusion criteria were ambiguous episodes, seizures occurring with acute encephalopathy (for example, meningitis, encephalitis, or electrolyte or glucose disturbances, within 1 week of significant head trauma, or in association with febrile illness), or the presence of organic brain lesions on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), such as tumor, brain injuries, progressive neurological diseases, or metabolic diseases. Furthermore, children with status epilepticus (defined as a seizure lasting >30 min or a series of seizures lasting >30 min without the child regaining consciousness) were also excluded because of the need to give AEDs and the tendency to then continue AED treatment. EEGs were scheduled for all patients. Patients were referred from the outpatient department for an EEG within 7–20 days after the seizure. All patients were evaluated on referral with a standardized history and neurologic examination. Etiology and seizure type were classified in accordance with International League Against Epilepsy criteria (CommissiononClassificationand Terminology ofthe International League Against Epilepsy, 1981; Commission on Epidemiology and Prognosis, International League Against Epilepsy, 1993). Epilepsy was considered present when unprovoked seizure recurrence was documented. A seizure was considered unprovoked when there was no identifiable proximate insult (e.g., fever or head trauma) that could account for the seizure (Shinnar et al., 1996).

## 2.2. EEG procedures

Awake and sleep EEGs were performed on a 12- or 16-channel machine 7–20 days after a first unprovoked seizure. The duration of tracings was at least 20 min. Monopolar and bipolar electroencephalographic recordings were obtained with the 10–20 international system of electrode placement. For inclusion, it was necessary that atleast one EEG be obtained without drug induction, showing a clear sequence of awake-drowsy-sleep-arousal-awake states. For this reason, parents were instructed to keep their children awake the night before the visit. Intermittent photic activation was done routinely, and hyperventilation was used when age permitted.

EEG studies were coded by number and read independently by two pediatric epileptologists or neurologists blinded to the identity of the patients. Agreement regarding the presence of a PA was required for inclusion of the patient in the study. If the neurologists disagreed about the major classification, a third opinion was obtained from an experienced reader. Recordings included sleep in all of the patients. Tracings were classified as: normal; with focal or generalized PA; and with unspecific (nonepileptiform) baseline abnormalities (Winckler and Rotta, 2004). EEG PAs were classified based on the presence of spikes, sharp waves, or spike-wave complexes, whether focal or generalized, that were considered abnormal for age and state. If the localization of a paroxysmal discharge had migrated, the area affected most was considered the localization of the EEG PA. All patients were evaluated according to their EEG findings and whether they were diagnosed with epilepsy during follow-up. The data were collected prospectively until the appearance of a second seizure. All subjects were followed for more than 4 years.

The study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from the parents of each patient following a full explanation of the procedures to be undertaken.

### 2.3. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS II software for descriptive analyses. Differences were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test, Chi-square test, Fisher's exact test, and Bonferroni corrections for the relationship between later development of epilepsy and localization of EEG PA. The odds ratio as estimated from the logistic model was used as a measure of association and referred to as relative risk.

## **4. Discussion**

Thus, epileptic seizures must be clearly differentiated from other paroxysmal events, which have a different prognosis and imply a different therapeutic approach (Beghi, 2008). On the other hand, in many patients, seizures tend to remain isolated and, for this reason, drug treatment may not be justified, since drugs may cause significant adverse effects

Numerous studies have been performed to identify predictors of seizure recurrence. A meta-analysis by Berg and Shinnar identified an abnormal EEG and an underlying documented cause as the most consistent findings (Berg and Shinnar, 1991). More recently, a large, multinational, randomized trial (the MRC Multicentre trial for Early Epilepsy and Single Seizures; MESS) presented a risk allocation model for seizure recurrence using a regression model that included the presence of an abnormal EEG (Kim et al., 2006). Thus, EEG may have value in determining the prognosis of epileptic seizure recurrence in children.

The present study also showed that EEG findings had clinical value for predicting seizure recurrence after a first unprovoked seizure. Especially among them, the present results showed that the spike patterns in patients with seizure recurrence differed from those in patients without recurrence. Localization in the frontal region was revealed in 48.5% of patients with epilepsy, with only 2.8% in the generalized and occipital regions. In 94.4% of patients with frontal EEG PAs, epilepsy developed later. These findings suggest that the incidence of later development of epilepsy in patients with a first unprovoked seizure may depend on the EEG PA location. Patients with frontal EEG PAs may have a significantly higher risk of developing epilepsy than those with focal paroxysms in other regions of EEG foci.

Whether an EEG should be obtained routinely in patients with a first unprovoked seizure is still under debate. Beyond the effect on treatment, several factors that are intrinsic to the individual can influence the risk of recurrence. Several studies have reported that there are two factors that are consistently associated with an increased risk of recurrence in both children and adults (Berg and Shinnar, 1991; Ramos Lizana et al., 2000; Kho et al., 2006). One of these two factors is an abnormal EEG finding, particularly if the abnormality is an epileptiform PA. Moreover, from the data by Kim et al, patients with an abnormal EEG finding appear to have an even higher risk than those with only one of the factors (Kim et al., 2006). However, some authors would not treat after a first unprovoked seizure, even if the EEG is abnormal (Fountain and Freeman, 2006). The risk is that treatment will not be effective in preventing seizure relapse (Perucca, 2008). Despite these controversies over the value of EEG, EEG remains an essential tool for understanding the underlying process and providing the best counseling. The value of the EEG lies in three respects: suggesting the presence of focal lesions; allowing individual prediction of seizure recurrence; and indicating a specific epilepsy syndrome (e.g., 3-Hz spike-wave pattern) (Pohlmann-Eden and Newton, 2008). In the present study, a frontal EEG PA was identified as a significant risk factor for seizure recurrence as compared to paroxysms in other regions of EEG foci.Although the present sample was small,the data suggest that children with a first unprovoked seizure presenting with frontal paroxysmal EEG abnormalities may be at risk for the development of epilepsy later in life.

The reported yield of EEG PAs in routine examinations ranged from 12 to 27% (Hopkins et al., 1988; van Donselaar et al., 1992; Neufeld et al., 2000; Schreiner and Pohlmann-Eden, 2003), and it increased to 23–50% if sleep recordings could be obtained (van Donselaar et al., 1992; Schreiner and Pohlmann-Eden, 2003; King et al., 1988). The present results showed that the proportion with EEG PAs (59.8%) was greater compared to these previous reports. However, sleep recordings were obtained in the present study. In addition, the patients in this study were under 16 years of age. The proportion with epileptiform activity on the first EEG was found to be significantly greater in children compared to patients older than 16 years (Neufeld et al., 2000; King et al., 1988). The high rate of EEG PAs in the present study may be related to these aspects. However,the present sample was too smallto make definitive conclusions. Further investigations are needed to clarify these points.

A child who presents to the treating physician with a first unprovoked seizure faces a number of problems that require correct diagnostic and therapeutic management. Deciding when to begin treatment with AEDs in patients with a first seizure or infrequent seizures is difficult. Current evidence shows that the policy of immediate AED treatment, mainly carbamazepine or valproate sodium, in low doses reduces the occurrence of seizures in the next 1–2 years, but does not modify rates of longer-term remission in subjects with a single seizure (Chadwick, 2008). On the other hand, ongoing medical and psychosocial problems continue for patients with epilepsy, even in a sample where the majority report good control of their epilepsy (Jacoby, 1995; Fisher et al., 2000). In the present study, among first EEG, 11 patients (32.4%) are useful for the diagnostic of idiopathic focal epilepsy such as BCECTS and PS. The results of the present study may give additional information about the clinical factors for seizure recurrence. Moreover, this result could be also important to emphasize the role of sleep EEG after a first unprovoked seizure. Understanding these issues may improve communication of risks and benefits to individuals and may support making the decision to start or not to start AED treatment after a first seizure for clinicians and/or patients and their families.

Epileptic seizures may occur time dependently (Iasemidis et al., 1994).Inthe present study, all subjects were followed formore than 4 years. The duration of the seizure-free period that is necessary to consider the probability of a single seizure is difficult to determine. In most cases, the issue of whether a first seizure will remain isolated or whether it marks the onset of epilepsy is resolved within one or two years (Haut et al., 2007). In our previous study, the overall product-limit estimate of recurrence was 95.2% at 2 years after seizure onset (Mizorogi et al., 2015). Moreover, no recurrence was evident >4 years after the first seizure in that study. These findings suggest that the follow-up period in the present study may be appropriate to evaluate seizure recurrence.

The present study showed that the clinical profiles, such as age at onset, history of neonatal period, family history of epilepsy, and developmental delay prior to first seizure, were not associated with a greater risk of recurrent seizures. However, the present sample was too small to make any definite conclusions about these associations. Moreover, the methodology used in this study did not allow stratification for multiple factors and proper consideration of these associations. Accordingly, it is difficult to conclude that these clinical profiles are not associated with a greater risk of recurrent seizures from the results of this study. Further research is needed to evaluate these aspects.

In addition, although EEG findings may be important in children to predict subsequent epilepsy after a first unprovoked seizure, their significance can be improved when associated with imaging findings. However, patients with organic brain lesions on MRI were excluded in this study to avoid them affecting seizure recurrence. Accordingly, the association between EEG paroxysmal events and focal lesions observed onMRI could not be evaluated. Further investigations will clarify these points.

In conclusion, children with a first unprovoked seizure presenting with frontal paroxysmal EEG abnormalities may be at risk for the development of epilepsy later in life. We suggest that sleep EEGs should be a part of systematic comprehensive diagnostic approach in the patients with first unprovoked seizure. This finding could have a direct therapeutic impact of treatment (mostly not necessary) and parents' reassurance, especially in the idiopathic focal epilepsy such as BCECTS and PS. A careful workup including EEG is usually warranted. First unprovoked seizures are disruptive events for children and their families, and they worry about seizure recurrence later in life. Adequate provision of information may reduce patient and parental fear.
