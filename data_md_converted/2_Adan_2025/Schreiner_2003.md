# Value of the Early Electroencephalogram After a First Unprovoked Seizure

A.Schreiner and B.Pohlmann·Eden

## Key words

Electroencephalography, Predictive Value Epilepsy, Adults Seizure, First Unprovoked

# ABSTRACT

Studies on the predictive value of the electroencephalogram (EEG) concerning the risk of seizure recurrence have shown contradictory results. We prospectively studied the predictive value ofthe standard EEG and EEG with sleep deprivation for seizure relapse in adult patients presenting with afirst unprovoked seizure.

EEGs were performed on 157 adult patients within the first 48 hours of the first seizure. Additional EEGs with sleep deprivation were obtained in60 cases. The standard EEG was abnormal in 70.7% and significantly associated with an increased risk of seizure recurrence [risk ratio 4.5, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.8; 11.3, p=O.OOl). Subgroup analysis revealed the highest recurrence rates forpatients with focal epileptiform activity (risk ratio 2.2, CI 1.2; 4.2, p=O.Ol). EEGs with sleep deprivation were abnormal in 48.3% ofall cases and revealed epileptiform discharges in 13.3% of the patients who had no epileptiform activity in the standard EEG.

Routine EEG revealed abnormalities in 60 of 94 patients who presented with normal neurologic status on admission. Further neuroradiological examinations detected previously unknown brain lesions in 19ofthese cases, particularly cerebrovascular disease (CVD, n=7), brain tumors (n=6), and posttraumatic scars (n=4).

Inconclusion, the EEG isimportant for the early detection offocal nonepileptic and epileptic abnormalities after a first unprovoked seizure inadult patients and may provide valuable information on previously unknown disorders, particularly CVD and cerebral tumors. The abnormal EEG isa highly significant predictor for seizure recurrence. An additional EEG with sleep deprivation is helpful incases when standard EEG does not reveal epileptiform discharges.

#### **EEGs**

All EEGs were recorded on 16-channel video-splitscreen instruments with both referential and bipolar recordings bymeans ofthe International 10-20 electrode placement System. All EEGs were performed with hyperventilation for 3 minutes and intermittent photic stimulation. Sleep-deprived EEGs were performed on 60 patients whose initial EEG was normal orinconclusive, e.g., unspecific focal slowing. Sleep-deprivation EEGs were obtained after 3 to7 days between 9:00 to 11:00 a.m. after a maximum of3hours ofsleep the night before. EEGs were rated by two experienced neurophysiologists (the authors) according to a fixed protocol. The findings were classified in5categories (Table 1). Ifmore than one abnormality was present, the highest value based on the categorization was used for analysis.

#### **Statistics**

The cumulative risks ofrecurrence were determined by life-table-methods with event defined as an unprovoked seizure recurrence, 18These methods take into account the length of follow-up in each individual. Univariate analysis (Kaplan-Meier survival curves) for each variable relates the cumulative percent recurrence to the time after first seizure." We used the proportional hazards model toestimate univariate rate ratios, defined as the ratio ofthe rate ofseizure recurrence inthe group ofpatients with agiven factor tothe rate ofseizure recurrence without that factor."

Findings were considered significant when the bounds of the 95% confidence interval did not include unity.

## **RESULTS**

Seizure types: 95 patients (60.5%) had generalized tonic-clonic seizures without focal onset, of which 2 presented with status epilepticus. Nine (5.7%) had simple partial and 5(3.2%) had complex-partial seizures. Secondarily generalized seizures occurred in 27 patients (28.4%), 5 seizures (3.2%) remained unclassified.

Standard EEG: More than two-thirds ofstandard EEGs were abnormal (70.7%). Most frequent were focal abnormalities, i.e., atheta ordelta focus (36.9%) orfocal epileptiform activity (17.2%). We detected considerably low rates for generalized epileptiform activity (9.6%) and diffuse slowing (7.0%) (Table 2).

Forty-nine patients (31.2%) had a seizure recurrence (SR). Recurrence risks were estimated tobe 23.1%after 6 months, 27,5% atone year and 31.0% attwo years for all patients with asignificantly increased risk, i.e., for patients with an abnormal EEG (Figure 1).

Ofpatients with SR, 10.2% had anormal standard EEG compared to 38.0% who remained seizure free. Focal epileptiform activity was detected significantly more frequent in patients with SR (26.5% vs. 13.0%). Focal nonepileptic abnormalities were associated more frequently, but not statistically significant, with an increased recurrence risk (46.9% vs. 32.4%). There were no significant differences inthe rates ofgeneralized epileptiform activity or diffuse slowing (Table 2).

# DISCUSSION

The predictive value of the EEG after a first unprovoked seizure has proved tobea controversial topic. The controversies are based on different aspects: study design (e.g., prospective vs. retrospective. new-onset epilepsy vs. first-seizure method), age, site of recruitment. ortime point ofstudy entry. This led toconsiderable variations inestimation ofrecurrence rates and identification of predictors forseizure relapse.'·3,8.1113.'D There have been only a few studies based on the assessment atthe time oftheir first seizure.2.B The cumulative risk ofseizure recurrence in our study is consistent with the results of previous authors'·2.8.I1·20 and a recent meta-analysis" for studies using a prospective ascertainment and a firstseizure method.

The rate of EEG abnormalities was 70% in our study. This rate corresponds well with previous results that range from 41 to80% overall and from 9to63% inselected populations, e.g., patients with idiopathic or remote symptomatic seizures.1.3.a.12.22 An abnormal EEG was predictive for a significantly increased recurrence risk in all'·3810.12 but two 5.11 studies. However, the interval between the first seizure and EEG recording, which may be decisive for sensitivity, was not mentioned in previous studies (in two studies the interval was> 48hours and < 10days).35 This has recently been criticized. In our study standard EEG was performed within 48 hours ofthe first seizure. Inour opinion, this short interval explains the substantial rate of abnormalities and the increased recurrence risk associatedwith an abnormal EEG, a risk considerably higher than inprevious studies.1.J.8-12 Aloss ofepileptic excitability with increasing interval from the first seizure might be the reason forthe lower rate of abnormalities in the sleep deprivation EEG in our study (48.3% vs. 70.7%). A similar change was also reported byvan Donselaar etal.' especially regarding focal epileptic and nonepileptic discharges. However, EEG with sleep deprivation may provide useful information in patients without epileptiform

activity instandard EEG,7 and inthese cases it should be performed assoon as possible.

Data on specific EEG abnormalities associated with higher recurrence rates are rare. The high predictive value for SR of primary generalized spike-waves was reported only in a selected population of patients who most likely suffered from idiopathic epilepsy.27 Our results are comparable with previous studies, which reported the highest risks ofrecurrence for patients with focal epileptiform discharges and focal nonepileptic EEG abnormalities.71oThe low rate of generalized epileptiform activity and idiopathic seizures inour study can beexplained bythe inclusion criteria: patients with petit mal seizures or a history suspicious of epileptic attacks, which frequently occur in idiopathic epilepsies, were not enrolled.

High excitability, the substantial rate of epileptiform activity and the significant association with recurrent seizures emphasizes the necessity ofconsulting aspecialistand the need for an EEG examination immediately after a first unprovoked seizure. We conclude that a standard EEG, if performed within 48hours, iscrucial for the detection offocal nonepileptic and epileptic abnormalities after a first unprovoked seizure. In a substantial number it may help to detect previously unknown disorders, particularly cerebrovascular disease and cerebral tumors. Although the EEG may be an indicator ofunderlying brain lesions, it will not become a substitute for imaging studies. Both functional and structural tests are appropriate inthese patients and provide valuable complementary information.

## CONCLUDING REMARKS

An early EEG performed within 48 hours of the first seizure is a highly significant predictor for seizure recurrence. Additional EEGs with sleep deprivation may be helpful whenever standard EEG does not reveal epileptiform discharges. As a consequence toour results, we will prospectively include 24 hour continuous EEG-monitoring toobtain even more information on disturbed brain function immediately after a first seizure.
