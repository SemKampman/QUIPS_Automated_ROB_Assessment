### **ABSTRACT**

Objective: Our aim was to assess the long-term follow-up of juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME), with an emphasis on the course of the myoclonic seizures.

Methods: We enrolled 48 patients with JME (29 F, 19 M; aged 39.9  $\pm$  9.5 years) followed up for a mean of 19.6  $\pm$  5.7 years. The remission for 5 years and relapses were evaluated for all seizure types and the changes in severity/frequency of myoclonia were systematically questioned. The clinical and EEG features, antiepileptic drug (AED) treatment regimen, and systemic and psychiatric comorbid diseases were evaluated.

**Results:** We found a benign course in 66.6% whereas 16.7% had pseudo-resistance due to problems in treatment or lifestyle. The true-resistant course observed in the remaining 16.7% was significantly associated with psychiatric disorders and the presence of thyroid diseases. In 54.2% of the patients, myoclonia were in remission for a mean duration of  $8.4 \pm 7.7$  years, after an average age of  $32.9 \pm 9.6$ . Of these patients, 6 were on a lower dose of AED in comparison to the dosage needed to control the seizures in the beginning, and 5 patients had stopped AED treatment. None of the latter 11 patients except one relapsed during the follow-up. Furthermore, 21 other patients (43.8%) described substantial alleviation after age 31.3  $\pm$  8.4 in the severity of myoclonia.

Conclusion: Although a great majority of the patients with juvenile myoclonic epilepsy had continuing seizures after a follow-up of 20 years, almost all had either 5-year remission or a substantial alleviation of the myoclonic seizures. **Neurology**® 2008;70:2123-2129

# **GLOSSARY**

**AED** = antiepileptic drug; **AS** = absence seizures; **CBZ** = carbamazepine; **GTCS** = generalized tonic-clonic seizures; **IGE** = idiopathic generalized epilepsies; **JME** = juvenile myoclonic epilepsy; **LTG** = lamotrigine; **PB** = phenobarbital; **PHT** = phenytoin; **TLE** = temporal lobe epilepsy; **VPA** = valproate.

Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME) is a common syndrome classified among the idiopathic generalized epilepsies (IGE).<sup>1</sup> It has several seizure patterns, with the major characteristic being myoclonic seizures.<sup>2,3</sup> Generalized tonic–clonic seizures (GTCS) often occur, and one-third of individuals also have absence seizures (AS),<sup>4</sup> but myoclonia may remain the only seizure type in some individuals. Onset is usually in adolescence but seizures may begin or be diagnosed in the early 20s. Although myoclonic seizures are not specific for JME, their appearance as the cardinal symptom mostly in the morning in a neurologically normal patient makes the diagnosis easy.<sup>5</sup> The typical abnormality on EEG is bilateral spike or multispike and wave complexes, at a rate of four to six per second, usually with anterior predominance. Photosensitivity occurs in about 30%, especially in women, but this feature appears to be separately inherited.

Although JME is widely recognized and presumed to be a lifelong trait with a high recurrence rate after withdrawal of antiepileptic drugs (AED), prospective long-term studies documenting this concept are extremely rare. A recent long-term follow-up study of JME included 257 patients initially enrolled for genetic studies.<sup>6</sup> This multicentric

Our aim was to report the long-term follow-up of patients with JME, with an emphasis on the course of the myoclonic seizures.

**METHODS** In the initial step of this study, we had retrospectively analyzed the files of 156 patients diagnosed with JME according to the criteria of International League Against Epilepsy7 between 1972 and 1996 in our epilepsy outpatient clinic. This epilepsy center is a part of a university hospital and a well-established tertiary referral center for patients with epilepsy in Turkey. On the other hand, due to its convenient location in the center of the metropolis Istanbul, admittance of the patients for primary neurology and emergency departments are also common, constituting at least one half of our cases. The patients having a minimum follow-up duration of 7 years in our institution were included without any other exclusion criteria and the first retrospective results of this survey were published in a national journal at that time.8 Afterwards, we continued to follow up these subjects periodically after obtaining their consent. The prospective evaluation began in 1997 and the analyses included all data retrospectively and prospectively collected between 1972 and 2006. If the patients delayed the scheduled visits for more than 1 year, they were invited to a subsequent visit with telephone calls or letters. No specific treatment regimen, laboratory investigations, or EEG controls were planned for them and they were managed according to clinical needs, like all other patients with IGE. Lifestyle (sleep patterns, alcohol use) and its changes were closely monitored during the follow-up. Our protocol included counseling about these factors and about their seizure threshold lowering effects. None of these patients reported using alcohol.

All but two patients (both with familial evidence of IGE) had at least one diagnostic EEG and all available EEGs were reviewed by the experienced investigators. Our routine EEG protocol established after 1990 included hyperventilation and intermittent photic stimulation as described in detail previously.9 All patients were independently evaluated by at least two of the experienced epileptologists during the longterm follow-up. If the neurologists noted the existence of psychiatric problems, they were further seen by the psychiatry team to clarify the psychiatric diagnosis according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders–IV.

We initially had enrolled 51 patients, but 2 patients with progressive course who were diagnosed with Lafora disease with skin biopsy and with the identification of EPM2 mutations and one last patient having partial epilepsy with secondary bilateral synchrony on video-EEG were excluded from follow-up. The remaining 48 patients were reevaluated in 2006.

Remission was defined as ever being seizure-free for an individual seizure type (either myoclonic or GTCS or both) at least for 5 years in any time during the course with or without AED treatment. Relapse was defined as recurrence of seizures after being seizure-free for at least 5 years.

The history of myoclonia was taken in full detail to outline its severity, besides its monthly frequency. We asked if the myoclonus caused the patient to fall or to drop objects, if it was visible to others or if it was only perceived by the patient. The patient's self-assessment was used basically to rate the myoclonus severity: 0: no myoclonus; 1: milder; 2: same severity compared to that in the first years of JME. In the final analysis, myoclonic seizures were rated as milder if patients' self-assessment was equal to 1 and monthly frequency was 50% less as compared to the frequency in the first 2 years.

The patients were labeled as having benign course (group I) if they had no GTCS and less than two myoclonic seizures monthly. Resistance to AED therapy (group II) was defined as having one or more GTCS or clonic-tonic-clonic seizures per year or more than one myoclonus monthly despite treatment with valproate (VPA) or other appropriate AED for this syndrome with therapeutic drug levels for the last 5 years. We considered lamotrigine, levetiracetam, topiramate, clonazepam phenobarbital (last two drugs were mostly used in the past) and their combinations with VPA as appropriate choices in JME. The patients having seizures due to problems in lifestyle or inappropriate use of seizure aggravating drugs or being noncompliant were grouped as pseudo-resistant (group III). We also evaluated fluctuations during the course from benign to resistant and vice versa.

Other evaluated features were seizure types, ages at onset of all seizure types, personal and family history, results of physical and neurologic examinations, findings of all EEGs, imaging studies when available, psychiatric involvement, comorbid diseases, and treatment and response to the AED with known aggravating effects such as carbamazepine (CBZ), phenytoin (PHT), and other confounding factors of seizure recurrence during long-term follow-up. The treatment regimen used in the last 5 years was grouped as follows: 1) no treatment, 2) VPA monotherapy, 3) other monotherapy (such as phenobarbital [PB], lamotrigine [LTG], topiramate, levetiracetam, clonazepam), 4) reasonable polytherapy, and 5) inappropriate therapy (with CBZ and PHT).

We used Fisher exact tests to compare categorical variables and Student *t* test to compare continuous variables. A Kaplan–Meier curve evaluating the cumulative probability of achieving myoclonus freedom in relation to course was generated using SPSS software.

Thirty-two patients (20 female; 62.5% vs 12 male; 37.5%) had benign course (group I) whereas 8 patients (6 female; 75% vs 2 male; 25%) had resistant course (group II) at the end of the follow-up. We also noted pseudoresistance in 8 patients (group III; 16.7%) (3 female; 37.5%) due to an inadequate lifestyle in two patients, low compliance in one patient, and insufficient AED treatment in five patients due to financial problems (two patients on PB monotherapy, one patient on PHT monotherapy, and two patients on low dose VPA).

The remaining two patients with psychiatric disorders (table) belong to the pseudoresistant group. Two patients of group I (6.3%) vs 3 patients of resistant group (37.5%) had thyroid disease (Fisher exact test p = 0.046).

The gender distribution reported above, delay of diagnosis (mean: 2 years in group I vs mean 3.3 years in group II), and presence of photoparoxysmal response (21.9% in group I vs 12.5% in group II) and other EEG findings were not significantly associated with AED resistance. The presence of all three types of seizures (15.6% in group I vs 50% in group II) was not quite significant for resistant course (p = 0.059; Fisher exact test). The difference of the family history of epilepsy (28.1% in group I vs 62.5% in group II) is also not significant, but these results are inconclusive due to the small sample sizes.

**DISCUSSION** Although there have been many studies dealing with every possible aspect of JME in the last two decades, long-term follow-up studies are rare in the literature. A recent report indicated that JME was perhaps lifelong6 in a larger series from various centers over a mean follow-up of 11 6 years. This study is 8 years shorter than our study, which was done in a homogenous group seen in a single epilepsy center.

In two patients with JME with a follow-up duration of 20 years, myoclonic seizures decreased under the same drug regimen after 30 years of age.10 We showed a substantial alleviation of the myoclonic seizures in the fourth decade for the first time in the literature.

Only a few anecdotal case reports of JME in old age were presented.11-13 The rarity of patients with JME among the patients with epilepsy aged more than 40 years led us to design this long-term observational study. To have a long-term follow-up duration in the same center, we started the prospective part of this study in 1997, with 48 patients with JME having at least 7 years of retrospective follow-up in our center.

The striking deterioration observed in some patients with JME taking PHT or CBZ has not been explained.14 In our series, 60.4% of patients had received PHT or CBZ as add-on therapy at some time during the JME course with an aggravation of myoclonic seizures in 44.8%, an improvement in 37.9%, and no change in 17.3%. It is not easy to change the therapy, which was found useful for the patients on VPA and those who reported an improvement on either CBZ or PHT refused to withdraw the drugs. The usefulness of these drugs in some selected JME cases was also reported by others as add-on treatment, especially for GTCS.15 Thus, despite recent advances, much remains to be discovered about this common epilepsy syndrome and careful clinical follow-up is needed.

The coexistence of JME with cryptogenic temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) in one of our cases is interesting to note and this association was previously mentioned in a few case reports only.16,17 Our patient had a clear picture of JME with benign course initially. Afterwards TLE appeared with temporal lobe spikes in the EEG. Although these two syndromes retained their inherent electroclinical profile, we could not control her seizures after the emergence of TLE, which did not respond to VPA, but had better seizure control with CBZ.

Three cases of premature death among 170 consecutive JME cases were reported18 and the authors concluded that serious mental disorders seem to be risk factors for unexpected death. We observed two deaths, one due to metastatic cancer unrelated to epilepsy. The second patient, a very obese 51-year-old man with well-controlled JME and with an atypical psychosis, was found dead in his sleep, although both of his disorders were under control for years.

We found true resistance to AED in 16.7% of this series and our findings are in accordance with the experience reported by others.19 Among 155 consecutive patients followed up for at least 1 year (13.5 9.9 years), there were 15 pseudoresistant patients in a recent study19 and we found 16.7% to have pseudo-resistance during a longterm follow-up in a specific epilepsy center.

The presence of all three seizure types of JME was reported to be a risk factor for AED resistance,19 whereas myoclonus alone20 or a combination of AS and myoclonus are not.21 We could not comment on this issue, because our series included two patients having either myoclonus only or myoclonus and AS and we did not find the presence of all three seizure types quite significant in our small series (*p* - 0.059). It should be noted, however, that a substantial proportion of patients with JME had 5-year remission of myoclonic seizures, although still having rare GTCS.

Another clinical feature associated with AED resistance in JME was the presence of psychiatric problems in our study. A retrospective study of patients with adult-onset idiopathic generalized epilepsy reported that 33% were diagnosed with and treated for mental disorders,22 emphasizing the need for looking for them in patients with IGE. The role of systemic disorders such as thyroid diseases23,24 is also important for AED resistance in JME as well as fluctuations in seizure control in an otherwise well-controlled patient as observed in our series.

EEG findings seem not to have any influence on the prognosis. In our first retrospective evaluation with a 7-year follow-up, we found that the normalization of the first diagnostic EEG might have some indication for a good prognosis.8 However, our final analysis did not support this initial result.

The first report on patients with intractable JME25 included patients with long duration of epilepsy during which the diagnosis and appropriate treatment was delayed and with a high percentage of asymmetries or focal discharges on EEG. Focal clinical or EEG features or both in patients with JME are common.26-31 However, we could not confirm that focal changes and long duration or delay of diagnosis have an association with drug resistance in JME.

The most remarkable result of the current study was that the myoclonic seizures in JME had a tendency toward remitting or becoming milder with age. We believe that this tendency is unrelated to the AED treatment or to the lifestyle changes, but we could not rule out possible lasting effects of these interventions. Recently, a clinical study assessed the longterm evolution of progressive myoclonic epilepsy of Unverricht-Lundborg type (EPM1), especially concerning the prognosis of seizures.32 Myoclonia progressed only during the first 5 years; no worsening of myoclonia was seen beyond the 10 –15 early years of EPM1.32 Improvement of myoclonia was also observed in some patients with EPM1 in the last 5 years of follow-up and this finding was interpreted by the authors as being probably due to the introduction of new drugs. Even though JME and EPM1 have no similarities regarding pathogenesis and course, it is interesting to note that both forms of myoclonic epilepsies have similarity in the alleviation of myoclonus with advancing age.

Recent genetic work suggests that JME may represent several different disorders with different genetic and pathologic signatures found in patients who are clinically indistinguishable or practically so.33 However, the subtle differences in clinical findings may also be important. It is not easy to know whether patients with remission vs substantial amelioration in myoclonia have a particular genetic signature, or what the relationship may be between the genetic background and prognosis of the individual patient with JME. The roles played by elements such as environmental factors and susceptibility genes remain to be clarified.

We suggest that the unique pathophysiology of the cortical myoclonus of JME improves with time in at least some of the patients though persisting for a long time. There is a marked overlap in the mean ages at remission or alleviation of myoclonia (32.9 9.6 vs 31.3 8.4 years).

Myoclonic seizures are important symptoms, causing impairments and restrictions of social life for some patients with JME at least in the beginning. The patients easily realize that JME is considered as a benign but lifelong genetic disease by the scientific community. We hope that our results showing either a meaningful decrease or remission of myoclonic seizures after a median of 33 years of age will prevent patients with JME and their relatives from losing hope.
