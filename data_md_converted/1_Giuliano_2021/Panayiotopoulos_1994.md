# Juvenile Myoclonic Epilepsy: A 5-Year Prospective Study

C. P. Panayiotopoulos, \*T. Obeid, and †A. R. Tahan

St. Thomas' Hospital, London, England; \*Social Insurance Hospital, Riyadh; and †King Khalid University Hospital, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia

Summary: We made a long term prospective study of 66 patients with juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME). Prevalence was 10.2% among 672 patients with epilepsies. Sex distribution was equal. Sixty-three were not diagnosed on referral; JME was not initially recognized in the epilepsy clinic in 22. Clinical typical absence seizures were reported in 33.3%, myoclonic jerks in 97% and generalized tonic-clonic seizures (GTC) in 78.8% of the patients. Mean age ( $\pm$ SD) at onset was 10.5  $\pm$  3.4 years (range 5-16 years) for absence seizures,  $15 \pm 3.5$  years (range 8–26 years) for myoclonic jerks, and  $16 \pm 3.5$  years (9–28) for GTC. Absence predated myoclonic jerks by  $3.9 \pm 2.3$ years (range 1-9 years) and GTC by 4.4  $\pm$  2.7 years (range 1–8 years) in 14 (21.2%) patients who manifested all three types of seizure. Absence were never antedated by myoclonic jerks or GTC. Myoclonic jerks occurred on awakening in 87.5% of the patients. GTC occurred mainly on awakening, but other patients had nocturnal or diurnal GTC with no circadian distribution. Neurologic examination was normal for all patients except for tremor of the hands similar to essential tremor, noted in 35% of patients. Computed tomography (CT) brain scans were normal: 93% of patients had precipitating factors: sleep deprivation (89.5%), fatigue (73.7%), photosensitivity (36.8%; television and video games 8.8%), menstruation (24.1% of women), mental concentration (22.8%), and stress (12.3%). Incidence of JME among siblings (13 of 41 examined families) implies an autosomal recessive mode of inheritance for this Arab population. EEGs were frequently normal in treated patients. At least one abnormal EEG was recorded in 56 (84.9%) patients. Abnormalities consisted mainly of generalized discharges of spike/ double spike and/or polyspike and slow wave. Frequent multiple spikes and discharge fragmentations varied from 0.5- to 20-s duration (mean 6.8 s). Twenty (30.3%) had focal abnormalities, and 18 (27.3%) had photoconvulsive discharges. Eighty-eight percent of patients remained seizure-free for ≥3 years of follow-up. Effective treatment was achieved with valproate (VPA); control of myoclonic jerks was improved with clonazepam (CZP). CZP monotherapy did not consistently prevent GTC. Adding small doses of CZP with simultaneous reduction of VPA was the most effective and better tolerated form of medication, particularly in patients demonstrating an adverse reaction or requiring a large VPA dosage. VPA dosage was successfully reduced in 15 patients who were seizure-free for >2 years and had infrequent seizures before treatment, but 9 of 11 patients relapsed after VPA discontinuation. Key Words: Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy—Prevalence— Absence seizures—Generalized tonic-clonic seizures— Myoclonic jerks—Precipitating factors—Circadian distribution—Inheritance patterns—Valproate—Clonazepam.

Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME) is a generalized, idiopathic epileptic syndrome with age-related onset with a prevalence of 5–11% among other epilepsies (Janz and Christian, 1957; Janz, 1969, 1985, 1989; Obeid and Panayiotopoulos, 1988; Commission, 1989; Grunewald et al., 1992). JME is characterized by myoclonic jerks, occurring mainly on awakening and often associated with generalized tonic-clonic seizures (GTC) and typical absence seizures in an age-related sequence. Seizure-precipitating factors, mainly sleep deprivation and

fatigue, are common. JME appears usually in the midteens and is probably a lifelong disorder (Janz, 1969, 1985, 1989; Asconapé and Penry, 1984; Delgado-Escueta and Enrile-Bacsal, 1984; Wolf, 1985; Obeid and Panayiotopoulos, 1988; Dreifuss, 1989; Panayiotopoulos et al., 1989a,b; Penry et al., 1989; Grunewald and Panayiotopoulos, 1993).

JME is an inherited disorder, but the pattern of transmission is controversial (Tsuboi and Christian, 1973; Panayiotopoulos and Obeid, 1989; Greenberg et al., 1988, 1990; Panayiotopoulos et al., 1991). Autosomal recessive (Panayiotopoulos and Obeid, 1989) and polygenic (Tsuboi and Christian, 1973) patterns of inheritance have been suggested but have not been accepted by other investigators (Greenberg et al., 1990). Recent reports indicated that JME may be tightly linked to the Bf-HLA locus

The EEG of untreated patients is usually abnormal, with generalized discharges described as fast **4-** to **6-Hz** polyspike-slow wave; one third of patients exhibit photoconvulsive responses (PCR) (Janz, **1985, 1989;** AsconapC and Penry, **1984;** Delgado-Escueta and Enrile-Bacsal, **1984;** Wolf and Goosses, **1986).** The EEG pattern of JME recently was more clearly identified (Panayiotopoulos et al., **1989a,b).** 

At least **80%** of patients may become seizure-free with appropriate medication, but relapses are inevitable on treatment discontinuation (Covanis et al., **1982;** Delgado-Escueta and Enrile-Bacsal, **1984;**  Obeid and Panayiotopoulos, **1988;** Panayiotopoulos et al., **1989a;** Penry et al., **1989;** Christe, **1989;** Janz, **1991).** 

Despite the distinctive features of JME and the significance of diagnosis for management, prognosis, and inheritance, JME is widely underdiagnosed (Delgado-Escueta and Enrile-Bacsal, **1984;** Janz, **1985;** Obeid and Panayiotopoulos, **1988;** Penry et al., **1989,** Panayiotopoulos et al., **1991;** Anonymous, **1992;** Grunewald et al., **1992).** Recognition of the significance of JME in the English language neurologic literature, based on retrospective studies, is new.

We present our cumulative data from a 5-year prospective JME study. We know of no other similar study in which comprehensive clinical, genetic, EEG, video-EEG, radiologic, and therapeutic aspects of JME were studied prospectively. Results obtained in the course of our study regarding clinical and video-EEG manifestations **of** typical absence seizures, genetics, and factors of error in diagnosing JME were previously published (Panayiotopoulos and Obeid, **1989;** Panayiotopoulos et al., **1989a,b, 1991)** and are only briefly described herein.

## **PATIENTS AND METHODS**

## **Patients**

Our methods and patients were described previously (Panayiotopoulos et al., **1986, 1989a,b, 1991).**  An epilepsy clinic was established in King Khalid University Hospital, Riyadh, in **1983.** Patients with seizures were referred from the hospital primary care physicians. Most patients were evaluated by more than 1 of **3** to **4** consultant neurologists. In view of the difficulty in eliciting the characteristic

myoclonic jerks of JME, we had adopted the policy of physically demonstrating to our patients and their relatives mild myoclonic jerks confined to the hands and inquiring about possible events associated with waking from sleep (particularly morning tremors and clumsiness). Similarly, in view of the often inconspicuous character of absence seizures, we consistently inquired about mild and transient events of impairment of consciousness. All patients diagnosed with JME were screened to confirm that they fulfilled the study inclusion and exclusion criteria. Patients who satisfied JME criteria were studied prospectively with a uniform protocol, recording seizures, precipitating factors, circadian distribution, and family history.

Inclusion criteria for JME were (a) unequivocal clinical evidence of generalized seizures with myoclonic jerks mainly on awakening; (b) no evidence of neurologic or intellectual deficit; (c) abnormal EEG in untreated patients with generalized spike andior multiple spike-slow wave discharges (focal or background abnormalities were acceptable if other clinical and EEG criteria were strictly justified, and a normal EEG in treated patients was common); and (d) normal CT brain scan (if performed).

Exclusion criteria were (a) clinical and/or EEG evidence of myoclonic jerks secondary to brain hypoxia, metabolic diseases, or other structural brain abnormalities; (b) other epileptic syndromes such as eyelid myoclonia with absence seizures, pure forms of photosensitive epilepsy, or selfinduced epilepsy; (c) generalized seizures or tonicclonic and/or absence without firm documentation of myoclonic jerks; and (d) EEG abnormalities but no clinical evidence **of** any type of seizures.

Unlike Delgado-Escueta at al. **(1989),** we enrolled patients whose "petit ma1 seizures included characteristics of childhood absence, e.g., seizures in clusters of **20-100** per day and clinically photosensitive patients ," provided that inclusion and exclusion criteria were justified. We also included 2 siblings with typical absence and no other form of seizures because of a strong family history of JME (mother, sister, and brother) indicating that these 2 patients were diagnosed before development of myoclonic jerks.

# **EEG**

All patients underwent conventional EEGs, usually annually. Intermittent photic stimulation (IPS) was applied with combined flash and pattern stimulation (Panayiotopoulos et al., **1989a).** Patients were tested without modification of current drug treatment.

Video-EEG studies were performed through a 16-channel Telefactor Modac 1, mainly in patients with absence seizures. Our methods of recording and evaluation were described previously (Panayioto-poulos et al., 1989a,b). EEG discharges were evaluated for duration, frequency, and morphology of the complexes.

#### **Treatment**

Most patients' seizures were not controlled with the antiepileptic drug (AED) being received at the time of referral. Usually the AED was phenytoin (PHT) or carbamazepine (CBZ); only a few patients were receiving valproate (VPA) (Panayiotopoulos et al., 1991).

We initiated treatment with VPA ~300-500 mg twice daily (b.i.d.) as a starting dose. Other AEDs were discontinued slowly at monthly decrements of 50 mg for PHT, 100 mg for CBZ, and 10-20 mg for phenobarbital (PB). VPA was increased at steps of 200-400 mg/week until seizure control was achieved or unacceptable adverse reactions developed. Patients with side effects from VPA (particularly tremors, falling hair, weight gain) received clonazepam (CZP) 0.5-3 mg/day as partial substitution for VPA. Monotherapy with CZP tried in a few patients at the initial stages of this study but was abandoned because some of the patients had or developed violent GTC (Obeid and Panayiotopoulos, 1989). Values are mean ± SD.

Prevalence of JME and of seizure type is shown in Fig. 1 and Table 1. Sixty-six patients (10.2%) fulfilled the criteria for JME among 672 patients attending the epilepsy clinic: 22 (33.3%) had typical absence seizures, 64 (97.0%) had myoclonic jerks, 52 (78.8%) had GTC. Male and female patients were equally affected overall and throughout the subgroups with absence seizures, myoclonic jerks, and GTC (Table 2), but a female predominance of early onset absence seizures was observed, with a male predominance of late-onset absence seizures. Mean age of patients on referral was  $22.1 \pm 7$  years (range 8–40 years). Absence seizures had onset at  $10.5 \pm 3.4$  years (range 5–16 years). In 8 of the 22 patients with absence seizures had onset before age 10 years (median 6 years). Myoclonic jerks began at age  $15.0 \pm 3.5$  years (8–26 years), and GTC began at  $16.0 \pm 3.5$  years (range 9–28 years).

#### Circadian distribution

#### Myoclonic jerks

Myoclonic jerks in 56 (87.5%) patients occurred after they awoke from night sleep or a nap. In the remaining 8 patients (12.5%), myoclonic jerks were random during the day (7 patients) or were more frequent at night (1 patient).

#### GTC

The circadian distribution of GTC was studied in 16 patients. Seven had GTC on awakening only, and 4 had GTC mainly on awakening but also during sleep (3 patients) and randomly (1 patient). Two patients had nocturnal GTC only. The remaining 3 had diurnal GTC with no definite circadian distribution (in 1 of them, GTC were elicited only by photic stimuli).

## Typical absence seizures

Typical absence seizures could occur at any time of the day, especially during excitation and mental concentration. No relationship to waking was noted, although circadian variation was not specifically studied.

#### Clinical manifestations of seizures

#### Myoclonic jerks

Myoclonic jerks were shocklike irregular and arrhythmic clonic movements of proximal and distal muscles, mainly of the upper extremities. They were of variable amplitude and force. They were sufficiently violent to cause falls in 40 (62.5%) of the 64 patients. In others, mild jerks occurred in the fingers, making the patient clumsy or prone to drop things unexpectedly. None of our patients complained of facial or eyelid myoclonus.

# *Morphology and characteristics of discharges*

Morphology and characteristics of the discharges were described previously (Panayiotopoulos et al., **1989a,b).** The intradischarge frequency of the spike/ multiple spike slow wave complexes was mainly **3-5 Hz,** but individual complexes varied from **2** to **10 Hz.** Fragmentations of the discharges and multiple spikes with a prominent compressed capital **W**  appearance **(Ws)** were common and characteristic (Fig. **3).** EEG discharges associated with myoclonic jerks consisted mainly of polyspikes (Fig. **4).** 

#### *Focal abnormalities*

Focal abnormalities (Figs. **3** and 5) were recorded in **20 (30.3%)** patients. Ten had focal single spikes and spike-slow wave complexes. The other 10 patients showed focal slow waves consisting of sharp medium to high amplitude theta or delta waves, independently right **or** left in **9** and with unilateral preponderance in **6.**

PCR (Fig. *5)* were evoked in 18 **(27.3%)** patients; **3** had all types of seizures, **13** had myoclonic jerks

# *Background abnormalities*

Background abnormalities consisting **of** a moderately excessive theta activity and/or some slowing **of** the alpha rhythm were recorded in **28 (42.4%)**  patients.

## *Eyes-closed-related abnormalities*

Eyes-closed-related abnormalities, i.e., abnormalities appearing and sustained during the period in which eyes are closed and which are eliminated or attenuated when eyes are open (Panayiotopoulos, **1974),** were observed in **8** patients. Eyeclosure-related abnormalities, i.e., transient EEG discharges occurring **6** 1-2 s after the eyes close but not sustained during the remaining period when eyes are either closed or open (Panayiotopoulos, **1974)** occurred in **2.** No effect was noted in **4** of **14**  patients studied.

## Family history

A reliable family history was obtained from *5* **<sup>1</sup>** patients in **41** families (Table *5).* A history of epilepsy was detected in **27 (65.9%)** families: **15 (36%)**  families had at least **2** members affected by JME (Table *5).* Siblings of 13 families had JME. Cousins of the probands and siblings of unaffected parents may have had JME. Parental and half-sibling involvement with JME were noted in **1** family each. In **3** of the families with at least 2 members with JME, other relatives were reported to have GTC (Table *5).* Twelve other patients had relatives with seizures, but JME could not be confirmed: GTC in 7, symptomatic in **1,** and febrile convulsions in *5*  families of the probands.

Consanguinity was noted in **18 (43.9%)** of **41** families. There were **13 (48.1%)** consanguineous marriages among the **27** families with and among 5 **(35.7%)** of **14** without a family history of epilepsy. Consanguinity was noted in **6 (40%)** of the **15** families with at least **2** JME members as compared with **7 (58.3%)** of 12 families with seizures (no JME) in relatives of the probands.

# Diagnosis **on** referral and in the epilepsy clinic

Sixty three (95.5%) of the patients were not correctly diagnosed on referral, and JME was not initially recognized in the epilepsy clinic in **22 (33.3%),**  as previously reported (Panayiotopoulos et al., **1991).** 

# Treatment

Fifty patients had follow-up **>3** years. Fortythree were receiving VPA either alone **(29** patients) or in combination with other AEDs, mainly CZP. Patients with persistent myoclonic jerks despite optimal VPA dosage and those with VPA-related adverse reactions were treated by adding small doses of CZP (at night) and reducing VPA.

Forty-four **(88%)** patients became seizure-free, but in **10** occasional mild myoclonic jerking continued, particularly after sleep deprivation. In **7,** seizure control was achieved only after CZP **(1-4** mg daily) was combined with VPA. CZP monotherapy failed to prevent and in **1** patient may have precipitated GTC. However, **4** of *5* patients with CZP monotherapy were well controlled; **3** had a history of myoclonic jerks alone, and **1** had jerks with infrequent GTC.

Six patients continued to have seizures (GTC and myoclonic jerks) despite optimum treatment with VPA alone and/or in combination with other AEDs.

## Discontinuation **of** treatment

In **15** patients who were seizure free for **>2** years and had infrequent myoclonic jerks and GTC before treatment, VPA was reduced to **400** mg daily. There were no relapses. However, **1** of **4** patients who discontinued VPA treatment relapsed **12** months later another relapsed **16** months later. The other **2**  remained seizure-free at **1** and **2** years of follow-up.

All **7** patients who discontinued medication relapsed: **4** developed GTC **(1** and **3** weeks and **1** and 2 months after discontinuation), **1** had myoclonic status (after **2** weeks), **1** had absence status (after **3**  weeks), and **1** had a relapse of violent myoclonic jerks.

#### **DISCUSSION**

Ours is the first 5-year prospective study of clinical, EEG, video-EEG characteristics and therapeutics in patients with JME.

Although a case of JME (a doctor's son) was substantially described by Herpin in **1867** (translated in English by Grunewald and Panayiotopoulos, **1993),**  the high prevalence of JME and its importance in genetics, prognosis, and treatment has only recently been appreciated. Despite well-defined clinical and EEG features, JME remains underdiagnosed not only by general physicians but also by experienced neurologists (Delgado-Escueta and Enrile-Bacsal, **1984;** Janz, **1985;** Obeid and Panayioto-

This was also our experience with 95.5% of patients not correctly diagnosed on referral and 33.3% of those not correctly diagnosed by the epilepsy clinic neurologists. The factors contributing to these errors and their consequences were detailed previously (Panayiotopoulos et al., 1991; Anonymous, 1992; Grunewald et al., 1992; Panayiotopoulos et al., 1992). The prevalence of JME in our epilepsy clinic was 10.2%, which is slightly higher than the 8.7% prevalence detected by us in England (Grunewald et al., 1992) and by other investigators worldwide (Janz, 1989; Obeid and Panayiotopoulos, 1989).

We confirmed that JME affects male and female patients equally (Janz and Christian, 1957; Janz, 1969; Tsuboi and Christian, 1973; Delgado-Escueta and Enrile-Bacsal, 1984; Goosses, 1984; Obeid and Panayiotopoulos, 1988) and demonstrated as well that sex distribution for seizure type is equal. However, early-onset absence seizures may be more common in girls, whereas late-onset and asymptomatic (phantom) absence seizures may occur more often in boys. This age-related sex preponderance may be of interest because more girls are affected by childhood absence epilepsy (Loiseau, 1992) and more boys are affected by juvenile (Wolf, 1992) and myoclonic absence epilepsy (Tassinari and Bureau,

1985), but this finding requires further confirmation because of the small numbers of these subgroups of our patients.

Mean age at onset of myoclonic jerks (15.5 years) in the present study compares well with previous reports of 14.6 years (Janz, 1989), 14.7 (Asconapé and Penry, 1984), 16.7 (Tsuboi, 1977), 13.6 (Delgado-Escueta and Enrile-Bacsal, 1984), and 12.3 (Clement and Wallace, 1990). It was unusual for a patient to have onset of myoclonic jerks after the second decade of life, and none of our patients developed myoclonic jerks after age 26 years, despite a claim that JME may begin in later life (Gram et al., 1988). None of our JME patients was aged >40 years while 67 other patients were aged 40–80 years (none having JME). This may indicate a decrease in seizure susceptibility after the fourth decade of life, contradicting suggestions that JME is a lifelong disorder (Dreifuss, 1989). Furthermore, the senior author (C.P.P.) of this report has examined >150 JME patients; only 1 man aged 65 years and 2 women aged 43 and 44 years had persisting seizures. These 3 patients all had frequent jerks, absence seizures, and GTCs; they were undiagnosed and unsuccessfully treated for 3-5 decades.

Thus, children with typical absence seizures of JME may be diagnosed erroneously as having childhood absence epilepsy, a different epileptic syndrome (Panayiotopoulos et al., 19896).

Myoclonic jerks alone, without GTC and absence seizures occurred in 9.1% of patients, a figure that compares well with previous reports of 7-17% (AsconapC and Penry, 1984; Janz, 1985, 1989; Penry et al., 1989). These percentage of patients may be underestimated, because myoclonic jerks may be mild, being misinterpreted as early morning clumsiness or nervousness. GTC were detected in 78.8%, as compared with 8695% in previous studies (Tsuboi, 1977; Janz, 1989; Penry et al., 1989). These numbers are certainly influenced by early

recognition of JME and treatment. We noted clinical absence seizures in one third of patients; juvenile absence seizures were twice as common as childhood absence seizures. Typical absence seizures were previously reported in 14.6% (Janz, 1969), 21.5% (Tsuboi, 1977), and 39.5% (Delgado-Escueta and Enrile-Bacsal, 1984) of patients with JME. **A** characteristic clinical and EEG pattern of typical absence seizures in JME was reported previously (Panayiotopoulos et al., 1989a,b, 1992).

Study of the circadian distribution of seizures confirmed that myoclonic jerks occur on awakening (Janz, 1985, 1989), but GTC previously described as occurring only on awakening (Janz, 1989) were also nocturnal in **20% of** our patients. These findings are consistent with a sleep study of JME (Touchon, 1982). CZP, and probably other AEDs, affected the circadian distribution of GTC, which may become nocturnal (Obeid and Panayiotopoulos, 1989).

The most common seizure-precipitating factors were sleep deprivation and fatigue. This finding is at variance with the results of Penry et al. (1989), who reported that seizures were exacerbated in all their 50 patients by stress (loo%), strong emotions (96%), and chronic anxiety **(70%),** but less by sleep deprivation (42%), menses (15%), and alcohol consumption **(30%).** Photosensitivity is common in JME, although reported incidence varies, probably owing to AED differences, IPS methods, and patient age (Jeavons et al., 1980; Delgado-Escueta and Enrile-Bacsal, 1984; Wolf and Goosses, 1986; Janz, 1989; Penry et al., 1989). Eighteen patients had PCR, and 13 others reported sensitivity to light. A slight preponderance among females was noted, but we could not confirm the opinion of Janz (1989) that all female patients with only myoclonic jerks are photosensitive. In *5* patients, seizures were evoked by television and/or video games. The first case of JME and video game-induced seizures probably was reported by Dahlquist et al. (1983) of the Mayo clinic, but JME was not recognized and the patient was described as having "light-sensitive epilepsy" despite ''frequent generalized spike and slow-wave complexes and myoclonus after arousal from sleep." Patients with JME may be particularly vulnerable to video games because of accumulated precipitating factors (photosensitivity, sleep deprivation, excitement and frustration, strong emotions); patients should be appropriately warned of the risks (Ferrie et al., 1994).

The neurologic and mental state of the patients was normal except for a high incidence of essential tremor, not previously described, which could not be attributed to medication being received before VPA treatment was initiated.

The phenotypic expression of JME in this Arab population does not appear to be different from that of JME of Greek and British patients studied by one of us (C.P.P.). Four of 18 patients with JME studied in England also had essential tremor (Grunewald et al., 1992).

The family history of the 66 patients we report is similar to that of a smaller sample of patients (who all had the triad of generalized seizures, i.e., absence seizures, myoclonic jerks, and GTC) reported previously and confirms the expressed view that JME may be an autosomal recessive disorder in this population in Saudi Arabia (Panayiotopoulos and Obeid, 1989). However, different views have been expressed (Greenberg et al., 1990). Whether the genetics of this Arab population are different from those of other JME populations requires further investigation.

Our study confirmed that VPA is the preferable

AED for treatment of JME and that treatment should not be discontinued even after patients are seizure-free for a long time (Covanis et al., 1982; Christe, 1989; Dreifuss, 1989; Obeid and Panayiotopoulos, 1989; Penry et al., 1989; Janz, 1991). Relapses occurred, with instances of status epilepticus, within weeks or months in patients who voluntarily discontinued AED. Patients with myoclonic jerks had a high incidence of relapse in a recent study of AED discontinuation in patients with seizures on remission (Medical Research Council, 1991). Our study also demonstrated that reducing AEDs in patients may be safe with infrequent seizures, but relapses are likely to occur months or even years after discontinuation of medication.

Patients with violent myoclonic jerks or adverse reactions to VPA benefited from small adjunctive doses of CZP (1-2 mg at night). However, CZP monotherapy did not prevent and possibly precipitated GTC in some patients. Patients were also deprived of the warning effect of myoclonic jerks (Obeid and Panayiotopoulos, 1989) that often herald impending GTC. In patients with myoclonic jerks alone, CZP monotherapy may provide control of seizures.

#### RÉSUMÉ

Les auteurs ont entrepris une étude prospective à long terme de 66 patients présentant une épilepsie myoclonique juvénile (EMJ). La prévalence était de 10.2% parmi 672 patients présentant une épilepsie. La distribution entre les sexes était égale. Soixante trois patients n'étaient pas diagnostiqués au moment de leur premier contact; l'épilepsie myoclonique juvénile n'avait pas été reconnue initialement dans la consultation épileptologique chez 22 patients. Les absences cliniques étaient reportées chez 33,3%, des secousses myocloniques chez 97% et des crises tonico-cloniques chez 78.8% des patients. L'âge moyen du début était de  $10.5 \pm 3.4$  ans ( $\pm SD$ , limite 5 à 16) pour les absences,  $15 \pm 3.5$  (8 à 26) pour les secousses myocloniques et 16 ± 3.5 (9 à 28) pour les crises généralisées tonico-cloniques. Les absences précédaient les myoclonies de 3.4  $\pm$  2.3 années (de 1 à 9) et les crises généralisées tonico-cloniques de  $4.4 \pm 2.7$  années (1 à 8) chez les 14 patients (21.2%) qui présentaient les trois types de crises. Les absences n'ont jamais été précédées par des secousses myocloniques ou par des crises généralisées tonicocloniques. Les secousses myocloniques étaient constatées au réveil chez 87.5% des patients. Les crises généralisées tonicocloniques survenaient principalement au réveil, mais d'autres patients avaient des crises nocturnes ou diurnes sans distribution circadienne. L'examen neurologique était normal chez tous les patients, en dehors d'un tremblement des mains semblable au tremblement essentiel, constaté chez 35% des patients. Les scanners cérébraux ont toujours été normaux. Les facteurs déclenchant des crises ont été constatés chez 93% des patients: privation de sommeil chez 89.5%, fatigue chez 73.7%, photosensibilité chez 36.8% (télévision et jeux vidéo 8.8%), menstruation chez 24.1% des femmes, concentration mentale chez 22.8%, stress chez 12.3%. L'incidence de l'EMJ dans les fratries (13 des 41 familles examinées) implique un mode de transmission autosomique récessif dans cette population d'origine arabe. Les EEG étaient souvent normaux chez les patients traités. Au moins un EEG anormal a été enregistré chez 56 patients (84.9%). Les anomalies étaient représentées surtout par des décharges généralisées de pointes, doubles pointes ou poly pointes-ondes lentes. La durée des pointes multiples et des fragmentations des décharges variaient de 0.5 à 20 secondes (en movenne 6.8 secondes). Les anomalies focales ont été constatées chez 20 patients (30.3%) et des décharges photoconvulsives chez 18 patients (27.3%). Quatre vingthuit pour cent des patients sont restés sans crise pendant 3 ans ou plus de suivi. Le traitement efficace a utilisé le valproate (VPA). Le contrôle des secousses myocloniques a été amélioré avec le clonazépam (CZP). Une monothérapie par CZP n'a pas permis de prévenir de façon constante les crises généralisées tonico-clonique. Une substitution partielle utilisant le valproate, avec de petites doses de CZP, a été le traitement le plus efficace et le mieux toléré, particulièrement chez les patients présentant des effets secondaires ou nécessitant de fortes doses de VPA. La dose de VPA a été réduite avec succès chez 14 patients qui n'avaient plus de crises depuis plus

de 2 ans et qui n'avaient que de rares crises avant le traitement. Cependant, 9 patients sur 11 ont récidivé après l'arrêt du VPA.

(P. Genton, Marseille)

#### RESUMEN

Los autores han llevado a cabo un estudio prospectivo a largo plazo de 66 enfermos con Epilepsía Mioclónica Juvenil (JME). La prevalencia fue de 10.2% en 672 casos con epilepsía. La distribución entre sexos fue equivalente. Sesenta y tres casos no habían sido diagnosticados por quienes enviaban los enfermos y la JME no fue reconocida inicialmente en la clínica de epilepsía en 22 sujetos. Los típicos ataques de ausencia clínica aparecieron en 33.3%, los movimientos mioclónicos en 97% y los ataques generalizados tónico-clónicos (GTCs) en 78.8% de los enfermos. La edad media ( $\pm$ SD) al comienzo fue de 10.5  $\pm$  3.4 (rango 5-16) años para las ausencias,  $15 \pm 3.5$  (8-26) para los movimientos mioclónicos y 16 ± 3.5 (9-28) para los GTCs. Las ausencias precedieron a los movimientos mioclónicos en 3.4 ± 2.3 años (rango 1-9) y en 4.4  $\pm$  2.7 a los GTCs (rango 1-8) y en 14 años (21.2%) en enfermos que presentaban los tres tipos de ataques. Las ausencias nunca fueron precedidas de ataques mioclónicos o GTCs. Las mioclonias se presentaron durante la vigilia en 87.5% de los enfermos. Los GTCs predominaron durante la vigilia pero otros enfermos tenían GTCs nocturnos o diurnos sin distribución circadiana. La exploración neurológica fue normal en todos los pacientes con la excepción de temblor en las manos semejante al temblor esencial que apareció en el 35% de los enfermos. El CT de cerebro fue normal. En el 93% de los enfermos se encontraron factores precipitantes: la deprivación del sueño en 89.5%, la fatiga en 73.7%, la fotosensibilidad en 36.8% (televisión y juegos de video en 8.8%), la menstruación en el 24.1% de las mujeres, el retraso mental en el 22.8% y el stress en el 12.3%. La incidencia de los JME entre hermanos (13 en 41 de las familias examinadas) sugiere un modo autosómico recesivo de herencia para esta población árabe. Los EEGs fueron frecuentemente normales en los enfermos tratados. Al menos un EEG anormal fue registrado en 56 enfermos (84.9%). Las anomalías consistieron, generalmente, en descargas generalizadas de punta/doble punta y/o polipuntas y ondas lentas. La frecuencia de las múltiples puntas y la fragmentación de las descargas variaron de 0.5-20 s (promedio 6.8 s) de duración. Las anomalías focales se observaron en 20 (30.3%) y las descargas fotoconvulsivas en 18(27,3%) pacientes. El 88% de los enfermos permanecieron libres de ataques durante 3 o más años de seguimiento. La eficacia del tratamiento se consiguió con valproato (VPA) que controló los movimientos mioclónicos y mejoró con clonazepam (CZP). La monoterapia con CZP no evitó de modo consistente las GTCs. La sustitución parcial de VPA por dosis bajas de CZP fue la medicación más eficaz y mejor tolerada particularmente en enfermos que demostraban reacciones adversas o requerían dosis altas de VPA. La reducción de las dosis de VPA fue posible en 14 enfermos que estaban libres de ataques durante más de 2 años y habían tenido ataques infrecuentes antes del tratamiento. Sin embargo 9 de 11 pacientes volvieron a tener episodios al interrumpir el VPA.

(A. Portera-Sánchez, Madrid)
