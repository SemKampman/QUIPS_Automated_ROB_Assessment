# A Profile of Adolescent-Onset Epilepsy

Elisabeth Simard-Tremblay, MD, and Michael Shevell, MD, CM, FRCPC

To describe the profile of children with adolescent-onset epilepsy and to determine factors predictive of outcome. A database was searched for all patients with a first seizure between the age of 12 and 16 years. Sixty-five adolescents met inclusion criteria. Ten patients needed at least two medications to control seizures, 36 remained on medication at their last visit and 12 patients had at least 1 seizure in the year preceding. A diagnosis of juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, the presence of coexisting seizures, coexisting myoclonic seizures, age

≤14.5 years at initial diagnosis, and the presence of compliance issues were significantly associated with the need for medication at last visit. Female gender and the presence of compliance issues were associated with the occurrence of at least 1 seizure in the year preceding last visit. A good outcome for adolescent-onset epilepsy can generally be expected in the short term.

Structurally, the brain continues to gain volume until the age of 14 years, whereas white matter volume increases until the early 20s. Neurological and psychiatric comorbidities are also traditionally considered rare in this population, perhaps reflecting the often nonlesional etiology of these epilepsies.<sup>5</sup>

The recent literature has been largely directed to specific epileptic syndromes, including the well-recognized idiopathic generalized epilepsies like juvenile myoclonic epilepsy.<sup>6,7</sup> Unfortunately, not all adolescents with newonset epilepsy fall into a recognized epileptic syndrome, which explains the great need to clarify the profile of adolescent-onset epilepsy as a group. Moreover, the identification of possible risk factors for drug resistance or for a poor outcome could help in the early identification of adolescents at higher risk and thus allow the introduction of more aggressive and targeted early interventions. It is also reasonable to think that this early identification and intervention could ameliorate some of the adverse social, educational, and personal consequences related to adolescent-onset epilepsy. In this context, the objectives of this study were to describe the profile of children with adolescent-onset epilepsy and to determine factors predictive of short-term outcome.

#### Methods

Participants were identified via the standardized computerized database of a single pediatric neurologist (M.S.). Patients entered into this database were examined by the neurologist in 3 different clinical settings: (a) hospital-based ambulatory clinic, (b) hospital-based private neurology office (1991-1996), and (c) suburban private pediatric neurology clinic. The database contained 14 domains pertaining to demographic, diagnostic, and treatment windows with information entered at initial visit and updated at each follow-up visit. The database was systematically

searched for all patients with an onset of epilepsy between the age of 12 and 16 years. The medical charts of the patients previously identified were then reviewed by one investigator (E.S.-T.).

All patients meeting the following inclusion criteria and none of the exclusion criteria were included in the study. The inclusion criteria consist of (a) initial intake and neurologic assessment for a suspected seizure between July 1, 1991, and June 30, 2006, (b) first afebrile seizure between 12 and 16 years, (c) presumptive diagnosis of epilepsy (at least 2 unprovoked seizures or treatment after the first seizure), and (d) minimum follow-up period of 24 months. All patients seen in the context of a second opinion were excluded from the study.

The following information was obtained through systematic inpatient and outpatient chart review; gender, family history of epilepsy or febrile seizure, perinatal history, prior febrile seizure, comorbid conditions, seizure type at presentation, age at first suspected seizure, number of seizures prior to diagnosis, coexisting seizures, neurologic examination, most abnormal electroencephalogram (EEG) before initiation of treatment, neuroimaging, treatment, presence of an epileptic syndrome as defined by the International League Against Epilepsy, and the clinical status at last visit.

A normal perinatal history was defined as a term delivery without evident asphyxia or malformation and with no need for neonatal intensive care unit admission other than for routine observation. As comorbid conditions, the presence of mental retardation, attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder, learning disability, tics, Tourette syndrome, behavioral problems (ie, conduct disorder, oppositional defiance disorder, episodic dyscontrol syndrome), and affective and psychotic disorders were recorded. Those disorders were all defined by their respective Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (Fourth Edition) criteria. The following characteristics of the neurologic examination were noted: microcephaly, macrocephaly, and focal neurological signs. All the EEGs performed before initiation of treatment were reviewed for the presence of an abnormal background (focal or generalized), epileptiform activity (focal, multifocal, and generalized), and any evident triggers of epileptiform activity (sleep, hyperventilation, and photic stimulation). The most abnormal of these EEGs was then retained for analysis. In the rare case where no EEG was available before initiation of treatment, EEGs performed within 1 month of initiation of treatment were searched for the same features.

The treatment of epilepsy was recorded as the number and specific type of medication needed to achieve effective seizure control and documented need for ketogenic diet, vagal nerve stimulator, or epilepsy surgery. Medications stopped for an allergic reaction or side effects were not considered in the analysis. The age of the patient at last visit along with the time interval between first and last visit was recorded. It was also noted if the patient at the time of the last clinic visit was still on antiepileptic medication, the level of seizure control (excellent, moderate, poor), and the disposition of the patient at last visit (ie, continue follow-up in pediatric neurology, tapered off prior medications, transfer to adult care). Excellent control was defined as no evident seizure in the prior year while on medication, whereas moderate or poor seizure control were respectively defined as 1 to 5 seizures and more than 6 seizures in the year preceding the last clinic visit while on medication.

## Discussion

This study shows an overall good prognosis for adolescentonset epilepsy. Indeed, almost 85% of the patients were successfully controlled on monotherapy, with 10.8%, 3.1%, and 1.5% being on 2, 3, and 4 medications, respectively. A previous study performed in adolescents with both childhood-onset and adolescence-onset epilepsy showed very similar results with 85% of the sample controlled on monotherapy.<sup>3</sup> Moreover, in our study, almost 82% of the patients had excellent control of their seizures while on medication during the year preceding last followup. This overall good prognosis is concordant with the traditional thought that the great majority of adolescentonset epilepsies responds well to an antiepileptic agent and thus would be compatible with a reasonably normal quality of life.<sup>5</sup> Multiple studies performed in individuals with epilepsy of various ages have shown that a high seizure frequency and polypharmacy were strongly associated with increased psychosocial difficulties (eg, lower self-esteem, decreased quality of life, and higher perceived stigma), making the early identification of those children at higher risk of having a poor prognosis important.9-12

Noncompliance with treatment is a well-known problem encountered in the adolescent population, and multiple factors can contribute to this weakening of compliance. Adolescents with epilepsy may feel that taking the medication is ''stigmatizing'' and could represent an obstacle to an identification to their peer group. In addition, normal adolescent behavior, such as defiance, a search for independence, provocation, and challenge of authority, could also lead to noncompliance with medication.13,14 In a previous study, one third (33%) of teenagers with epilepsy were found to have demonstrated a poor compliance with their treatment, 43% had a satisfactory degree of compliance, and only 24% had a good degree of compliance.<sup>15</sup> This represents a higher proportion of noncompliance than the one found in our study (18.5%), which could be explained by our identification of a compliance issue based on documentation in the chart by the physician (through questioning or sub-therapeutic serum drug levels) at the time of visit, which could have underestimated the real proportion of noncompliance. On top of the higher risk of seizure recurrence, poor compliance in adolescents has also been found to be associated with restriction of independence in daily life, a lack of harmony in family life, and low self-esteem.<sup>16</sup>

Almost 56% of patients remained on antiepileptic medication at last visit. A diagnosis of juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, the presence of coexisting seizures, coexisting myoclonic seizures, age -14.5 years at first seizure, and the presence of a compliance issue were all associated with the need for antiepileptic medication at last visit. Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, one of the idiopathic generalized epilepsy syndromes, is a well-described syndrome that usually has its onset during adolescence. The natural history of this disorder is usually one necessitating long-term treatment with antiepileptic medication because of a high rate of recurrence of seizures after the discontinuation of treatment. In the current study, among the factors associated with the need for antiepileptic medication at last visit, the presence of coexisting myoclonic seizures can easily be linked with a diagnosis of juvenile myoclonic epilepsy. Furthermore, more than half of our patients who had coexisting seizures also had a diagnosis of juvenile myoclonic epilepsy. To our knowledge, this is the first study showing a link between the age at first suspected seizure and a need for prolonged treatment with antiepileptic medication.

Sixty percent of our cohort had a diagnosis of an idiopathic generalized epilepsy syndrome with the remainder diagnosed with a partial epilepsy syndrome. Other studies performed in adolescents with both childhoodonset and adolescent-onset epilepsy have shown similar results with 58% of the cohort with a diagnosis of idiopathic generalized epilepsy,<sup>3</sup> whereas other studies have found a smaller proportion of 43.9%.2 Moreover, in our study, one third of the adolescents with an idiopathic generalized epilepsy syndrome were classified as having juvenile myoclonic epilepsy and almost 62% were classified with another generalized idiopathic epilepsy syndrome. Previous studies have shown that among patients with idiopathic generalized epilepsy, 1 in 4 to 1 in 5 were diagnosed with juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, 17 which represents a smaller proportion than in our population. Interestingly, no patient was diagnosed with epilepsy with grand mal on awakening in our study. Among the broad group of idiopathic generalized epilepsy, the heterogeneous partition of epileptic syndromes found in different studies could be the result of the overlapping definitions of the individual syndromes. Furthermore, some experts now consider that the individual syndromes included in the idiopathic generalized epilepsies are part of a clinical continuum rather than actually being separate discrete syndromes or disease entities.6

A high proportion of learning disorders (15.2%) and mental retardation (6.2%) was found in our sample of patients with adolescent-onset epilepsy. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (Fourth Edition, Text Revision), the prevalence of learning disorders in the general population is thought to be 2% to 10%. It is well-known that pediatric epilepsies are associated with a higher rate of learning disorders than in the general population and that up to 50% of children with epilepsy have school-related difficulties. 18 Adolescent-onset epilepsy was traditionally thought to occur in previously normal children without neurologic or neurodevelopmental defects.<sup>5</sup> At this point, the relationship between adolescentonset epilepsy and cognition remains largely unknown and needs to be clarified. In fact, some studies showed that teenagers with epilepsy and a comorbid learning disorder are at higher risk of adopting poor health behaviors and thus represent a more vulnerable subset of patients with adolescent-onset epilepsy. 19

In conclusion, a good outcome can generally be expected in adolescent-onset epilepsy. This study showed that a diagnosis of juvenile myoclonic epilepsy and the presence of coexisting seizures, particularly myoclonic seizures, were associated with a prolonged need for antiepileptic medication, which is in agreement with the well-described long-term outcome of this syndrome. In addition, we found a higher rate of learning disorders in our sample than what is described for the general population, which is in opposition to the traditional thought that adolescent-onset epilepsy occurs in "neurologically normal" individuals. Finally, the presence of a compliance issue was found not to surprisingly have a negative impact on seizure control. New strategies need to be developed to improve compliance in this challenging population. This could be started by giving more information on the importance of seizure control or using more concrete arguments such as the possible driving restrictions that need to be imposed in cases of inadequate seizure control.
