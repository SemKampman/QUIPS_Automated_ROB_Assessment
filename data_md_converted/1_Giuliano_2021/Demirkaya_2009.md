## **Research Article**

# **Clinical and EEG Analysis of Patients With Idiopathic Generalized Epilepsy and Photosensitivity**

Nazlı DEMİRKAYA, Ebru Nur VANLI YAVUZ, Ebru ALTINDAĞ, Betul BAYKAN *İstanbul University, İstanbul School of Medicine, Dept. of Neurology, İstanbul, Turkey*

# **Summary**

**Introduction:** Our aim is to define the electroclinical characteristics of patients with photosensitive idiopathic generalized epilepsy (IGE) and to investigate the differences in seizure types, therapy and outcome between subgroups, based on photoparoxysmal response (PPR)/EEG characteristics and different syndromes.

**Patients and Methods:** Fifty consecutive patients with a diagnosis of IGE and having EEG or clinical evidence for photosensitivity have been identified from the epilepsy outpatient clinic database of the Department of Neurology (Istanbul Medical Faculty, Istanbul University). Clinical and EEG data was collected retrospectively.

**Results:** Thirty-six female and 14 male patients with median age 25.5 (14-63) were included to the study. The juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME) subgroup (n:24) had a significantly higher current age (p=0.035) and age at onset of seizures (p=0.029) compared with the absence epilepsy (AE) (n:12). Sleep deprivation triggers the seizures significantly more often in the JME subgroup compared with AE (p=0.003). Seizure records during EEG significantly more often in patients with AE (p=0.016). Patients with prolonged PPRs experienced significantly more clinical seizures during an EEG recording than patients with self-limited PPRs (p=0.000). The patients who were not seizure free had significantly more absence seizures (AS) (initially) compared with the patients who became seizure free (p=0.001).

**Conclusion:** Our findings in a retrospective cohort suggest that having AS is a negative prognostic factor for the seizure outcome in patients with photosensitive IGE.

**Key words:** PPR, photosensitivity, IGE

# **INTRODUCTION**

Photosensitivity (PS) is an abnormal sensitivity of the brain to visual/light stimuli. It can be detected with electroencephalogram (EEG) as a paroxysmal epileptiform reaction to intermittent photic stimulation (IPS), which was called as photoparoxysmal response (PPR)(3,19,20) and have been demonstrated in epileptic as well as nonepileptic persons(10,15,18,21). Studies in epileptic patients show that an epileptiform response to IPS is found in about 10-20% of children and 5-10% of adults. This response is more common in females at any age(12,23) .

It has been known that flickering sunlight can provoke epileptic seizures. Other typical trigger factors include TV, computer and video games, venetian blinds, striped walls, rolling stairs, striped clothing and natural and environmental lighting. Additional human factors such as cognitive abilities, emotional factors, fatigue and sleep deprivation may facilitate the occurrence of PPRs and seizures(3,5,11,20). Individual factors such as age and gender and many other, unknown possibly genetic factors seem to influence the expression of the PPR(17). The precise mechanisms of inheritance of PS are not known yet and seem to be complex, involving several genes, which probably interact with each other.

PS can be assessed with different diagnostic procedures, but the most common method is IPS(16). Different protocols and photic stimulators are used across laboratories(10,14). A widely used EEG classification system was proposed by Waltz(22); subclassifying the PPR in four phenotypically different types:

PPR type I: spikes within the occipital rhytm

PPR type II: parieto-occipital spikes and biphasic slow waves

PPR type III: parieto-occipital spikes and biphasic slow waves spreading to frontal regions

PPR type IV: generalized spikes or polyspikes and waves

Prolonged (self-sustained) PPRs, which outlast the stimulus train, can be distinguished from self-limited PPRs, which cease spontaneously or when the flashes stop. Some studies suggest an association between prolonged PPRs and a higher incidence of seizures(14). The relation between the type of IPS response and risk of visually induced seizures is still unclear (20) .

The clinical signs and symptoms when exposed to photic stimuli can vary from mild subjective symptoms to all kinds of epileptic seizures like myoclonic seizures (MS), absence seizures (AS), generalized tonic-clonic seizures (GTCS) and focal seizures(3,6,11). PS is most commonly associated with idiopathic generalized epilepsies (IGEs), such as juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME) and childhood absence epilepsy (CAE).

The aims of this study are to define the electroclinical characteristics of patients with photosensitive IGE and to study differences in seizure types, therapy and outcome between subgroups, based on PPR/EEG characteristics and different syndromes of idiopathic generelized epilepsy such as JME and AE.

## **MATERIAL AND METHODS**

# **Patient selection**

Fifty patients with a diagnosis of IGE and having EEG or clinical evidence for photosensitivity have been identified from the epilepsy outpatient clinic database of the Department of Neurology (Istanbul Medical Faculty, Istanbul University). EEG evidence for photosensitivity was obtained from available EEG traces.

*Inclusion criteria:* Patients with a diagnosis of IGE and having EEG or clinical evidence for PS, follow-up period ≥ 1 year (last follow-up in 2007), no evidence of neurologic deficit or mental retardation, CT/MR scans normal or nonspecific/unrelated to epilepsy if performed.

## **Study protocol**

*EEG protocol* 

IPS protocol: The standard IPS protocol starts with eyes open for 5 seconds and then eyes closed for 5 seconds; the stimulus trains of 5-10-15-20-25-30-35 Hz are given at a distance of 30 cm in a normally illuminated room. If PS is found or suspected, the highest and lowest Hz ranges are determined.

Defining and grading PS: A clear distinction is being made between interictal epileptiform EEG-discharges to IPS and clinical seizures provoked by IPS. In the situation where epileptiform discharges are already present during rest and hyperventilation (HV), PS is assumed if twofold or more increase during IPS. The IPS response is graded according to Waltz's classification(22) by the investigators.

Clinical data: The following clinical parameters were assessed from the outpatient medical records: diagnosis/syndrome (JME, AE or other IGE), sex, current age, age at onset of epilepsy, follow-up period, past history/associated medical conditions, family history of epilepsy, febrile convulsions ever, status epilepticus ever, subjective symptoms ever, triggers in daily life, seizure types, therapy (AED) and seizure outcome. Remission was defined as seizure free for ≥ 5 years, regardless of AED treatment status.

EEG data: The following information was obtained from the available EEG reports/traces: age at first and last EEG, total number of EEGs, PPR type, % EEGs with a PPR, presence of interictal spontaneous epileptiform discharges, epileptiform discharges during HV, eye closure sensitivity, background activity, ictal findings, presence of a prolonged PPR and the range of IPS frequencies inducing a PPR.

## **Statistical analysis**

The statistical analysis was done with SPSS for Windows version 15.0. For continuous variables, associations were evaluated using the Mann-Whitney U test for two independent samples. Associations between categorical variables were assessed by using the Chi-square (Pearson Chi-square) test. Analysis of the same patient group on two different dates was done by using the nonparametric McNemar test for two related samples. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

At the first visit presentation, 46 (92%) patients had experienced one or more generalized tonic-clonic seizures (GTCS), at last follow-up only 4 (8%) patients reported still having GTCS (p=0.000). Valproic acid (VPA) constituted the AED therapy in the majority of the (62%) patients. No significant differences in treatment status were found between the JME and the AE patients.

### **Prolonged PPR versus self-limited PPR**

22 (48%) of 46 patients showed prolonged PPRs and 24 (52%) self-limited PPRs on EEG. There were no significant differences between these 2 subgroups in any of the clinical, EEG and seizure charecteristics. However, patients with prolonged PPRs experienced significantly more clinical seizures during an EEG recording than patients with self-limited PPRs (p=0.000).

**Factors associated with seizure outcome**  Significantly more AS (initially) were noted in the group of patients who were not seizure free compared to the group of patients who became seizure free (p=0.001). In addition, the combination of AS+MS+GTCS (initially) occurred significantly more in this patient group (0.009) and the combination of MS+GTCS (initially) occurred significantly more in the patient group that became seizure free (p=0.003).

## **Follow-up data**

The median follow-up period was 7 years (range, 1-22 years) for the whole photosensitive IGE group, 9 years (range, 2-22 years) for the JME subgroup and 6.5 years (range, 2-14 years) for the AE subgroup. At last follow-up, 19 (38%) patients reported being seizure free (9 JME patients, 5 AE patients and 5 other IGE patients). Eight patients (16%) had achieved complete remission of seizures ≥ 5 years and 2 of them were no longer taking AEDs. There were no significant differences in seizure outcome/remission between the JME patients and AE patients. No relation was noted between seizure outcome and family history of epilepsy, past history, visual triggers, sex, current age, age at onset of seizures, ictal findings during EEG, interictal spontaneous epileptiform discharges and prolonged PPR.

### **DISCUSSION**

Electroclinical characteristics of the patients with photosensitive IGE were investigated in this study. There is an obvious female preponderance (2.6:1), in concordance with literature(7,9) .

All patients showed a PPR during IPS; the majority (42/50; 84%) expressed a PPR type IV. At least 80% of patients with photosensitive epilepsy have a history of seizures precipitated by environmental visual stimuli(22). Similar results were found in this group: 35 (70%) patients reported having seizures triggered by visual stimuli; especially TV and (sun)light.

There was a significant decrease in prevalence of all seizure types and 19 (38%) patients reported being seizure free at last follow-up. Eight patients (16%) had achieved complete remission of seizures ≥ 5 years and 2 of them were no longer taking AEDs. This might be explained by suppression of seizures by appropriate treatment or a possible favourable prognosis of photosensitive IGE. In literature, the prognosis of photosensitive epilepsy is not clear(8,19). Harding et al.(8) state that there is no particular age at which photosensitivity disappears and their study indicates a 14-37% probability that photosensitivity will disappear spontaneously. VPA is the drug of first choice in treating photosensitive patients(8,9) and accordingly, in this cohort, the majority of patients used VPA to control their seizures.

Determination of IPS sensitivity range can be used as a measure of epileptogenesis. This range is related to liability of seizures in daily life, i.e. patients with a greater range have a higher likelihood of visuallyinduced seizures than those with a smaller range. In this cohort, most patients were sensitive at a IPS-range between 10-20 Hz and 20-30 Hz; 38 (93%) and 33 (81%) of 41 patients, respectively. This is in concordance with what is known in literature: the lower and upper limits are usually between 10 and 30 Hz(13) and the most provocative frequencies are in the range of 15-25 Hz, although some individuals are sensitive to single flashes or to frequencies as high as 65 Hz<sup>(10)</sup>.

#### JME versus AE

The cohort could be divided into three groups, depending on the type of syndrome: JME, AE and other IGE. The JME subgroup had a significant higher current age (p=0.035) and age at onset of seizures (p=0.029) compared with the AE subgroup. The AE group included all absence syndromes/types and could therefore also include patients with CAE, who in general have a younger age at onset of seizures than patients with JME.

The occurrence of sleep deprivation as a seizure trigger was reported significantly more often in the JME subgroup compared with the AE subgroup (p=0.003) as expected. However, sleep deprivation may precipitate seizures in all kinds of photosensitive epilepsies<sup>(3,5,11,20)</sup>.

Ictal clinical phenomena occurred significantly more in patients of the AE subgroup, compared with the JME subgroup (p=0.016). This could be due to a higher sensitivity of the AE patients to IPS, although no significant differences in sensitivity range, PPR type or prolonged PPR were observed between the 2 groups.

There were also no significant differences in seizure types/outcome, AED treatment status and follow-up between the AE and JME patients.

### **Prolonged PPR versus self-limited PPR**

22 (48%) of 46 patients showed prolonged PPRs and 24 (52%) self-limited PPRs on EEG. Puglia et al<sup>(14)</sup> concluded in their study that a prolonged PPR is associated with a higher incidence of seizures than a self-limited response, but that patients with a prolonged PPR more often had other epileptiform abnormalities than the self-limited group. In our group there were no significant differences in seizure incidence or other epileptiform abnormalities on EEG between patients with prolonged PPRs and self-limited PPRs. However, patients with prolonged PPRs experienced

significantly more clinical seizures during an EEG recording than patients with self-limited PPRs (p=0.000).

#### Factors associated with seizure outcome

The patients who were not seizure free had significantly more AS (initially) compared with the patients who became seizure free (p=0.001). In addition, the combination of AS+MS+GTCS (initially) significantly more in this patient group (p=0.009) and the combination MS+GTCS (initially) occurred significantly more in the patient group that became seizure free (p=0.003). These findings suggest that having AS is a negative prognostic factor for the seizure outcome. In literature, the association of photosensitivity with typical absence seizures has been shown to be a poor prognostic factor<sup>(4)</sup>. Similarly, Baykan et al. (2) concluded in their study that (photosensitive) typical absence seizures are associated with an unremitting course.

This is a non-randomized retrospective cohort study with a relatively small number of patients. The AE subgroup included all types of absence syndromes (like CAE en JAE) what makes comparison and interpretation of results between subgroups difficult. So it was not clear but our findings suggest that having AS is a negative prognostic factor for the seizure outcome in patients with photosensitive IGE.
