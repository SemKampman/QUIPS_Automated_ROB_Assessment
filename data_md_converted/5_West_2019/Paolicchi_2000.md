## **Predictors of outcome in pediatric epilepsy surgery**

J.M. Paolicchi, MD; P. Jayakar, MD, PhD; P. Dean, MSN, ARNP; I. Yaylali, MD, PhD; G. Morrison, MD; A. Prats, MD; T. Resnik, MD; L. Alvarez, MD; and M. Duchowny, MD

**Article abstract—***Objective:* To determine the correlation between pre- and perioperative variables on the outcome of children undergoing focal resections for medically intractable partial epilepsy. *Methods:* Retrospective analysis of pre- and perioperative variables in a cohort of 75 patients younger than 12 years of age who underwent excisional surgery and had at least 1 year of follow-up. Outcome, measured by postoperative seizure frequency, was analyzed as a function of age at seizure onset, duration of epilepsy, presence of cognitive impairment, lobe of seizure origin, presence of a lesion, histopathology, and completeness of resection. Completeness of resection was defined on the basis of excising both the entire structural lesion if present and the region revealing prominent interictal and ictal abnormalities on intracranial EEG. *Results:* Seventy-seven percent of patients had good outcomes (class 1 or 2), and 59% were seizure-free. Lesional status, site of resection, and pathologic diagnoses were not significant predictors of outcome except for in multilobar resection, for which overall outcome was relatively poor (44% class 3 or 4; 22% seizure-free). Completeness of resection was the only significant predictor of good outcome (*p* , 0.001), with 92% of patients who underwent complete resection of the epileptogenic zone achieving good outcome compared with 50% of patients who had incomplete resections. *Conclusion:* In this series of pediatric patients, complete resection of the lesion and the electrographically abnormal region was the main determinant of outcome after focal resections. Except for multilobar resections, other factors examined in this study did not significantly influence postoperative seizure prognosis and should not influence candidate selection for the surgical process. **Key words:** Epileptogenic zone—Focal epilepsy—Nonlesional temporal lobe epilepsy—Pediatric epilepsy— Surgical outcome—Dysplasia—Pathology.

NEUROLOGY 2000;54:642–647

Surgical excision of the epileptogenic region is an established therapeutic option for children with medically resistant partial seizures. Although the success of surgical therapy in children is well documented,1-8 it is generally assumed that factors associated with good outcome in adults are operative in childhood. Thus, for example, children with normal imaging studies, severe cognitive delay, or extratemporal seizures are denied surgery and referred for alternative treatments including the ketogenic diet and vagal nerve stimulation. Although these alternative therapies may temporarily alleviate or reduce seizures,9,10 definitive cure occurs only in a small percentage of individuals.

Several features of pediatric surgical candidates distinguish them from their adult counterparts and are potential determinants of postoperative outcome. For example, the pathologic substrate of chronic childhood epilepsy frequently results from disordered cortical development.2,5,7,11,12 Affected patients are prone to early seizure onset and higher incidence of extratemporal seizures.3,4,6 An understanding of the influence of pre- and perioperative variables in this setting would facilitate candidate selection and potentially improve the seizure prognosis.

To address these issues, we reviewed our experi-

ence with children younger than 12 years of age undergoing excisional surgery for epilepsy, in order to help determine which pre- and perioperative variables predicted surgical outcome. We analyzed outcome based on age at seizure onset, duration of epilepsy, presence of cognitive impairment, lobe of involvement, presence of a gross structural lesion, histopathologic findings, and completeness of the resection.

**Methods.** *Patients.* We retrospectively reviewed the presurgical data from a 10-year period in a cohort of 83 patients younger than 12 years of age who underwent focal resection for epilepsy and had follow-up of at least 1 year. All had longstanding medically intractable epilepsy, defined as seizures not responding to adequate trials of maximally tolerated antiepileptic drugs.13 We excluded eight patients on the basis of incomplete follow-up (n 5 5) or death (n 5 3; two nonsurgical, one postoperative), leaving a total of 75 patients (40 boys, 35 girls) for analysis.

*Presurgical evaluation.* All patients underwent preoperative video EEG and MRI (0.5-tesla or 1.5-tesla magnet). There were 35 nonlesional and 40 lesional cases. The 1.5 tesla MRI unit was obtained at our institution in mid-1992; 47 cases were performed prior to then (26 lesional, 21 nonlesional), and 28 cases were performed subsequently (14 lesional and 14 nonlesional). Ictal SPECT was performed in nine patients using standardized protocols.14 Developmental level was assessed by neurologic evaluation and formal neuropsychological testing.

Fifty-five (73%) patients had extraoperative monitoring. These included all nonlesional cases and 20 (50%) of 40 lesional cases. Resections in the remaining 20 lesional cases were guided by intraoperative electrocorticography. Extraoperative intracranial recording was obtained using subdural electrodes (strips or grids). If appropriate, six or eight contact-depth electrodes were placed strategically into a deep-seated lesion guided by MRI findings or intraoperative ultrasound.15-17 Functional mapping was performed utilizing direct electrical stimulation whenever epileptogenic activity was recorded in proximity to eloquent cortex.18

*Epileptogenic zone and surgical resections.* Resections were planned and carried out on the basis of neuroimaging (CT or MRI) and intracranially recorded electrophysiologic data. Whenever possible, lesions were resected in their entirety, and all cases of preoperatively identified hippocampal sclerosis (n 5 6) prompted resections of the mesial temporal structures.

For patients undergoing one-stage excisional procedures, regions of active spiking on the pre-excision electrocorticography were used to define the epileptogenic zone and resection plane. Prominent interictal spiking was considered significant if it showed consistent focality or had rhythmic features, occurred as trains of focal "fast" activity, or was associated with focal attenuation of background. In contrast, infrequent spikes, spikes without consistent focality, or "rim" spikes recorded on the postresection electrocorticograph were ignored.

In implanted patients, the ictal onset zone was the single most critical factor in determining the epileptogenic region, especially in patients with normal imaging studies. We defined ictal onset zones as regions exhibiting focal rhythmic activity, bursts of high-frequency discharges, repetitive spiking, or electrodecremental patterns.15-17 In addition, secondary foci that consistently activated intraictally19 (during a seizure) were always included in the resection if they occurred in tissue adjacent or in regional proximity to the primary ictal focus. Sinusoidal theta or delta frequencies, which occurred over widespread regions soon after ictal onset, were not regarded as critical to the completeness of resection. Consistent with the electrocorticograph data, regions of prominent interictal spiking and background abnormalities were also considered significant.

*Cortical resections.* None of the temporal resections were "standard"; all included the anterior neocortical and mesial limbic structures, tailored posteriorly according to EEG, lesional data, and location of language cortex.20 For seizures that originated posteriorly, resection of the temporal convexity and basal neocortex was extended further posteriorly, with the vein of Labbe being undercut if needed.12,20

Extratemporal resections consisted of complete removal of the lesion combined with corticectomy tailored to the epileptogenic region. Anterior frontal epileptogenic regions were often treated by medial or lateral wedge resections; posterior frontal, parietal, and occipital foci were more likely to be treated by tailored corticectomy alone.

The resection was judged complete only if the region of significant EEG abnormalities defined previously and the structural lesion (if present) were removed in their entirety. Resections were judged incomplete if either the structural lesion or the electrographically abnormal zone could not be completely excised because of proximity to functionally critical cortex. Completeness versus incompleteness was determined at the time of the resection by the epilepsy team present and was subsequently reviewed by the authors postoperatively using the primary data. In lesional cases, this determination also included the gross appearance of the surgical field at the time of the resection, and the subsequent review of postoperative neuroimaging data.

For study purposes, we analyzed surgical variables according to the following criteria:

- 1) Lobe resected: temporal, extratemporal, or combined multilobar (temporal and extratemporal)
- 2) Staging: one-stage resection performed under electrocorticographic guidance or two-stage surgery utilizing extraoperative interictal and ictal data
- 3) Resection: complete or incomplete
- 4) Histopathology: prenatally (cortical dysplasia, hamartoma, developmental tumor) or peri- or postnatally (infectious, vascular, hypoxic, neoplastic, and hippocampal sclerosis) acquired

*Follow-up.* Postoperative seizure status was assessed during outpatient visits and telephone contact at least yearly after the first postoperative year. Seizure outcome was classified according to a generally accepted classification scheme9: class 1, seizure-free (SF); class 2, greater than 90% seizure reduction; class 3, greater than 50% seizure reduction; and class 4, unchanged (no change or less than 50% seizure reduction). Patients who had persistent seizures after the immediate postoperative period and required multiple manipulations of their antiepileptic drug regimen to gain seizure freedom we classified as class 2. For purposes of data analysis, classes 1 and 2 were classified as good outcomes, and classes 3 and 4 were considered poor outcome. Mean follow-up was 5 years (range 1 to 10 years).

*Statistical analysis.* Standard x<sup>2</sup> analysis was performed comparing good versus poor and SF versus non-SF outcome against the variables described. Stepwise, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed using univariate conditions (presence of cognitive impairment, temporal or extratemporal resection, lesional versus nonlesional resection, developmental versus acquired pathology, complete versus incomplete resection, and good versus poor outcome or SF versus non-SF outcome) and continuous variables (age at epilepsy onset and duration of epilepsy prior to surgery).

Differences between outcomes in complete and incomplete resection group were highly significant ( $\chi^2 = 16.96$ , p < 0.001 for good versus poor outcome;  $\chi^2 = 16.5$ , p < 0.001 for seizure freedom).

Stepwise, multiple logistic regression analysis. Age at epilepsy onset, duration of epilepsy before surgical resection, presence of cognitive impairment, temporal versus extratemporal resection, lesional versus nonlesional resection, developmental versus acquired pathology, and complete versus incomplete resection were compared with good versus poor outcome. For purposes of analysis, developmental pathology included cortical dysplasia and developmental lesions; acquired pathology included perior postnatally acquired pathology and nondevelopmental masses.

On univariate analysis, completeness of resection was the only significant variable. The OR for completeness of resection was 11, indicating that the probability of a poor outcome is 11 times greater after an incomplete resection than a complete resection. Logistic regression for continuous variables, such as duration of epilepsy, did not identify significant predictors of outcome. If completeness of resection was standardized, no other variables were found to be significant. Using seizure freedom versus nonseizure freedom in the analysis, completeness of resection was also the only significant variable, with an OR of 7.8.

**Discussion.** Our data demonstrate that surgery for pharmacoresistant partial epilepsy is most effective if a complete resection is performed. In contrast

to the adult experience, a temporal lobe location of the seizure focus<sup>21-24</sup> and the presence of a gross structural lesion<sup>21-25</sup> do not significantly improve outcome. Of the pre- and perioperative variables included in our analysis, completeness of the resection was the main determinant of outcome, with 92% of patients who had complete resections achieving good results compared with a 50% improvement rate with incomplete resections.

Multilobar resections had less favorable outcome in terms of seizure freedom, but the significance was not demonstrated in logistic regression analysis. Given the small number of patients in our series with multilobar resections, further analysis of the factors contributing to the significance is limited, but it suggests that widespread areas of epileptogenesis are more difficult to remove in their entirety.

Our results provide support for the adult surgical experience reported by Rossi et al.<sup>26</sup> They retrospectively reviewed 138 surgically treated epilepsy patients and found that the presence of a lesion did not predict surgical outcome. Similar to our cohort, complete removal of the structural lesion and epileptogenic zone in adults was highly predictive of success and was independent of the surgical approach. Our findings extend these observations to the pediatric epilepsy surgical population, in which there are high proportions of nonlesional and extratemporal cases, and a higher proportion of candidates with disorders of cortical development.

Our findings also reveal an absence of any correlation between postoperative prognosis and tissue histopathology or lesional status. This finding is unexpected and contrasts with the adult experience. This may be, in part, attributable to the greater proportion of dysplastic lesions in our series compared with the higher proportion of adult cases with hippocampal sclerosis, which tend to have good outcomes. Although hippocampal sclerosis is also seen in pediatric surgical candidates with chronic temporal lobe epilepsy, 2,5,7 it may be encountered in the setting of dual pathology. 5,7

Histopathologic lesions are likely to differ in their epileptogenic properties, however, and these differences should influence the surgical approach. For example, anatomically well-defined lesions such as DNETs have been shown to have restricted epileptic zones30 and respond well to one-stage lesionectomy. Without a preoperative pathologic diagnosis, a comprehensive workup with detailed electrophysiologic definition of the epileptogenic region should be obtained.

To perform a complete resection, the electrographically abnormal zone or lesion must be well localized preoperatively. Application of the electrophysiologic definition of completeness is unlikely to be uniform among epilepsy centers. At our center, regions of prominent interictal activity and attenuated background and ictal onset zones, as well as intraictally activated regions,19 are regarded as electrographically significant; remote regions of infrequent spiking are not considered important. Completeness also includes the removal of lesional tissue in its entirety. In this series, lesional status was based primarily on CT or MRI scans. PET data were not included in the preoperative evaluation. In recent years we have incorporated focal ictal SPECT hyperperfusion in our surgical planning, but its role as an outcome predictor was not analyzed.

A high proportion of our population underwent two-stage evaluations with extraoperative subdural recording. This trend is influenced by referral patterns to our institution for more complicated and nonlesional cases. Nearly one-half of our cases were nonlesional, a proportion appreciably higher than the average case mix. Because higher proportions of nonlesional and extratemporal cases are more characteristic of the pediatric population, implantation of a higher proportion of children is likely to continue. Higher field-strength MRI capabilities and the inclusion of ictal SPECT in presurgical protocols should ultimately reduce the number of pediatric patients requiring subdural implantation.

The importance of performing a complete resection has significant implications for surgical planning and patient counseling. If the presurgical evaluation indicates a high likelihood of epileptogenic involvement of critical cortex, our data indicate that the incidence of seizure freedom is markedly reduced. The reduced likelihood of seizure freedom without functional deficit should be discussed openly before implantation, because families may understandably decline surgery. The need for multilobar resection and the potential involvement of functionally critical cortex present the greatest challenges for improving the patient's seizures and require further innovation before similar rates of seizure freedom can be achieved.

Clinical, EEG, imaging, and neuropsychological features assist in the presurgical evaluation for temporal lobectomy in adults,31 and multivariate algorithms for predicting success have been described.32 In contrast, no preoperative factors predict seizure outcome in pediatric temporal lobectomy candidates.33 Our current findings regarding the importance of a complete surgical resection may help to explain the poor predictive power of preoperative variables. Although the need for a multilobar resection may be a factor, the duration of epilepsy, frequency of seizures, or presence of mental retardation should not influence the determination of surgical candidacy in pediatric epilepsy surgery candidates. Instead, the preoperative evaluation must be individualized, because children with normal imaging studies or cortical dysplasia may be strong surgical candidates.

# **Reduction of corpus callosum growth after severe traumatic brain injury in children**

H.S. Levin, PhD; D.A. Benavidez, PhD; K. Verger-Maestre, PhD; N. Perachio, MA; J. Song, MS; D.B. Mendelsohn, MD; and J.M. Fletcher, PhD

**Article abstract—***Objective:* To study effects of closed head injury (CHI) severity on development of corpus callosum (CC) in children, using MRI. *Background:* Vulnerability of CC to diffuse axonal injury has been shown in adults and children by neuropathologic and MRI studies. Given continued development of CC through the second decade, serial MRI could characterize effects of CHI on CC growth in children. *Methods:* MRI performed at 3 and 36 months after severe (mean age 5 10.3 years, n 5 25) and mild to moderate (mean age 5 9.7 years, n 5 28) CHI. Mild to moderate and severe CHI groups did not differ in demographic features. Morphometry of T1-weighted midsagittal CC by two operators with satisfactory interrater reliability yielded uncorrected and corrected CC volume. *Results:* An interaction of occasion with CHI severity was present as CC area decreased from 3 to 36 months in severely injured children and increased in the mild to moderate CHI group. Apart from potential use as a marker of residual brain injury after CHI, deficient CC development could compromise performance of activities that stress interhemispheric integration. To investigate the effects of CHI severity on development of the CC, we obtained repeat MRIs at 3 and 36 months postinjury in children and adolescents who had sustained severe or mild to moderate CHI. **Patients and methods.** *Patients.* Fifty-three pediatric CHI patients were recruited from consecutive admissions to the neurosurgical services at Parkland/Children's Medical Centers in Dallas and Hermann Hospital in Houston (table). Selection criteria included age 5 to 15 years at the time of injury, age 5 to 18 years at the time of initial and follow-up MRIs, and hospitalization for CHI with CT within 24 hours of injury. This study was part of a larger project that stipulated a lower age limit of 5 years because it was not feasible to administer the cognitive tests to younger children. Exclusion criteria were previously diagnosed neuropsychiatric disorder, including previous hospitalization for CHI, epilepsy, or developmental disorders such as attention deficit–hyperactivity disorder. Severe CHI was defined by a postresuscitation Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)15 score of 8 or less without a previous interval of normal consciousness irrespective of CT findings, whereas a mild CHI was defined by lowest GCS score of 13 to 15, normal neurologic examination, normal CT findings other than a linear skull fracture, and no extracranial injury requiring general anesthesia for surgical repair. Moderate CHI was defined by a lowest postresuscitation GCS score of 9 to 12 irrespective of other findings. In view of neuropathologic and MRI studies10,11 in adults indicating that CC atrophy occurs primarily in CHI producing immediate onset of coma, we compared the findings of the severely injured patients (n 5 25) with the results obtained from the combined mild to moderate CHI groups (n 5 28). All 53 head-injured children enrolled in a large project on outcome of pediatric CHI who met the eligibility criteria, including initial and follow-up MRI, were included in this study. Consequently, no power analysis was performed.

Assessment of functional outcome. Magnetic resonance imaging. MRI for all 53 patients in this study was scheduled to approximate 3 months and 36 months postinjury (see table). These MRI intervals corresponded to scheduled follow-up assessments, thus limiting the number of return visits requested of the families. Based on previous MRI investigation of pediatric CHI,6 3 months was selected for the initial MRI because transient effects such as edema resolve by this time, and the child is better able to cooperate with the scan. Although neurodegenerative changes can be seen by 3 months after severe CHI,5 the 3-year follow-up was selected to investigate the impact of severe CHI on CC development. All CC measurements were obtained from digitized midsagittal T1 weighted images. Technologic advances in MRI permitted a reduction in slice thickness from 5 mm to 1.5 mm over the 5-year course of study (1990 to 1995).

Anatomic measures. A single T1 midsagittal slice in which the fourth ventricle, pituitary stalk, lower brainstem, and massa intermedia were visible was used to measure the CC area and estimate the total brain area. Two operators who had no information regarding the severity of acute traumatic brain injuury performed the morphometry on a Sun Sparc Classic computer (Sun Microsystems, Mountain View, CA) using a program written in the Sunview windows environment (Sun Microsystems) with mouse-activated controls for selecting the various steps of each analytical sequence.20 After manually outlining the CC, a semiautomated program determined the anteriormost and posteriormost points by analyzing the x-coordinates of the CC outline. The program then drew a line connecting the anteriormost and posteriormost points (the AP line). The CC was then divided into seven sections21 (figure 1). The first two sections were determined by the "hook" forming the rostrum and genu regions; this dividing line was perpendicular to the AP line. The other regions were determined by perpendicular lines drawn from the AP line. These dividing lines were drawn at 20%, 33%, 50%, 67%, and 80% from the center of the total AP line length. **Discussion.** Neuropathologic studies of CHI in adults and children11,24 and experimental nonimpact head injury models in nonhuman primates2 have reported that focal hemorrhages of the CC are characteristic features of DAI. MRI performed at least 3 months postinjury6,7 has confirmed that CC lesions are present in approximately 20% of children sustaining severe CHI. The posterior body and splenium are the most frequent sites of lesion, a pattern that has been attributed to the distribution of shear strain produced by rotational acceleration and the effects of intracranial dural compartments such as the falx.4,8 Based on recent MRI findings indicating that development of the CC in humans continues through adolescence and the middle 20s,12,13 we postulated that severe CHI in children would disrupt its growth. Repeat MRI 3 years after severe CHI showed a mean reduction in area of the CC. Our finding that CHI severity interacted with occasion reflects divergent changes in CC size in children who sustained severe CHI relative to patients of similar age who had injuries that did not produce coma as defined by the GCS. Consistent with this pattern, we10 found that MRI evidence of CC atrophy in adults was present approximately 4 months after severe, but not mild to moderate, CHI. The relationship of CC area at 3 years postinjury to the lowest postresuscitation GCS score provides indirect support for the role of DAI. High-velocity traumatic forces (e.g., motor vehicle crashes), which predominated in the severe CHI group, could have imparted rotational acceleration to the brain, thus producing shearing of the interhemispheric fibers.

Consistent with developmental MRI studies based on longitudinal and cross-sectional data,12,13 we found that children exhibited growth of the CC over the 3 years after a mild to moderate CHI as defined by their GCS score. Although caution is advised in comparing morphometric MRI data across studies because of variation in measurement methods, CC growth in the mild to moderate CHI group approximates the findings reported for healthy children.12,13 With rapid myelination during the first decade of life,25 it is conceivable that severe CHI occurring in the birth to 5 years age range could produce greater disruption of CC growth than comparable injuries in older children or adolescents. Although we found no evidence for an age effect or interaction of age with CHI severity to support this possibility, our selection of children who were at least 5 years old at the time of the initial MRI could have mitigated age effects. Consistent with this interpretation of our data, neuropathologic studies of CHI in children24 and adults1 have reported that macroscopic CC lesions occur with severe grades of DAI that are typically produced by high-velocity traumatic forces such as motor vehicle crashes.1 Similarly, findings showed2 in a subhuman primate model using nonimpact rotational acceleration that severe DAI associated with coma was necessary to produce CC lesions. Although a spectrum of DAI severity has been documented that included a lucid interval in one third of DAI patients, macroscopic CC lesions were confined to the most severe injuries. In contrast to the relatively brief durations of survival of patients reported in these neuropathologic studies, the 3-year post-injury interval before performing the second MRI provided a longer period for degenerative changes to progress. Consequently, we postulate that prolonged degenerative changes contributed to the lack of growth and even shrinkage of CC in the most severely injured children.

Because the CC interconnects all major subdivisions of the cerebral cortex including fibers from associative regions,26 its growth could facilitate maturation of integrative skills. Although we found that the CC area at 3 years postinjury was related to functional outcome of CHI, we cannot impute causation from the morphometric-outcome correlations.

Technical limitations of this study include partial volume effects13 in measuring the CC area. Because this source of error was common to both the severe and mild to moderate CHI groups, it is unlikely to explain our findings. Despite semiautomated measurement of the CC segments, interobserver variation in drawing the line connecting the anterior and posterior commissures could have occurred. However, we found satisfactory agreement between the measures of CC area obtained from two observers.

From a clinical perspective, we suggest that CC morphometry could provide a reliable index of cerebral white matter injury in children. It is also plausible that morphometric analysis of the CC would be sensitive to pharmacologic interventions designed to preserve dysfunctional axons and mitigate secondary brain injury.

*Evaluation of behavioral status before head trauma.* For many of our patients, the association between the head–neck trauma and sleepiness was not strongly challenged, but this was not the case for patients diagnosed with sleep-disordered breathing. Although sleepdisordered breathing could be a consequence of head–neck trauma, in some patients it could have been a preexisting condition—one that may have even contributed to the accident. For this reason, we had to evaluate systematically (as much as possible) the health status of the patient before the accident. The following information was obtained:

- Sleep/wake history from bed partners
- Reports from coworkers on sleep/wake behavior before the accident
- Evaluation from employers, including absenteeism and accidents
- Financial information demonstrating the level of gainful employment for 2 years before the accident
- Health records from health agencies (for specific professions) and from private physicians, with patient authorization
- Driving records (with the patient's or lawyer's authorization)

Fifty-five of the 59 sleep-disordered breathing patients had a regular bed partner before and at the time of the head trauma. All had regular employment before the accident. Several patients had jobs that required regular health checkups and performance evaluations.

We were unable to demonstrate the presence of overt symptoms of sleep-disordered breathing in 52 of 59 patients. Six patients were identified as snorers before the accident (one patient unknown). In none of the patients were we able to demonstrate overt and regular sleepiness. All patients were fully employed and had no history of driving or professional accidents before the head trauma. There was no information indicating difficulty on the job, including personnel records at health agencies.

*Evaluation of depression and sleepiness.* The Hamilton Depression Scale score was taken as an index of depression. Daytime sleepiness was assessed by MSLT. The patient population studied consisted of 28 patients with an MSLT of 5 minutes, 58 patients with an MSLT of . 5 minutes and # 10 minutes, and 15 patients with an MSLT of . 10 minutes. The corresponding mean Hamilton Depression Scale scores were, respectively, 19 6 6, 17 6 7, and 12 6 6. Analysis of variance did not indicate a significant difference between the two "sleepy" groups, but there was a difference in depression scores ( *p* , 0.05) between the group with the worst sleepiness (MSLT of 5 minutes) and the group with an MSLT of . 10 minutes.

*Presence of sleep-onset REM periods (SOREMP) at MSLT.* SOREMPs at MSLT are an abnormal result that has been associated with narcolepsy. Five patients had two or more SOREMPs during the five MSLT naps.

Two of the 51 patients with MSLT scores of 5 minutes had two SOREMPs during the MSLT; four of these 51 patients had one SOREMP. Three of the 101 patients with MSLT scores between 5 and 10 minutes had SOREMPs; four of the 101 patients had one SOREMP. No SOREMP was noted in patients with MSLT scores of 10 minutes.

None of the patients with SOREMPs reported cataplexy. The two patients with an MSLT of 5 minutes and two SOREMPs were in the group with the shortest total sleep time (360 minutes) and complained of pain. One of the patients with an MSLT score between 5 and 10 minutes and two SOREMPs was an overweight patient with obstructive sleep apnea. Of the five patients with two SOREMPs, four had DR15 DQ6 (DR2 DQ1) HLA typing, and two of the four were positive for the haplotypes.9

*Patients with an MSLT of 12 minutes.* Based on MSLT results, eight patients were not "objectively sleepy."10 When first seen at the clinic, however, a mean of 13 6 3 months after the head trauma, they were unable to perform activities of daily living due to sleepiness and were indifferent to daily requirements and to the task at hand. These patients could perform simple tasks if challenged, but if unchallenged their activity would cease and they would resume behavior that was indicative of sleepiness or sleep. These patients were otherwise well oriented and had appropriate recent memory. They were not confused, and had no speech defects. Although none of these patients had head fracture, brain hemorrhage, or immediate neurosurgical intervention, all had been in a coma (no information on stage) for a mean duration of 27 6 10 hours. No focal lesions were noted on clinical evaluation, EEG, or MRI, and no seizures were noted during the follow-up period. Their mean ESS score was 19 6 3, and their mean Hamilton Depression Scale score was 14 6 4. Five of these nine patients had been given amphetamines that were reported to be helpful.

*Polygraphic recordings of patients with an MSLT of 12 minutes.* Patients free of medication for a minimum of 3 weeks were monitored for 48 hours. During the first 24 hours, patients had 510 minutes of nocturnal monitoring. After breakfast, five nap MSLTs were scheduled. At the end of the first 24 hours, patients were left to choose their own bedtime, wake-up time, and nap times for the following 24 hours.

On day 1, the mean total nocturnal sleep time was 471 6 25 minutes, and the mean percentages of total sleep time were the following: stage 1 non-REM (NREM) sleep, 21%; stage 2 NREM sleep, 49%; stages 3 to 4 NREM sleep, 12.2%; and REM sleep, 17.5%. The mean MSLT scores for the five naps ranged from 13 6 3 minutes to 17 6 2.1 minutes.

On day 2, the total bedtime was 975 6 48 minutes. The mean total nocturnal sleep time was 459 6 38 minutes, with presence of all sleep stages and states. Patients spent an additional 139 6 21 minutes of inactive time during the day, sitting on chairs or couches with their eyes closed. During these inactive periods, the EEG in the central leads (C3/A2, C4/A1) was very monomorphic, with continuous recording of EEG patterns in the 7 to 9-Hz range (i.e., the EEG patterns indicated drowsiness without the development of the classic sleep stages). Mean total daytime sleep time (stages 1 and 2) was 31 6 14 minutes.

*Factors related to subjective or MSLT-defined sleepiness.* Several statistical models were investigated. The clinical variables selected for multiple logistic regression (stepwise) were age, coma of 24 hours, head fracture (there was collinearity associated with neurosurgical intervention), neurologic sequelae, Hamilton Depression Scale score of 18, sleep-disordered breathing, whiplash, nocturnal pain, nocturnal headache, antiepileptic medication intake, BMI, and total time in bed per 24-hour period. These clinical variables were considered for several dependent variables indicative of daytime sleepiness.

When the dependent variable was an ESS score of 16, the following variables were found to be significant: coma of 24 hours ( *p* , 0.001), head fracture (collinearity with neurosurgical intervention and neurologic sequelae, *p* , 0.01), total time in bed . 8.5 hours ( *p* , 0.05), and age of 30 years ( *p* , 0.05). The R2 of the model was 0.43.

When the dependent variable was an MSLT score of 5 minutes, the significant variables were coma of 24 hours, head fracture, and total time in bed. Age was not significant in this model. The R2 of the model was 0.40.

An MSLT of 5 minutes and an ESS score of 16 were not independent variables.

To understand daytime sleepiness better, we also considered a nocturnal sleep time of 360 minutes as a dependent variable. The following variables were significant: report of daily pain ( *p* , 0.01), neurologic sequelae (this was collinear with head fracture, which was not in the model; *p* , 0.05), and a Hamilton Depression Scale score of 18 ( *p* , 0.05). The R2 of the model was 0.20.

When sleep-disordered breathing was the dependent variable, the following 12 clinical variables were tested: age, coma of 24 hours, whiplash, head fracture, Hamilton Depression Scale score, nocturnal headache, nocturnal pain, loud regular snoring, antiepileptic drug intake, BMI, MSLT score, and total time in bed per 24-hour period. Only two variables were significant: whiplash and an MSLT of 8 minutes. Interestingly, BMI was not a significant variable. The R2 of the model was 0.21.

**Discussion.** Our population is very heterogeneous, which reflects the variety of problems associated with post-traumatic hypersomnia and the need for the wide array of tests involved in our evaluation. Our experience, however, has led us to add the Maintenance of Wakefulness Test (MWT) to our evaluation because it provides additional information on patients' alertness.11 We have also added the Beck Depression Inventory because our findings suggested that patients with severe daytime impairment are susceptible to reactive depression. We also did not find the ESS to be very useful; we have recently switched to the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale.12 Finally, we regret not keeping more DNA samples from our patients. An increasing number of genes in the control of sleep/wakefulness and chronobiology is being identified, and future investigation may reveal whether certain individuals are more susceptible to developing daytime sleepiness after head trauma.

*How do we deal with the medicolegal dilemma?* First we assess patients for objective evidence of "hypersomnia" using the MSLT. There are controversies on the usage of the MSLT as the most objective test for evaluation of daytime somnolence. Some prefer to evaluate the frequency and duration of sleep episode either in a control environment, or in the midst of daily activity during continuous 24-hour polygraphic recordings. This type of testing was also used when we faced a very large discrepancy between MSLT results and subjective sleepiness scales (as seen with our patients with compulsive presleep behavior).

There are also controversies regarding the definition of the limit of normalcy with the MSLT. Although an MSLT between 10 and 12 minutes is not considered to be pathologic according to the guidelines, an MSLT of 11 minutes is not uncommon in association with a report of "daytime tiredness" in the presence of an abnormal respiratory disturbance index.

The MSLT was developed at Stanford for the recognition of narcolepsy, but its use has been extended to the recognition of excessive daytime sleepiness. Until the MSLT and MWT are studied on a very large group of "normal" patients in different age groups, we will not know the specificity and sensitivity of these tests. We feel that there are probably overlaps between normal and pathologic scores, depending on the study population and age group.

In our population, based on one MSLT, 24 patients scored between 10 and 12 minutes. These patients probably had daytime sleepiness, but this question was not clearly resolved by our study.

Second, we looked for an abnormal distribution of REM sleep during the daytime. This is an objective demonstration of sleep-related pathology. The presence of two or more SOREMPs during the MSLT suggest possible post-traumatic narcolepsy (based on the definition of narcolepsy in the International Classification of Sleep Disorders13). In our population, two or more SOREMPs were uncommon. New data suggest that SOREMPs may be related to significant nocturnal sleep reduction and fragmentation.14,15 This seems to be the case in at least three of our five patients with two or more SOREMPs. However, SOREMPs may be related to a genetic predisposition (with involvement of DQ beta 0702), without the presence of the classic narcoleptic tetrad.16 Additional DNA studies on patients with head trauma and hypersomnolence are needed.

When the presence of "hypersomnia" based on the most commonly used MSLT was established, we then looked at whether the head trauma was responsible for the reported daytime sleepiness, and whether there were preexisting features that might have predisposed or amplified the current complaint. This was difficult because we had polygraphic documentation of sleep-related breathing disorders in 59 subjects. We then examined previous documentation of the patient's alertness, daytime performance, and employment record before the accident. We concluded that there was no indication of behavioral consequences of sleep-disordered breathing before the head–neck trauma in 52 of the 59 patients. Six of the seven remaining subjects had a history of nocturnal snoring, but did not demonstrate any breathing disorder.

Our search of a preexisting risk factor was based on clinical evaluation, using published information on the relationship between maxillomandibular development, orofacial dysmorphism, and sleepdisordered breathing. We believe that in the presence of sleep-disordered breathing after head trauma, one should look for the aforementioned anatomic risk factors.

Interestingly, all patients with a history of whiplash had sleep-disordered breathing. This is important because one may not associate neck trauma with the development of sleep-disordered breathing. This relationship between whiplash and sleepdisordered breathing was noted 15 years ago.2 One patient was followed for several years after his head trauma, and he experienced a progressive regression of his sleep-disordered breathing over a 2-year period. We have not observed this in our current study. In view that with the presence of anatomic risk factors,17,18 cervical fusion and surgical intervention on cervical discopathies leads commonly to sleepdisordered breathing; the development of sleepdisordered breathing secondary to whiplash may not be surprising.

Our approach has been conservative when dealing with medicolegal patients. When patients with anatomic risk factors were found to have sleepdisordered breathing and clinical symptoms that date from the time of the trauma, we concluded that a "health problem occurred on a preexisting condition."

We considered also the relationship between the severity of the subjective sleepiness, the scores of the sleepiness scale, and the score of the MSLT. Patients who had head fracture and coma \$ 24 hours had severe daytime sleepiness. These patients had other neurologic sequelae, including seizure disorder and antiepileptic medication intake. Although these medications might have worsened their daytime sleepiness, it is less likely because other patients on similar antiepileptic drugs had normal results on the MSLT.

This finding raised an obvious question. Why did we not see a greater number of patients with severe head trauma and daytime sleepiness? One possibility is that daytime sleepiness is an uncommon consequence of head trauma. Another possible explanation is that there are so many neurologic sequelae to head trauma that sleepiness may not be judged to be a significant problem. It might have been treated symptomatically by the neurologist, or questions on daytime sleepiness, tiredness, or fatigue might not have been asked. Our investigations suggest the need to question systematically this impairment, which needs treatment and compensation if legally justified.

The logistic regression analysis further identified factors that seem obvious but are not necessarily taken into consideration when daytime sleepiness is involved. Pain and neurologic sequelae disrupted sleep and led to a decrease in total nocturnal sleep time for many of our patients. The treating neurologist must always consider the impact of a patient's handicap on sleep. The patients with other neurologic sequelae (mono-, bi-, tetraparesis) and history of lasting coma with or without head fracture were in the group of patients with an MSLT of # 7 minutes and most commonly # 5 minutes. The sleepiness of these patients may be related largely to their report of nocturnal distress and disrupted sleep. Any nocturnal disrupted sleep has an impact on the following daytime's wakefulness, and impairment of both will have an impact on the patient's rehabilitation. A full sleep/wake history must be obtained on the patient with head trauma and neurologic sequelae. Daytime sleepiness must be explored and not assumed to be related to palsy.

An important element of our investigation was also to note a disabling problem of "pseudohypersomnia," which we believe is related to the head trauma. We had eight patients who were apathetic and subjectively sleepy but had normal MSLT scores. Apathy and daytime sleepiness have been associated with bilateral paramedian thalamic infarcts. Most of these lesions also involved the adjacent hypothalamus. Ischemic infarcts in the paramedian region of the thalamus have been shown to be associated with subthalamic and mesencephalic lesions19 and normal wakefulness, but otherwise apathetic and unmotivated behavior after the first month. The authors stressed that the problem was more of an "attention deficit" than a lack of alertness.

Another study20 dissociated "sleepiness" from "compulsive presleep behavior" when monitoring a 32-year-old woman with bilateral obstruction of the posterior cerebral arteries at their posterior origin, leading to hypodense lesions in the paramedian parts of the thalamus. After an initial coma, the patient was severely apathetic. A 24-hour ambulatory EEG showed "a normal 8-hour sleeping pattern" with normal waking EEG, including when she demonstrated "presleep behavior." We have also had three patients with bilateral paramedian thalamic lesions21 who developed a similar pattern of apathetic behavior to the one reported here.

Significant deviations from the normal behavior associated with sleep were described in the athalamic cat22—whether it be presomnic, somnic, or postsomnic behavior—with the persistence of normal sleep. It seems that lesions that involve the paramedian nuclei of the thalamus are associated with a strong apathetic syndrome. In our eight posttraumatic patients, no lesion was seen on MRI, but this does not eliminate the possibility of neurophysiological impairment secondary to the significant head trauma. However we have no clear substrate to explain the syndrome in these patients. Nonetheless, these patients had a significant neurologic handicapdespite the absence of polygraphic findings supporting the diagnosis of hypersomnia. It is important to recognize this "pseudohypersomnia" and not to label patients as "malingerers."
