**BACKGROUND:** There is still some reluctance to refer pediatric patients for epilepsy surgery, despite evidence of success in retrospective series.

**OBJECTIVE:** To describe surgical experience and long-term outcome in pediatric temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) at a single institution.

**METHODS:** Retrospective review of pediatric (<18-years-old) TLE patients who underwent surgery between November 1996 and December 2006 at Cleveland Clinic Epilepsy Center. Cox proportional hazard modeling was used to assess outcome predictors.

**RESULTS:** One hundred thirty pediatric patients met study criteria. Mean time between seizure onset and surgery was 6.3 years. Invasive evaluation was used in 32 patients (24.5%). Hippocampal sclerosis was present in 70 patients (53.8%), either alone or associated in dual pathology. The complication rate was 7%. The seizure-freedom rates at 1, 2, 5, and 12 years were 76%, 72%, 54%, and 41%, respectively (Kaplan-Meier). With the use of the Engel outcome classification, 98 (75.3%) patients were class I, 11 (8.5%) class II, 9 (7%) class III, and 12 (9.2%) were class IV at last follow-up. Only 4 (3.1%) patients underwent reoperations. Antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) were discontinued in 36 patients (28.3%) in a mean period of 18 months (SD  $\pm$ 17 months; range, 6-102 months). Although left-sided resection, lower number of preoperative AED trials ( $\leq$ 4), and tumor pathology correlated with favorable seizure outcomes, extensive surgical resection remained the only significant outcome predictor after multivariate analysis (P = .007; HR = 0.13 [95% confidence interval 0.007-0.64]).

**CONCLUSION:** Careful selection of surgical candidates by multidisciplinary evaluations is required. Long-term seizure control is achieved successfully with acceptable low complication rates.

# METHODS

Between November 1996 and December 2006, 1610 patients with medically intractable epilepsy underwent surgery at The Cleveland Clinic Foundation by the senior author (W.B.); 706 were in the pediatric population (younger than 18 years). Among these patients, 156 had temporal lobe surgery (22.1%). With the approval by our institutional review board, we performed a retrospective analysis in this group, including demographic, clinical, electrophysiological, neuroimaging, surgical, and postoperative data. Patients with previous temporal lobe surgery (12 patients), a pathology report of malignant lesions (1 patient), combined temporal and extratemporal resections (8 patients), and patients lost to follow-up immediately after discharge (5 patients) were excluded from this study.

# Preoperative Evaluations

All patients were evaluated by a pediatric epileptologist, and preoperative evaluations were completed in a standardized fashion. Each case was discussed at a weekly Epilepsy Management Conference by neurologists, neurosurgeons, neuroradiologists, neuropsychologists, and a bioethicist. Preoperative evaluations included detailed clinical history, seizure semiology,<sup>14</sup> frequency of seizures,<sup>15</sup> and response to AEDs. Data presented included results of prolonged interictal and ictal videoelectroencephalography (EEG) monitoring, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with a dedicated epilepsy protocol (T1 coronal volumetric acquisition, T2, and fluid attenuation inversion recovery sequences using 1.5 T/64-MHz MR system), 18-F deoxyglucose positron emission tomography (PET), single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), and intracarotid amobarbital test.16 Recommendations of the committee were then discussed with the patients and families, and informed consent was obtained.

# Resective Surgical Procedures

The surgical approach was selected according to the preoperative diagnosis, electroencephalographic data, and anatomic characteristics as identified on MRI. The surgical techniques were as follows.

- 1) Corticoamygdalohippocampectomy. This procedure includes removal of a portion of lateral temporal cortex as well as the mesial temporal lobe structures (amygdala, hippocampus, parahippocampus). It was performed in patients with mesial temporal lobe pathology and those with pathology in both the lateral and mesial temporal lobe compartments. The extent of the lateral temporal resection was defined by the senior author (W.B.), with average of 4.5 cm on the dominant side, and 5.5 cm on the nondominant side, with some variation based on cortical vascular anatomy and individualized in lesional cases to include the posterior extent of the lesion. The standard hippocampal en bloc resection included resection of the hippocampus posteriorly to the level of the trigone.
- 2) Lesionectomy plus corticoamygdalohippocampectomy. This extended beyond the corticoamygdalohippocampectomy described above to also include a lesion (benign tumor, focal cortical dysplasia, etc) usually identified on preoperative MRI.
- 3) Lesionectomy only. This procedure included resection of the lesion identified on preoperative imaging (neoplasm or focal cortical dysplasia). Typically, the extent of resection was based on intraoperative findings with or without the use of intraoperative electrocorticography. The mesial temporal lobe structures were not

- included in the resection for this group of patients. The choice to perform a lesionectomy vs an extensive corticoamygdalohippocampectomy was individualized by the patient during the patient management conference, and usually relied heavily on concerns for mesial temporal structures epileptogenicity and risk for memory decline should they be resected with the neocortical lesion.
- 4) Tailored temporal neocortical resection and corticoamygdalohippocampectomy. This technique involves mapping of the ictal onset zone and functional temporal lobe cortex before resection. This mapping was usually performed with the use of invasive cortical electrodes (subdural and/or depth electrodes).
- 5) Lateral neocortical resection alone. This included resection of nonlesional temporal lateral neocortex identified as the ictal onset zone by invasive intracranial recordings. Extent of resection was defined by interpretation of ictal and interictal findings during the chronic extraoperative recordings and the location of eloquent temporal cortex.
- 6) Selective amygdalohippocampectomy. This included resection of mesial temporal lobe structures via a transcortical approach through the middle temporal gyrus. It was rarely used in cases where there existed a concern for decline in neuropsychological function following dominant temporal lobe surgery.<sup>17</sup>

Invasive recording techniques included stereotactic depth electrode implantation with the use of frameless stereotaxis and scalp-mounted fiducials, craniotomy with implantation of subdural electrode grids (variable configurations), and craniotomy with subdural grid implantation and stereotactic depth electrode insertion by the use of direct visualization of the entry point. Foramen ovale electrode placement was not performed on any of our patients because sphenoidal electrodes were easier and safer to implement.18,19

## Postoperative Evaluation

During the immediate postoperative period, all patients received postoperative care, including intensive care unit observation for 1 day, continuing anticonvulsant therapy, and brain MRI on postoperative day 1. Follow-up included visits at 6 weeks and 6 months. The 6-month visit included a high-resolution MRI, neuropsychology, and routine EEG testing. Subsequently, follow-up was obtained at yearly intervals (or more frequently, if indicated) via outpatient visits with the epileptologist or systematic phone follow-up.

## Statistical Analysis

Before modeling, the data were summarized with descriptive statistics for each variable including means, medians, and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies for categorical variables. For exploratory purposes, an initial analysis of the data was first performed univariably by use of the Wilcoxon rank sum, x2, and Fisher exact tests to compare seizure-free patients with those with seizure recurrence, regardless of follow-up time. This allowed identification of potential prognostic indicators. Variables with a significance level of 10% on initial univariate analysis were then tested in a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model. Results were then considered statistically significant at the 5% level. This method allows testing of the correlation of specific variables with outcome while taking into account any interactions and associations among those variables, and their variation with time.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was first used to calculate the probability of seizure freedom in the overall group before any outcome predictor analysis, and later by considering each of the significant risk factors. Statistical significance was tested using the log-rank test and comparison of 95% confidence intervals.

# RESULTS

## Demographic and Preoperative Evaluation

One hundred thirty patients fulfilled all study criteria and were analyzed; 63 were male (48%). Mean age at surgery was 12.3 years (range, 1-18 years). Handedness was right in 106 patients (81.6%), left in 17 (13%), ambidextrous in 1 (0.8%), and not defined in 6 (4.6%).

Identified predisposing factors for epilepsy, including preoperative MRI findings, are shown in Table 1. The mean time between onset of seizures and surgery was 6.3 years (range, 1-17 years), having between 1 and 10 AED trials before surgical treatment (mean, 4). At least 2 antiepileptic drug trials failed in all patients with chronic epilepsy, with the exception of 17 (13%) patients who underwent surgery after 1 trial because of preoperative imaging features concerning for neoplasm.

Auras were present in 49 patients (37.4%), and the main types of seizures identified were automotor and dialeptic, with 67 (51.1%) and 39 (30%) patients, respectively. History of status epilepticus requiring emergency department admissions was present in 19 patients (14.4%). Seizure frequency according to our institutional classification<sup>15</sup> was daily seizures in 36 patients (27.7%), persistent in 89 (68.4%), and rare in 5 (3.8%). Preoperative psychiatric and behavioral disorders were present in 47 patients (36%).

#### DISCUSSION

Patients with epilepsy arising from the temporal lobe are among the best candidates for surgical treatment.<sup>3</sup> This option should be considered when seizures persist despite optimal medical treatment. In the adult population, this has been defined as the failure to respond to at least 2 AEDs whether as monotherapies or in combination, with adequate therapeutic levels.<sup>20</sup> However, this strategy may not apply to the pediatric population because of the risk of cognitive impairment if seizures persist.<sup>9,21</sup> Multiple series have reported the success rate of temporal lobe surgery in the pediatric population,<sup>6,11,22</sup> but, surprisingly, its utilization has not been widespread.<sup>11-13</sup> In our study, the mean time between onset of seizures and surgery was 6.3 years (range, 1-17 years), having between 1 and 10 anticonvulsant trials before surgical treatment (mean, 4).

Optimal selection of surgical candidates requires a thorough evaluation of the clinical, electrophysiological, neuroimaging, and neuropsychology data by a multidisciplinary, team including pediatric epileptologists, neuroradiologists, neurosurgeons, neurophysiologists, and neuropsychologists.

The classic clinical presentation of TLE was found with automotor and dialeptic seizures in 51% and 30% of patients, respectively. Different symptomatogenic zones<sup>14,15</sup> were seen, manifesting as complex motor, hypomotor, and gelastic seizures.

Language localization studies (Wada) and other functional studies were rarely used because of the pediatric nature of the study population. ## Surgical Procedures and Outcome

Our results indicate that at 1, 2, 5, and 12 years, seizure freedom was reached in 76%, 72%, 54%, and 41%, respectively. It is apparent that, as time passes, the seizure freedom rate declines. Although the reasons for this remain unknown, this decline may reflect secondary epileptogenesis or incomplete resection of the epileptogenic zone. More work is necessary to identify predictors for seizure recurrence after pediatric temporal lobe surgery. Nonetheless, our results with surgical resection agree with other published series (Table 8).5,6,22,32-35 Overall, a favorable outcome was achieved in 84% of patients (Engel class I with 75.5%, and class II with 8.5%), correlating with previous surgical series ranging from 60% to 86%.5,6,8,33,36-38 Results of temporal lobe surgery in children are less favorable than in the adult population previously reported at our institution (53% seizure freedom at 10 years),<sup>39</sup> which may be related to fewer opportunities to identify the ictal onset zone because of age-related limitations in performing additional testing (ictal SPECT, invasive evaluations, Wada test). It may also reflect different unknown intrinsic pathophysiologic mechanisms accounting for epilepsy recurrence in the 2 populations.

Theoretically, the most important factor for seizure control among the different surgical techniques is the complete resection of the epileptogenic zone.<sup>40</sup> While preoperative testing and invasive recordings can lead to a hypothesis of the epileptogenic zone, only the seizure outcome after surgery over time will determine whether the area resected was truly the region that generated the epileptic seizures.<sup>41</sup> In our study, extensive resection for lesional epilepsy (lesionectomy plus corticoamygdalohippocampectomy) led to better seizure freedom, whereas, in our nonlesional epilepsy cases, the results were similar to previous descriptions.35,39,42,43 Given that the pathology in pediatric temporal lobe epilepsies is often widespread, the role for minimally invasive approaches such as selective stereotactic amygdalohippocampectomy should rarely be used.

Overall, recurrence of seizures was present in 41% of patients, reobtaining satisfactory seizure control in 72% of patients with 50% Engel class I, and 22% Engel class II at lastfollow-up.Only 4 patients (3.1%) underwent reoperation, with improvement in 2 (Table 4). On multivariate analysis, only extensive surgical resection (corticoamygdalohippocampectomy plus lesionectomy) demonstrated significance. There was some tendency (although no statistical significance) for higher seizure recurrence in nondominant temporal lobe cases. This can be the result of a less strict selection criteria for surgery because of less concern for language deterioration following surgery. Many of these patients had no other options, and surgery perhaps was considered out of compassionate use. Other risk factors such as diffuse pathology, bilateral abnormalities, preoperative secondarily generalized seizures, and febrile seizures did not show statistical significance in our study.2,39

Anticonvulsant therapy was discontinued in 36 patients (28.3%), at a mean time of 18 months after surgery. Discontinuation of AED therapy after surgery is controversial, but it is usually recommended to wait for at least 1 year of seizure freedom and absence of epileptic activity on EEG.44,45

Our overall complication rate was 7%, including wound infections, visual-field deficit, cerebral edema, subdural/epidural hematoma, CSF leak, and speech deficit. There was no mortality or long-term disabling morbidity. ## Pathology

Our most frequent finding was hippocampal sclerosis, found in 53% of cases. In 23% it was the only pathological finding and in 30% it occurred in combination with cortical dysplasia (dual pathology). Dual pathology is defined as mesial temporal sclerosis together with an additional epileptogenic lesion identified by neuroimaging, or additional microscopic changes revealed only by pathological examination.<sup>48</sup>

In contrast to the adult population, hippocampal sclerosis is less frequent, while dual pathology is more frequent.4,49 Both conditions have similar rates of seizure control after surgery, and surgical resection, including the 2 areas of dual pathology, did not affect seizure prognosis in our univariate and multivariate analysis. This is likely because the most common surgical procedure for TLE typically includes both the regions where the dual pathology is localized.

# CONCLUSION

The present series in pediatric TLE is one of the largest reported to date, demonstrating efficacy and a low rate of complications. We encourage an early identification of surgical candidacy and treatment in this population to reduce the long-term risk of neurocognitive decline associated with ongoing medically refractory epilepsy. Clearly, more data are necessary over an extended long-term follow-up in this group to better define neurocognitive and quality-of-life outcomes. The surgical technique applied should be individualized by consideration of the preoperative data and suspected pathology. With an experienced surgical team, surgery can be safely performed in young patients. Referral to an experienced surgical center should be considered in all patients for whom 2 trials of anticonvulsant therapy have failed.

## COMMENT

This is an interesting report on the impressively large experience with 130 children and adolescents operated on for temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) by the senior author (W.B.). It is noteworthy that the vast experience of a large epilepsy surgery center is necessary to provide such an overview, because pediatric cases are less frequent than adult cases, and the temporal lobe origin of seizures in pediatric patients is seen in about a fifth of cases, only. Thus, defining and analyzing this probably largest cohort of pediatric patients operated on for TLE contributes a solid and the best basis, at present, for information in this field. There will probably not be any comparable prospective studies in the near future.

Results with respect to seizure control were especially successful for the first years, then a number of seizure relapses was documented (76% seizure freeness initially, down to 41% after 12 years). However, patient numbers decreased with duration of follow-up and a majority of relapses (57%) was triggered. Nevertheless, outcome with Engel classification at last follow-up resulted in 75% of patients assigned to class 1 (seizure-free), and overall 84% had a favorable outcome (class 1 and 2). The clinical relevance of these recurrences is thus debatable and should not be used to prevent surgical treatment in children with TLE. The complication rate was 7%, most of these just minor sequels, without serious permanent morbidity.

It is interesting to read about the outcome predictors: upon multivariate analysis, only more extensive resection reached a level of significance. This is in line with our previously published data and supports the notion that pediatric TLE with seizure onset early during life is probably associated with more widespread pathology than typical adult TLE. The high rate of dual pathology is another fact arguing for more extensive resection in children compared with adults.

The results with 20 MR-negative cases should be regarded with caution, because nearly all patients had clear neuropathological findings, suggesting that, eg, sensitivity in the 1990s was not as high as it is today, especially with high-field (3 Tesla) MR imaging.

This article provides an important and clearly positive risk-benefit ratio in favor of surgery for pediatric patients with TLE. Thus, these data will contribute to a more widespread acceptance of epilepsy surgery for pediatric TLE with resulting good individual chances of seizure control, and better psychosocial development for many patients. It is noteworthy, in this respect, that persisting neuropsychological deficits are a lesser concern compared with adult patients treated for TLE. <sup>1</sup>
