## CLINICAL ARTICLE

# Randomized controlled trial of 2.5-cm versus 3.5-cm mesial temporal resection in temporal lobe epilepsy—part 1: intent-to-treat analysis

Johannes Schramm & T. N. Lehmann & J. Zentner & C. A. Mueller & J. Scorzin & R. Fimmers & H. J. Meencke & A. Schulze-Bonhage & C. E. Elger

Received: 8 September 2010 /Accepted: 25 November 2010 / Published online: 18 December 2010 # Springer-Verlag 2010

## Abstract

Background Only one prospective randomized study on the extent of mesial resection in surgery for temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) exists. This randomized controlled trial (RCT) examines whether 3.5-cm mesial resection is leading to a better seizure outcome than a 2.5-cm resection.

Methods Three epilepsy surgery centers using similar MRI protocols, neuropsychological tests, and resection types for TLE surgery included 207 patients in a RCT with pre- and postoperative volumetrics. One hundred and four patients were randomized into a 2.5-cm resection group and 103 patients into a 3.5-cm resection group, i.e., an intended minimum resection length of 25 versus 35 mm for the hippocampus and parahippocampus. Primary outcome measure was seizure freedom Engel class I throughout the first year. The study was powered to detect a 20% difference in class I outcome. Seizure outcome was available for 207 patients, complete volumetric results for 179 patients. Outcome analysis was restricted to control of successful randomization and an intentto-treat analysis of seizure outcome.

Results The mean true resection volumes were significantly different for the 2.5-cm and 3.5-cm resection groups; thus, the randomization was successful. Median resection volume in the 2.5-cm group was 72.86% of initial volume and 83.44% in the 3.5-cm group. At 1 year, seizure outcome Engel class I was 74% in the 2.5-cm and 72.8% in the 3.5-cm resection group. Conclusions The primary intent-to-treat analysis did not show a different seizure freedom rate for the more posteriorly reaching 3.5-cm resection group. It appears possible that not maximal volume resection but adequate volume resection leads to good seizure freedom.

Keywords Temporal lobe epilepsy . Epilepsy surgery. Randomized controlled trial . Amygdalohippocampectomy

#### Introduction

Among the epilepsy syndromes amenable to surgical treatment, mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE) is the

one most frequently operated on. Seizure freedom rates between 60% and 70% have been reported in recent years. Recently, it could be demonstrated [40] in a prospective randomized trial that temporal lobe epilepsy surgery is superior to prolonged medical therapy.

The surgery for MTLE has for a long time been dominated by the question which structures have to be removed to achieve the optimal rate of seizure freedom. A number of studies have been devoted not only to the question which structures [10, 12, 19–21, 31] but also to what extent the mesial temporal lobe structures have to be resected to achieve the highest rates of seizure freedom. Since Niemeyer [29] proved that the isolated removal of mesial structures, i.e., the combined hippocampus–parahippocampus (selective amygdalohippocampectomy, SAH) is also able to achieve seizure freedom, attention has focused on these mesial structures. One focus of discussion is how far backward from the tip should these mesial structures be resected.

A number of retrospective studies trying to define positive predictors of good seizure outcome after temporal lobe resection (TLR) and SAH pointed out that the extent of mesial resection didn't seem to play a role [16, 18, 23, 32, 36, 38]; fewer studies came to the conclusion that a larger extent is correlated with a better outcome [6, 7, 28, 35, 42]. There are only four randomized studies in the literature which look at extent of resection, one comparing SAH with TLR [13], one comparing two approaches for SAH [22], one comparing different extents of neocortical removal in TLR [15], and only Wyler's study [42] randomizing for mesial resection extent. The latter demonstrated that in temporal lobe resection, the rate of seizure freedom is considerably better if a hippocampal resection reaching far backward is performed [42]. There was one major problem with this study, namely the lack of postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) control of extent of resection. This is important since several authors have pointed out [3, 17, 28, 30] that it is an absolute requirement to precisely assess the extent of resection by volumetric MRI. Thus, the results of purely retrospective studies or prospective studies without postoperative MRI verification of true extent of resection cannot be considered as first class evidence, as pointed out in a recent review [33].

The disadvantages of non-randomized studies as summarized by Wiebe et al. [39–41] are well-known, and therefore, the conclusion is justified that there is no reliable study available (i.e., randomized design, prospectively collected data, MRI volumetrically controlled resection extent) comparing different extents of mesial resection to support the claim that a larger mesial extent of resection, i.e., a more posteriorly reaching resection of the hippocampus– parahippocampal formation is leading to a better seizure outcome. It would be of great interest if this could be proven, to systematically perform far posterior reaching resections, as the proven benefit of surgery [40] could be enhanced.

In summary, the following reasons seem to justify this prospective randomized study: only one randomized study tested this relationship and only a minority of studies was done with postop MRI volumetric control of true resection extent.

Vice versa, one would like to know whether a lesser extent of resection is associated with a lower rate of seizure freedom. To elucidate this question, we organized a prospective randomized controlled trial with volumetric assessment of resection extent, comparing two groups with planned different extents of mesial resection for mesial temporal lobe epilepsy: one with an intended minimum mesial resection of 25 mm from the tip of the temporal horn backwards and the other one with a mesial minimum resection of 35 mm from the tip of the temporal horn backwards.

## Methods

Participants Patients in the three epilepsy surgery centers at the Universities of Bonn, Freiburg, and Berlin were recruited for this study between October 2002 and December 2005. The central data base was kept in one center, the other two centers had a local identical data base collecting all relevant patient data in a prospective fashion and regularly sending them to the central data base. In each center, patients with temporal lobe epilepsy were screened and evaluated, and if recruited for the study, were followed up by that center. The MRI studies were organized in each center following a common MRI protocol. MRI volumetry was done only centrally using an epilepsy-dedicated MRI protocol (for details, see Urbach et al.) [37], the key elements being 1-mm slice thickness and isotropic 1-mm³ voxels using manual tracings of six subregions and the ANALYZE software package (Biomedical imaging resource, Worcester, Minn.). A detailed description is given in Müller et al. 2007. Intrarater reliability is described in that study which was calculated with the intraclass correlation coefficient between 0.93 and 0.98 for the various subcompartments [27]. Patients were regularly seen in outpatient clinics, and the required data were collected and fed in the local data base regularly to be sent to the central data base.

Patients were eligible for this study if they suffered from drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy and presurgical evaluation had led to the recommendation of either a partial temporal lobe resection combined with amygdalohippocampectomy or a more restricted selected SAH. Only cases with mesial involvement were included, based on the presurgical evaluation. This included patients with mesial

temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) from hippocampal sclerosis or without sclerosis, mesial TLE cases due to other lesions in the hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus small enough to be randomized for a smaller and longer resection, and lesional or non-lesional TLE cases with a recommendation for partial lobe resection including amygdalo–hippocampo–parahippocampectomy. Aspects of patient selection, definition of TLE, and criteria during presurgical evaluation in the three centers can be found in several publications [4, 5, 8, 9, 14, 25, 26]. Patients needed to be at least 18 years old and able to understand the study plan and the procedure of informed consent. Drug resistance was defined as seizure duration of more than 2 years despite continuous treatment with at least two antiepileptic drugs at maximal tolerated doses. The further requirement was that the patients had to be available for a minimum follow-up in the city of surgery after 1 year and had to be willing to participate in the neuropsychological test after 1 year.

Patients were recruited between mid-October 2002 and end of December 2005. Preoperative volumetric MRIs were usually performed a few days before the surgery, postoperative volumetric MRIs were performed for a minimum of 3 months after the surgery, usually in the fourth month and in a few cases, in the fifth month. All patients were regularly followed up, the first time at 3 months after surgery, then at 6 months after surgery, and at 1 year after surgery. All patients, regardless of intervention group, were kept on the same postoperative antiepileptic drug regime as preoperatively. Neuropsychological testing was done during presurgical work-up and postoperative late neuropsychological testing was done 1 year after surgery, usually not later than 13 months. The neuropsychological analysis will be dealt with separately. Follow-up ended in December 2006. Volumetrics were finalized in 2007.

Presurgical evaluation was harmonized between the centers with all patients having video-EEG recordings, MRI evaluation using the same protocol in all three centers, and neuropsychological testing using the same test batteries in all three centers. Full details of neuropsychological testing have been published recently [14]. Within the study, two different resection types could be used (SAH and partial lobectomy). Partial lobectomies were performed in two variants either as two-third anterior lobectomy (TLR) or as one-third anterior lobectomy (i.e., pole resection) plus mesial resection (TPR +). The extent of lateral removal in the lobectomy types was applied to the same degree in each center for that resection type. The way to measure length of hippocampal resection was precisely defined and agreed upon between the three center surgeons. Therefore, the essential points of evaluation and surgical technique were agreed upon and outlined before the start of the trial. The histopathological diagnosis was done in the local departments of Neuropathology and the diagnosis and grade of hippocampal sclerosis were reviewed and confirmed by the reference center for histopathology of chronic epilepsy at the Department of Neuropathology in Erlangen.

Interventions The epileptologists in each center were free to decide whether they would propose a selective resection or one of the lobectomy types. The recommendation for type of resection (SAH or TLR) was based on presurgical evaluation and was followed strictly, independent of the study, and before patients were informed about the ongoing study, before the patients made their decision and, of course, also before randomization. The patients were then randomized into two groups concerning the intended length of resection of the hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus. The only difference between the two groups was the extent of mesial resection regarding the hippocampal– parahippocampal bloc. The length of resection was to be determined intraoperatively after the opening of the temporal horn by using a narrow millimeter paper strip from the anterior tip of the temporal horn backwards placed on the hippocampal head along its length axis. Thus, mainly two large patient groups would be formed (SAH and TLR), each having been randomized into the 2.5- and 3.5-cm mesial resection subgroups. One group was planned to have a minimal resection of 25 mm determined in this fashion, the other group was to have a resection of a minimum of 35 mm. To simplify reading, the first group will henceforth be called the 2.5-cm group and the second the 3.5-cm group, with the implied understanding that this means the intended extent of resection. It was determined only at the beginning of surgery to which extent of resection a case should be allocated. The resection included major parts of the amygdaloid body in each case and the uncus. For the partial anterior one-third lobectomy, a polar resection of approximately 3 cm was done and for so-called anterior two-thirds temporal lobectomy, a resection of between 4.5 and 5 cm of the lateral neocortical temporal lobe was also included. The postoperative management was identical in both groups. Antiepileptic drugs were continued until the end of the observation period of 1 year after surgery, usually another year after that. The study design was reviewed and agreed on by the ethical boards in all three institutions.

Objectives, outcome measures, and sample size The objective of this study was to assess the significance of the extent of resection of mesial structures (hippocampus and parahippocampus) to achieve seizure freedom after surgery for temporal lobe epilepsy. Therefore, the following formal hypothesis was tested:

Postoperative seizure freedom is independent from the extent of resection.

Primary outcome measure was seizure freedom at 1 year and during the full first year expressed as class I in the Engel outcome scale. To increase the quality of follow-up, patients were seen postoperatively at 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year. We are well aware that later decline in seizurefree rate could theoretically affect the groups differently.

Sample size was determined to be able to detect the advantage of 80% seizure freedom for one resection type against a rate of seizure freedom of 60% for the other resection types with a power of 80% using a two-sided Fisher's exact test at a level of 5%. Thus, smaller effect sizes of 5% or 10% would not be detectable. A computer-generated blocked randomization list was prepared at the Institute of Medical Biometrics, Informatics and Epidemiology. Each center received the randomization codes in numbered and sealed envelopes, each envelope allocating the extent of resection for one particular patient in that center. The envelopes were to be used in the given sequence for the patients as they were included into the study. They had to be opened in the OR not before the morning of surgery. Thus, the type of surgery (selective amygdalohippocampectomy or lobectomy) was determined before the envelope was opened, but the content of the envelope determined the mesial extent of resection. Study participants were assigned to their resection extent group by that randomized sequence independent from the physicians involved with presurgical evaluation and recruitment of patients.

Neither the study participants nor the epileptologists assessing the outcomes were told of the result of the randomization, i.e., they were not aware whether a short or a long resection had been done; the only person knowing this was the surgeon. OR notes did not mention the planned resection extent. In particular, the persons performing the MRI volumetry were blinded to group assignment.

The primary goal was the comparison of rates of continuous seizure freedom between the two groups with intended 2.5-cm and 3.5-cm resection during 1 year after surgery. The outcome applies to the total population; all types of surgery were lumped. However, another study addressing just one operation type could come to different conclusions.

The rates were compared using the Fisher's exact test primarily for the population of patients who had followed the study protocol for at least 12 months and for whom the primary outcome could be measured. That is, follow-up was identical for all centers and all resection types and intended resection intent groups.

A secondary analysis was performed taking into account all randomized patients whereas drop-outs, severe violations of the protocol, and the impossibility to measure the outcome were counted as failure of therapy (intention-totreat analysis). For both analyses, the patients were analyzed as part of the group to which they were randomized. More analyses were performed based on the per-protocol population comparing groups of patients defined by the true extent of resection. This is a smaller group (n=179) defined as those with complete volumetric data. A separate publication is devoted to these secondary analyses based not on intended resection extent but on truly measured resection extent. Postoperative volumetric analyses were used in this study only to test whether the randomization into 2.5- and 3.5-cm groups did in fact lead to different resection volumes for the two groups.

## Results

A total of 318 cases were assessed for eligibility because of temporal lobe epilepsy surgery. Thirty-one of these did not meet inclusion criteria, 23 refused to participate, and 57 were excluded for other reasons (Fig. 1). Of the 31 not meeting inclusion criteria, the reasons were: inability to do proper neuropsychological testing because of retardation or foreign language (n=16), previous temporal lobe surgery (n=4), seizure duration less than 2 years (n=1), age below 18 years (n=4), and mesial resection restricted to uncus and amygdala (n=6). The 57 other exclusion reasons were: no immediate preoperative MRI because of adipose body size, MRI breakdown or missing MRI (n=4), forgotten randomization or envelope opened too late (n= 7), and primarily no randomization because of pathology necessitating a dorsal reaching resection not allowing for randomization (n=46).

A total of 207 patients were allocated to the two intervention types, 103 to the 3.5-cm group and 104 to the 2.5-cm group. A total of 207 patients received the allocated intervention. Two patients died, one randomized to the short group by suicide 13 months after his operation with outcome Engel I. The second death (traffic accident) occurred 9 months after the operation, but we have a 7 months' outcome available for him and carried this forward for the primary analysis. The group available for the primary analysis consisted of these 207 cases. For the primary analysis, only the 1 year outcome and the result of the randomization procedure had to be available.

Baseline data Of 207 analyzed patients, 49 were recruited in center 1, 56 in center 2, and 102 in center 3. A total of 125 SAHs were performed, 53 anterior one-third lobectomies, and 15 standard two-third temporal lobectomies, four mesial lesionectomies plus hippocampo–parahippocampectomy, two tailored resections. ## Methodology

The choice of 2.5 cm and 3.5 cm for the two treatment groups was a pragmatic one: it was close to values around 2 cm from the literature and it was necessary to have an easy intraoperative measure available. It had to be a measure practical in the intraoperative set up and easy to do, which would not necessarily have been the case for resection extents based on anatomical landmarks such as "to the level of posterior border of brain stem" or in relation to the choroidal point such as "10 mm behind the choroidal point" versus "20 mm behind the choroidal point". We were well aware of the fact that anatomic variation, the degree of hippocampal atrophy, the degree of temporal lobe atrophy, and variability in enlargement of the temporal horn would influence this measure.

It should be noted that the study design did not aim to compare a minimal with a large hippocampal resection. The intention was to compare an easy to perform well-sized resection with a large hippocampal resection. The similarity in seizure outcome for the intended smaller resection compared to the intended larger resection should not lead to the wrong conclusion that the amount of hippocampal removal is completely irrelevant.

Due to the prerequisite of creating two groups with a shorter and longer resection, cases had to be excluded where only a lesionectomy in the uncus or amygdalum was to be performed, but more frequently cases could not be included where due to the results of presurgical evaluation, a far posteriorly reaching resection was advised.

We do not consider the fact that lobectomies and SAHs, i.e., different resection types, were included in the study to be a disadvantage or to be able to devalue the results. It is meaningful to only vary the extent of mesial resection as has been done before by Wyler et al. (1995) [42] or to only vary the extent of lateral resection, which has also been done in a prospective randomized study [15]. The fact that TLR and SAH were both acceptable resection types in the study design reflects the reality of TLE surgery worldwide. Some epileptologists continue to prefer a variant of the TLR and others feel that a more mesial restricted resection type like SAH is possible in many TLE cases. Two of the three centers employed both TLR and SAH resection types

and a third center always preferred to do an anterior lobectomy. So, whereas in one center anterior lobectomy constituted 100% of all resections, in the two other centers, SAH constituted 73% and 82% of resections. More restricted resection types such as mesial lesionectomy or tailored resection were done in one center in 5.4% and in the other center in 4.9% of cases. Thus, the choice of resection types leads to two large patient groups with SAH and lobectomies. We feel this reflects well a certain trend mainly present in Europe to go away from standardized lobectomy. Whether the preference for one resection type in a specific center should be called a bias or whether this justifiably reflects the individual conviction of the decision making of the epileptologist based on the results of presurgical evaluation and his interpretation of the world literature is left for the reader to decide.

Center effects from patient characteristics appear not very likely since the demographic parameters of the patients were not significantly different between the centers. In particular, this appears to be true for several factors known to be outcome variables for seizure freedom after temporal lobe surgery, such as duration of epilepsy or presence of hippocampal sclerosis. Other center-dependent effects, however, are possible.

#### Limitations

The results of this study cannot be generalized for each and every type or of temporal lobe resection; in particular, they can only be applied for cases where a mesial resection was recommended which was not a priori decided to reach to the very posterior aspect of the temporal lobe mesially. Other limitations are that the results are not applicable for patients under 18 years of age. Another possible limitation is that the development after the 1-year period of follow-up available for this study may differently affect the resection groups with 2.5 cm and 3.5 cm. Since the study was powered to detect a difference of 20%, smaller effect sizes of 5% or 10% may not be detectable. However, these results can be used for the vast majority of temporal lobe resections which include an extent of mesial resection of the hippocampus–parahippocampus area classically used in a typical SAH procedure or partial lobectomy procedure supplemented by mesial resection.

The study hypothesis was that postoperative seizure freedom is independent from the extent of resection. In this study, we found no significant differences in seizure freedom between the two resection extent groups using the intent-to-treat analysis.

This is in contrast to the study of Wyler et al. (1995) [42]. Wyler's cases were all temporal lobectomies and only the mesial extent of resection was randomly assigned to his patients creating one group with a shorter resection (i.e., …"resected only to the level of the anterior margin of the cerebral peduncle") and one with a maximal resection (i.e.,..."more posteriorly to the level of the superior colliculus".). That study design constitutes a difference to the one from this study, where a "well-sized" resection (i.e., intended 2.5 cm) is compared with a large (i.e., intended 3.5 cm) resection. Maybe, the short length of Wyler's smaller resection is an explanation for the large difference in percent seizure freedom between his two groups, 38% seizure-free for the small and 69% for the large resection group. In contrast, the smaller resection in this study removed 72.6% of the hippocampus volume as a median value for the whole 2.5-cm group. It appears that the study designs were rather different with Wyler's small resection group aiming for a rather small resection volume and the larger group aiming for a maximal volume. In the present study, the difference between the two resection groups being unremarkable is possibly because the smaller resection group already removed over 72% of hippocampus volume as a median for the group. One might say that the Wyler study proved that a small resection leads to much poorer results than a truly maximal resection (at least by design) whereas this study demonstrated that maximizing the resection extent did not further improve seizure outcome. Wyler's study design also differed in so far as the extent of lateral lobe removal was planned to be identical whereas in this study, it varied in the TLR group. An important difference between Wyler's randomized study and this study is the assessment of true resection extent by MRI volumetry in the latter one. An amazing amount of variability in extent of resection (as demonstrated in Figs. 2 and 4) was found in this study and in all others that ever published volumetric results. It remains unknown for Wyler's randomized study, and one could argue that it is not so important to know since being an intent-to-treat study, results should only be evaluated per intent-to-treat. Acknowledging the request of several authors [15, 23, 25] for postoperative volumetric assessment of resection extent, we feel that the results of the previous study [42] with randomized mesial resection extent are less precisely supported than results from this study.

If discussing the necessary amount of hippocampal– parahippocampal removal, various schools of thought might be followed: (1) a small mesial resection is sufficient, (2) a maximal resection is best, and (3) the amount of HC– PHG removal needs to be sufficiently large enough to disturb the build-up and maintenance of a seizure event, but does not have to be maximal to achieve this. The results of this study, where maximizing mesial resection extent did not lead to an increase in seizure freedom, lead to the concept that it is not maximal volume resection that may

help to achieve optimal seizure freedom but the resection of an adequate volume of resection in the fitting area. Similar ideas have been phrased by Abosch et al. [1] who suggested that "…there is a critical volume of amygdala, hippocampus, and/or entorhinal cortex that must be resected to obtain optimal seizure control…".

Another argument that not maximal resection is the most important factor for seizure outcome can be derived from the fact that the many different centers worldwide doing temporal lobe resections achieve similar seizure freedom results despite various resection techniques and including centers who do not aim to achieve maximal mesial resection. How could it be possible that a sub-maximal extent of resection can achieve the same percentage of seizure freedom as a bigger one. Maybe, the reason is that enough fiber tracts are interrupted by a well-sized or normal-sized resection to inhibit the build-up and propagation of epileptic discharges. Abosch et al. (2002) [1] have proposed that "… it is possible…..that it is the percentage of afferent and efferent connections.....and not the volumes of the structures themselves, that is the critical factor in determining seizure outcome…". The latter argument has been used in the past in the discussion of cases with temporal reresection after initial failure, where a considerable proportion of re-operated patients did not become seizure-free despite maximal resection, for example by Germano et al. [11], and discussed in some detail in a recent review [33].
