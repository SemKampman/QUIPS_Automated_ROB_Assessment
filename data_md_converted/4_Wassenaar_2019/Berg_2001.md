# **CME Early development of intractable epilepsy in children**

## **A prospective study**

A.T. Berg, PhD; S. Shinnar, MD, PhD; S.R. Levy, MD; F.M. Testa, MD; S. Smith–Rapaport, MS; and B. Beckerman, MS

**Article abstract—***Background*: Little is known about early prediction of intractable epilepsy (IE) in children. Such information could help guide the early use of new therapies in selected patients. *Methods:* Children with newly diagnosed epilepsy (n 5 613) were prospectively identified from child neurology practices in Connecticut (1993–1997) and followed-up for the occurrence of IE (failure of .2 drugs, .1 seizure/month, over 18 months). Etiology and epilepsy syndromes were classified per International League Against Epilepsy guidelines. *Results*: The median follow-up is 4.8 years, and 599 (97.7%) have been followed for more than 18 months. Sixty children (10.0%) have met the criteria for IE, including 34.6% with cryptogenic/symptomatic generalized, 2.7% with idiopathic, 10.7% with other localization–related, and 8.2% with unclassified epilepsy (*p* , 0.0001). After multivariable adjustment for epilepsy syndrome, initial seizure frequency (*p* , 0.0001), focal EEG slowing (*p* 5 0.02), and acute symptomatic or neonatal status epilepticus (*p* 5 0.001) were associated with an increased risk of IE, and age at onset between 5 and 9 years was associated with a lowered risk (*p* 5 0.03). The absolute number of seizures and unprovoked or febrile status epilepticus were not associated substantially with IE. *Conclusions:* Approximately 10% of children meet criteria for IE early in the course of their epilepsy. Cryptogenic/ symptomatic generalized syndromes carry the highest risk and idiopathic syndromes the lowest. Half of IE occurs in children with nonidiopathic localization-related syndromes. Initial seizure frequency is highly predictive of IE. By contrast, absolute number of seizures and unprovoked or febrile status epilepticus are not.

NEUROLOGY 2001;56:1445–1452

Prospective population-based and other types of studies of remission in epilepsy have dramatically changed our understanding of the natural history of epilepsy, the need for treatment, and the merits of eventually stopping medications.1-7 Although most individuals do well, some develop medically refractory (intractable) epilepsy. Studies of remission do not directly address development of intractability, primarily because not being in remission is not necessarily the same as being refractory to treatment. This is especially true early in the disorder, particularly in children,8,9 many of whom are not even treated.10,11 No prospective studies, and few other types of studies,12-14 explicitly address the early risk and predictors of intractable epilepsy.

Because many of the large-scale long-term remission studies were started, the approach to studying epilepsy syndromes has become increasingly accepted.15 Because this approach is relatively new, many existing prognostic studies focused on components of syndromes (e.g., age at onset, cause, seizure type) and not syndromes themselves. We currently do not know to what extent the syndrome explains many of the associations with outcome (usually remission) previously shown to be important. No studies examine intractability with respect to traditionally studied prognostic factors for remission simultaneously with the epilepsy syndrome to determine the relative importance of the syndrome versus factors that are components of syndromes.

Early prediction of intractability would be valuable, because rapid identification of patients at highest risk would allow physicians to consider them earlier for treatment with recently approved medications and other surgical and nonsurgical treatments. **Methods.** Children were prospectively identified when *first* diagnosed with epilepsy by participating physicians (including 16 of the 17 child neurologists and selected pediatricians and adult neurologists) in Connecticut, January 1993 through December 1997. Active surveillance was used in each practice to identify all eligible children. Although our study is not strictly population-based, we recruited from academic centers, private practices, and community clinics to achieve a group representative of children with epilepsy in the state. Children had to experience the first unprovoked seizure between 1 month and 15 years of age, have at least two unprovoked seizures on separate days,16 and be newly diagnosed during the recruitment period by a participating physician. Written informed parental consent and written or oral assent of the child (as appropriate) were obtained. The Institutional Review Boards of all participating institutions approved all procedures.

Trained staff members interviewed the parents. Investigators reviewed all pertinent medical records. Methodologic details were presented previously.17 Study personnel conducted follow-up calls every 3 months. Medical records were reexamined at 6-month intervals.

We defined intractability as failure, for lack of seizure control, of more than 2 first-line anti-epileptic drugs (AED)18 with an average of more than 1 seizure per month for 18 months and more than 3 consecutive months seizure-free during that interval. Drugs had to be pushed to maximum tolerated doses and noncompliance ruled out as determined through medical record review and follow-up calls with the parents. Initial AED use in this cohort has been presented11 and reflects current accepted guidelines for practice.18 An inappropriate drug was rarely given (e.g., carbamazepine for absence epilepsy). We did not consider that to be a drug failure for the purpose of defining intractability. Children who discontinued two AEDs for adverse reactions and failed one other for seizure control also satisfied the medication criterion. We focused on the early occurrence of intractability with onset during the first 2 years. Four children who met criteria only after 4 or more years are not considered intractable for the purposes of this report.

Epilepsy syndromes and seizures were classified based on all information available at diagnosis and according to International League Against Epilepsy's (ILAE) guidelines15,19 by three pediatric epilepsy specialists (S.S., S.L., F.T.).17 The syndromic classification recognizes some syndromes that have been well characterized and studied with respect to prognosis. These include the localization-related and generalized idiopathic syndromes (e.g., benign rolandic and childhood absence epilepsy), generally considered to have an excellent prognosis and the cryptogenic and symptomatic generalized syndromes, generally recognized as having a poor prognosis.20 In addition, there are less well-differentiated syndromes. The symptomatic and cryptogenic localization-related epilepsies represent a broad spectrum of syndromes defined by cause and localization, to the extent that these are known.15 There is also a small group with both clearly focal and generalized signs (e.g., Landau-Kleffner). The outcome in this group is mixed. The last category, "undetermined and without clear focal or generalized signs," is essentially for unclassifiable epilepsies and includes children who have generalized tonicclonic seizures and normal EEGs.

Etiology16 was classified separately from syndrome although it partially depends on the syndrome. Remote symptomatic refers to the presence of an underlying neurologic condition or insult associated with an increased risk of epilepsy (e.g., history of bacterial meningitis, stroke, cerebral palsy).16 Idiopathic syndromes are almost always assigned an idiopathic etiology. Occasionally, a neurologic abnormality coexists with an idiopathic syndrome (e.g., childhood absence epilepsy with mental retardation), in which case we classified the etiology as remote symptomatic despite the idiopathic syndrome. Results of neuroimaging studies were used in classifying etiology. This has been fully described previously.21 Cryptogenic etiology refers to epilepsy that does not meet the criteria for an idiopathic syndrome and for which there is no identified significant underlying neurologic abnormality or condition. Such individuals appear to be otherwise neurologically normal.

Initial seizure frequency was defined as the ratio of the total number of unprovoked seizures since epilepsy diagnosis divided by the time between the dates of first unprovoked seizure and formal diagnosis. Every effort was made to assess the number of seizures as accurately as possible. Reasonable estimates were made when necessary (e.g., 5–7/week for 10 weeks was considered 6/week for a total of 60).

Chi-square and *t*-tests were used for bivariate comparisons. Log transformations were employed when necessary to normalize a highly skewed distribution. For some continuous variables, categories were constructed to facilitate presentation of data and testing of the assumption of linearity.

Multivariable analysis with the proportional hazards model permitted assessment of the relative importance of several factors simultaneously. The technique provides a rate ratio (RR), the ratio of the rate at which a group with a characteristic of interest develops an outcome relative to the rate of a reference group without the characteristic. An RR of 1.0 indicates no association; ,1.0, a protective association; and .1.0, an increased risk associated with the factor. Statistical interactions were considered, as needed, during modeling procedures.

A separate analysis examined predictors of intractability just in children with nonidiopathic, localization-related epilepsy.

**Results.** A total of 613 children were recruited into the study. The median age at onset was 5.3 years, and half were boys. The median follow-up is 5.0 years. Of the original 613, 599 (97.7%) have been followed for longer than 18 months, the minimum time needed to meet the study criteria for intractability, and hence are included in this analysis. Overall, 60, 10.0% of the total and 13.3% of those with nonidiopathic syndromes, met the criteria for intractability with onset during the first 2 years after initial diagnosis of epilepsy (figure).

We also examined age, sex, abnormal neuroimaging, history of neonatal seizures and febrile seizures, unprovoked, febrile, and "other" (i.e., neonatal or other acute provoked) status epilepticus, multiple seizures in a single day, seizure types, and selected EEG characteristics (table 1). Age at onset and log transformation of number of seizures, duration of epilepsy, and initial seizure frequency/ month, all before diagnosis, were examined. Strong, significant bivariate associations were seen for all of these except number of seizures (table 2).

*Multivariable analysis, full group.* On multivariable analysis, cryptogenic/symptomatic generalized and idiopathic syndromes, log initial seizure frequency, "other" (neonatal and acute provoked) status epilepticus, focal slowing, and onset age 5 to 9 years remained in the model (table 3).

There was unexpected interaction between cryptogenic/ symptomatic generalized syndromes, seizure frequency, and myoclonic seizures. In the cryptogenic/symptomatic generalized syndromes, a high initial seizure frequency was protective against intractability in patients with myoclonic seizures. The small numbers with specific syndromes within this group precluded determining whether this was specific to myoclonic seizures per se or due to selected syndromes. This finding necessitated the inclusion of an interaction term for myoclonic seizures and seizure frequency within the cryptogenic/symptomatic generalized group. Myoclonic seizures remained in the model as a main effect for the interaction term, even though its main effect was not significant.22 Detailed examination of intractability within this heterogeneous group is beyond the scope of this report.

Age of onset less than 1 year as well as febrile and unprovoked status did not approach significance. The rate ratio for the log number of seizures was less than 1.0, indicating a modestly protective effect. Duration of epilepsy no longer mattered. Almost all of the associations with age younger than 1 year, etiology, and abnormal neuroimaging seen in the bivariate analysis were explained by syndromic grouping in the multivariable analysis. The effect of having multiple seizures in a single day was entirely explained by initial seizure frequency.

*Intractability in localization-related epilepsy.* On bivariate analysis, several factors were associated with intractability in children with nonidiopathic localization-related syndromes, those in whom resective surgical interventions might be used if the epilepsy became intractable (tables 4 and 5). On multivariable analysis, the predominant predictor of intractability was log of initial seizure frequency (table 6). Focal slowing, neonatal seizures and "other" (neonatal and acute provoked) status epilepticus were statistically significant. Etiology, febrile seizures, febrile and unprovoked status, multiple seizures in a single day, and indicators for young age at onset did not approach significance. There was no association with the number of seizures before initial diagnosis.

To explore initial seizure frequency further, we examined just children with nonidiopathic localization-related epilepsy who presented with only two seizures (n 5 115). Children with multiple seizures in a single day were excluded. The interval between the two seizures (essentially the inverse of seizure frequency in this case) was 0.5 months in those who met criteria for intractability and 4.7 months in those who did not (*p* 5 0.0001).

Average follow-up since meeting criteria for intractability is 38 months. Only seven children have since experienced a 1-year remission 1.5 to 3.5 years after becoming intractable. Five eventually responded to topiramate (n 5 2), lamotrigine (n 5 1), vigabatrin (n 5 1), or the ketogenic diet (n 5 1). Another failed seven drugs and then responded to adenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). The last had idiopathic generalized epilepsy and ultimately came under control. Ten children have died, three of underlying neurodegenerative conditions, six of other medical complications related to their etiology or to uncontrolled seizures, and one of reasons unrelated to epilepsy.

**Discussion.** Identification of children at high risk of early intractability permits planning for early intervention. Available remission studies do not provide the specific information needed to do this, and little is known about the risk and predictors of intractability early in the course of pediatric epilepsy. Overall, 10% of our cohort met the criteria for intractability (13.0% of those with nonidiopathic syndromes) early in the course of their disorder. The three most salient positive findings are the overwhelming prognostic significance of syndromic groupings, seizure frequency, and focal slowing on the EEG.

The finding for the cryptogenic/symptomatic generalized epilepsy syndromes is not surprising. These syndromes are notorious for their poor outcomes.20 The absolute and relative magnitude of their effects has not been previously demonstrated in a large, community-based study. Also, over half of the intractability observed did not involve these syndromes.

Age and etiology, individually or together, were highly predictive of intractability in our overall cohort. The syndromic groupings, however, explained most of their predictive value.

The epidemiologic study of epilepsy syndromes has become a well-established endeavor.5,23-26 The classification provides a valuable framework for understanding prognosis. There are probably differences in the application of the classification by different investigators. For the classification to be truly useful, a high degree of uniformity and consistency in its use must be achieved across research groups and clinicians.

After syndromes, initial seizure frequency was the single strongest predictor. Few data address the prognostic significance of seizure frequency. A recent retrospective case-control study found a similar effect for ,1 versus .1 seizure/month but did not control for syndrome.14 Seizure frequency (,1 versus .1 seizure/month) has been examined in studies of remission4 and relapse after stopping medications.27 Our more detailed approach found an effect over a continuum of seizure frequency and even in those with only two seizures. This result was not explained by other factors. Few other data address this point. We note that two studies examined multiple seizures in a single day and found no influence on recurrence after an initial episode of unprovoked seizure.28,29 Conversely, in children with two seizures, an interval ,6 months between the seizures was predictive of having many (.10) seizures.30

The finding for seizure frequency contrasts with the lack of any increased risk associated with number of seizures. This challenges the theories of earlier investigators, who believed that seizures caused increasing degrees of damage to the brain and led to intractability.31 Most studies find the initial seizure number does not predict remission4,8,32,33 or relapse after stopping medications.34,35 Having more seizures before diagnosis probably does not improve outcome; however, the upper confidence limit rules out a substantial deleterious effect of seizure number per se.

Neonatal seizures and status epilepticus during the neonatal period or accompanying an acute insult were rare; however, when either occurred, the outcome was especially poor. Little is known about the prognostic importance of focal slowing per se.

The nonsignificant, slightly protective association between febrile seizures and intractability is consistent with other studies that have not found febrile seizures to be associated with seizure outcome in childhood epilepsy.8,12 Previously, we found no association between febrile seizures and partial epilepsy, partial seizures, temporal lobe epilepsy, and complex partial seizures specifically.36 This suggests that the association between febrile seizures and intractable epilepsy seen in surgical series is not a simple cause–effect relationship. Prolonged follow-up is needed to determine whether a second peak of intractability, perhaps associated with febrile seizures or with other factors, occurs later in life.

Although the risk of intractability was highest in cryptogenic/symptomatic generalized syndromes, half the intractability occurred in nonidiopathic localization-related syndromes. This should not necessarily lead one to take extraordinary measures in children initially presenting with a high seizure frequency; however, such children might be observed and followed more closely.

The lack of a standard, accepted definition is a challenge in studying intractability.37,38 Our study was designed to identify children who developed drug-resistant epilepsy early in their seizure disorder and who therefore might be good candidates for more aggressive and new therapies, possibly even early surgical intervention. This requires intractability to be defined separately from remission, because failure to enter remission may be attributable to factors that preclude the designation of intractability. Such factors include not being treated, noncompliance, and relapse after recent discontinuation of medication.8,9 In fact, 10% of patients in our study and 20% in another recent study were untreated during the first year after diagnosis.10,11 We did not consider patients intractable if they were not in remission, for these and other similar reasons. There is no single definition of intractability, and the topic is complex.38 Our definition addresses three fundamental considerations: medication failure, seizure frequency, and time. Other investigators have offered definitions of intractability,12-14,39,40 and all have considered these same factors in their definitions with highly comparable if not identical results.

Although some children with intractable epilepsy may eventually enjoy complete seizure control after many years,39 avoiding a long period of uncontrolled seizures would be preferable if other options are available. In the past several years, at least eight new drugs have been approved in the United States; others are currently available elsewhere or are in development. Vagal nerve stimulators have been approved, the ketogenic diet is a recognized treatment, and the indications for epilepsy surgery have increased. Experience with many of these treatments in children is still limited. Early identification would permit selective targeting of those at greatest risk and in whom the benefits would be greatest for early aggressive therapy first in randomized trials and later in routine clinical practice. Our data suggest that children at substantially greater than average risk of developing intractable epilepsy can be identified early.
