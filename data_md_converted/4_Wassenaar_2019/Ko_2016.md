Clinical Neurophysiology 110 (1999) 1245±1251

# EEG and clinical predictors of medically intractable childhood epilepsy

Tae-Sung Ko, Gregory L. Holmes\*

Department of Neurology, Harvard Medical School, Children's Hospital, Boston, MA, USA Accepted 15 March 1999

### Abstract

Objectives: To identify electroencephalographic and clinical factors associated with both seizure control and medical intractability in children with epilepsy.

Methods: We retrospectively reviewed EEGs and medical records from children with well-controlled epilepsy or medically intractable epilepsy.

Subjects: Features of the initial EEG and clinical ®ndings were compared in 39 children with well controlled seizures and 144 with intractable epilepsy using both univariate and multivariate analyses.

Results: Strong univariate associates were noted between intractability and several EEG factors: abnormal EEG background including diffuse slowing, asymmetry, abnormal amplitude, a high frequency of spikes or sharp waves, and focal spike and wave activity. With multiple logistic regression, independent predictors of intractability were diffuse slowing and focal spike and wave activity. Strong univariate associates of clinical factors with intractability included: an early age of onset, simple partial, tonic, and myoclonic seizures, a history of status epilepticus, a symptomatic etiology of the seizures, and abnormal magnetic resonance imaging of the head. Multivariate analysis detected 4 independent clinical features associated with intractable epilepsy: symptomatic etiology, tonic seizures, simple partial seizures, and an early age of onset.

Conclusions: There are a number of EEG and clinical features that can be identi®ed early in the course of childhood epilepsy that are predictive of outcome. These ®ndings will need to be veri®ed in a prospective study. q 1999 Elsevier Science Ireland Ltd. All rights reserved.

# 1. Introduction

Epilepsy is one of the most common, yet frightening neurological conditions to occur in children. In the United States, there are approximately 125 000 new cases of epilepsy each year; 30% of this group will be less than 18 years old at the time of diagnosis (Hauser and Hersdorffer, 1990; Hauser, 1995). Fortunately, most children with epilepsy do well when treated with an antiepileptic drug (AED) (Cam®eld et al., 1993; Berg et al., 1995; Carpay et al., 1998). However, a signi®cant number of children with epilepsy do not respond well to AEDs and continue to have seizures (Huttenlocher and Hapke, 1990). While this is a small group of children, the medical, social, and economic consequences of poorly controlled seizures can be enormous. In addition to the unpredictable occurrence of

Identifying children destined to develop intractable epilepsy early in their course may be useful in designing therapeutic options. For example, early surgical intervention may be appropriate for children likely to be intractable to antiepileptic drugs. In this study we attempted to identify clinical and EEG factors that predicted medical intractability in newly diagnosed children with epilepsy.

Epilepsy was de®ned as two or more non-provoked seizures. Medical intractability was de®ned as continued seizures despite adequate trials of 3 or more antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), used either alone or in combination therapy. A trial of AEDs was considered adequate if, in the opinion of the child's neurologist, the dosage was increased until there was either seizure control or signs of clinical or laboratory toxicity. A drug used to treat status epilepticus in the emergency setting was not counted as one of the 3 AEDS. A drug was not considered a failure if serum levels were subtherapeutic or there was documentation of poor compliance. Well-controlled epilepsy was de®ned as having no seizures for a minimum of one year during the follow-up period.

We included children who had been followed regularly for at least two years. Cases with shorter periods of followup or in which there was inadequate follow-up were excluded, with the exception of 8 patients who underwent surgery for catastrophic epilepsy within the two year period (3 with Sturge-Weber Syndrome; 3 with Rasmussen's encephalitis; one with Aicardi syndrome; and one with hemimegalencephaly). While we cannot be absolutely certain that the epilepsy would not have eventually resolved, we felt that the clinical and EEG information from this group of children with particularly severe epilepsy was warranted. We also excluded children who did not have EEGs as part of their initial evaluation. While the majority of children were initially evaluated at Children's Hospital, we included some cases who had their ®rst EEG elsewhere if the clinical information and laboratory studies were reliable and adequate. Although many of the children had multiple EEGs, we only used information from the ®rst EEG obtained after the diagnosis of epilepsy was established.

# 2.2. Medical record review - EEG features

Electroencephalogram recordings were obtained with a 21 channel electroencephalograph, employing scalp electrodes placed according to the international 10-20 system. In the majority of children, sleep and photic stimulation was obtained. When the child was cooperative hyperventilation was also obtained.

Diffuse slowing was de®ned as slower than expected background frequencies for the patient's age (Blume, 1982). Excessive beta was de®ned as greater than expected amounts of beta during the awake state. Asymmetrical background was de®ned as a 50% or greater difference in amplitude between the two hemispheres. Asynchronous background was de®ned as a 50% or greater asynchrony in sleep spindles or posterior dominant rhythm. Amplitude was coded as high or low based on published norms for the patient's age (Blume, 1982). Frequency of spikes or sharp waves was coded as greater or less than 1 per 60 s.

# 2.3. Data analysis

Data analysis was obtained using SAS software. Data were analyzed with chi-square and the chi-square test for trend for univariate comparisons. Fisher's exact tests were used for univariate comparisons when there were no cases in a category. Odds ratios, 95% con®dence intervals, and Pvalues were computed from univariate and multivariate logistic regression models.

In the univariate analysis we performed hypothesis testing for each of the potential predictors. This process involves a possibility of observing statistical signi®cance in some of the predictors purely by chance, because of multiple testing. One approach to this problem may be applying the Bonferroni correction. #### 3. Results

### 3.1. Patient profiles

The clinical profiles of the patients with intractable and well-controlled group are presented in Table 2. There were no differences of age at the time of the first EEG, duration of follow-up, and sex between the two groups. However, the age of seizure onset in the intractable group (mean = 2.9 year) was younger than that in the well-controlled group (mean = 5.2 year), and the risk of intractability decreased with age (OR = 0.83 per year, P < 0.001). Duration of follow-up and sex distribution was similar in the two groups. The mean numbers of AEDs tried were 8.1 in the intractable group and 2.7 in the well-controlled group, and the difference was significant (P < 0.001).

The number of sharp waves and spikes were also different in the two groups, with children with intractable epilepsy more likely to have frequent discharges (>1/60 s) than those children with well-controlled epilepsy. Surprisingly, multifocal EEGs were not associated with medical intractability. However, benign partial epilepsies, such as benign Rolandic epilepsy, with two or more independent foci, was classified into the multifocal group. This may be the reason why multifocal EEGs were not significantly associated with medical intractability.

Among the provocation procedures, photic stimulation was the only statistically significant factor (P < 0.01); it was seen more frequently in the well-controlled group. All 6 of the 38 (15.8%) children with photoconvulsive responses and well-controlled seizures had other abnormalities on the EEG; 4 had normal background and interictal spike and wave activity and two had abnormal background with interictal spike and wave activity. Multivariate analyses were performed to assess the association between intractable epilepsy and potential predictive clinical factors and EEG factors (Table 4). Diffuse slowing (OR = 2.59, 95% CI 1.05, 6.84) and focal spike and wave (OR = 5.21, 95% CI 1.25, 38.16) were independent predictors of intractable epilepsy, while frequent sharp waves and spikes (>1/60 sec) were no longer significant in multivariable analyses. Asymmetric background could not be included in the multivariable model because of the zero cell. Reactivity to the photic stimulation (OR = 0.10, 95% CI 0.02, 0.51) and 3 Hz spike and wave discharges (OR = 0.21, 95% CI 0.05, 0.74) were independent predictors of well controlled epilepsy.

### 3.3. Clinical features

A comparison of two groups in regards to clinical factors showed several differences statistically (Table 5). Simple partial, tonic, and myoclonic seizures were more frequent in the intractable group, while absence seizures were more frequent in the well-controlled group. Among the specific epileptic syndrome, Lennox–Gastaut Syndrome was found only in the intractable group, while the frequency of infantile spasm was higher in the intractable group, but failed to reach statistical significance (P = 0.09). Seizure frequency was higher in the intractable group. While status epilepticus was much more common in the intractable group, febrile seizures and family history of seizures were not significant. A symptomatic etiology and abnormal brain MRI were more common in the intractable group.

Among the other clinical factors, motor developmental delay, motor deficit, and mental retardation were found more frequently in the intractable group. Language development delay, microcephaly, neonatal seizure, and behavioral problems were reported more commonly in the intractable group but failed to demonstrate statistical significance.

Using multivariate analysis, symptomatic etiology (OR = 4.41, 95% CI 1.68, 13.00), tonic seizures (OR = 13.50, 95% CI 2.56, 249.74), age of seizure onset (OR per year = 0.86, 95% CI 0.75, 0.97), and simple partial seizures (OR = 5.11, 95% CI 1.21, 36.08) were independent predictors of intractable epilepsy, while status epilepticus and absence seizures were no longer significant in multivariable analyses (Table 4). The potential predictors, seizure frequency and brain MRI, were explained by the other factors (age of seizure onset and symptomatic etiology) in the multivariable model. Lennox–Gastaut syndrome could not be included in the multivariable model because of the zero cell.

Although our findings are preliminary and will need verification with a prospective study, we found strong unilaterate associations between intractability and several EEG factors: abnormal EEG background including diffuse slowing, asymmetry, abnormal amplitude, a high number of spikes or sharp waves, and focal spike and wave activity. Conversely, reactivity to photic stimulation and 3 Hz spike and wave discharges were predictive of good outcomes in regards to seizure control. With multiple logistic regression, independent predictors of intractability were diffuse slowing and focal spike and wave activity. Strong univariate associates of clinical factors and intractability included simple partial, tonic, and myoclonic seizures; a history of status epilepticus; a symptomatic etiology of the seizures; and an abnormal MRI. Multivariate analysis detected 4 independent clinical features associated with intractable epilepsy: symptomatic etiology; tonic seizures; simple partial seizures; and an early age of onset.

While there have been a number of recent studies designed to evaluate predictors of medical intractability in children, most have concentrated on clinical factors. In 3 large studies dealing with predictors of outcome (Berg et al., 1996; Cam®eld et al., 1993; SillanpaÈaÈ, 1993), EEG features were not considered. Since the EEG is routinely performed during the initial phases of diagnosis, it is somewhat surprising that the usefulness of the test as a prognostic tool has not been closely evaluated.

There have been a limited number of studies in which the early EEG has been compared with outcome. Shafer et al. (1988), in a study of 306 residents of Rochester, Minnesota, who developed epilepsy and had an EEG, found that the absence of generalized epileptiform activity was associated with epilepsy remission, de®ned as 5 years without a seizure. No other EEG features were found to be predictive of outcome. However, in this study, subcategories of generalized spike-and-wave were collapsed, and included atypical spike-wave discharges as well as generalized 3 Hz spike and wave. The authors found that focal epileptiform activity on the ®rst EEG was associated with a good prognosis, perhaps re¯ecting a high percentage of patients with benign Rolandic epilepsy.

The clinical features that were associated with poor outcomes were similar to the ®ndings of others and were not particularly surprising. It has been noted that the early onset of seizures is an unfavorable prognostic sign (Huttenlocher and Hapke, 1990; Berg et al., 1996). Berg et al. (1996) also associated remote symptomatic etiologies, infantile spasm, and a history of status epilepticus with intractable childhood epilepsy. SillanpaÈaÈ (1993) examined 40 cases with refractory epilepsy, de®ned as annual seizures for at least the last 10 years with no more than one year of seizure freedom, and 138 patients with better seizure control. Poor short-term outcome of AED therapy, occurrence of status epilepticus, a high initial seizure frequency, and remote symptomatic seizures were predictors of seizure intractability. In reviewing favorable clinical characteristics predictive of remission, Cam®eld et al. (1993) reported that seizure onset before 12 years, normal intelligence, lack of neonatal seizures, and fewer than 21 seizures before treatment were predictors of seizure remission. However, they did not include patients with absence seizure, infantile spasm, myoclonic, and atonic seizures.

Our goal in this study was to determine those clinical and EEG ®ndings that are predictive of medical intractability. It is clear that identi®cation of an epileptic syndrome can provide enormous prognostic information. In this study infantile spasms and Lennox±Gastaut syndrome were associated with poor outcomes while absence seizures and benign Rolandic epilepsy was associated with favorable outcomes. However, when a clinician ®rst sees a child with the onset of epilepsy it may be dif®cult to determine the electroclinical syndrome. This study provides clinical and electroencephalographic information that may provide the clinician with some guidance.

Some comments regarding the potential weaknesses of this study are necessary. We used a strict criterion for well-controlled epilepsy; that is, being seizure free for one year. It could be argued that children with occasional seizures are well-controlled. However, the goal of most treating physicians is to eliminate seizures totally. There is also a lack of consensus regarding the de®nition of medical intractability, and our decision to de®ne this group as failing 3 or more AEDs was arbitrary. However, children who fail 3 AEDs have only a 10% chance of achieving a remission of more than one year with subsequent treatments (Carpay et al., 1998).

The conclusions of this study are also limited by the methodology used. The study was retrospective and data was not collected in a systematic fashion. Our results should be con®rmed with a prospective study. Nevertheless, the ®ndings demonstrate the value of both the EEG and clinical features in identifying those children destined to develop intractable epilepsy.

Because our study was a retrospective study and Children's Hospital is a tertiary referral hospital, our patient population may be biased toward including patients with severe epilepsy. Nevertheless, the EEG and clinical features we identi®ed as related to medical intractability may be helpful in developing treatment strategies in the future.
