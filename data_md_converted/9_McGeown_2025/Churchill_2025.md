# Post-Concussion Brain Changes Relative to Pre-Injury White Matter and Cerebral Blood Flow

A Prospective Observational Study

Nathan W. Churchill,  $^{1,2,3}$  Michael G. Hutchison,  $^{2,4}$  Simon J. Graham,  $^{5,6,7}$  and Tom A. Schweizer  $^{1,2,8}$  Neurology  $^{\$}$  2025;104:e213374. doi:10.1212/WNL.0000000000213374

# **Abstract**

# **Background and Objectives**

Medical clearance for return to play (RTP) after sports-related concussion is based on clinical assessment. It is unknown whether brain physiology has entirely returned to preinjury baseline at the time of clearance. In this longitudinal study, we assessed whether concussed individuals show functional and structural MRI brain changes relative to preinjury levels that persist beyond medical clearance. Secondary objectives were to test whether postconcussion changes exceed uninjured brain variability and to correlate MRI findings with clinical recovery time.

#### **Methods**

For this prospective observational study, healthy athletes without a history of psychiatric, neurologic, or sensory-motor conditions were recruited from a single university sport medicine clinic. Clinical and MRI data were collected at preseason baseline, and those who were later concussed were reassessed at 1–7 days after injury, RTP, 1–3 months after RTP, and 1 year after RTP. A demographically matched control cohort of uninjured athletes was also reassessed at their subsequent preseason baseline. Primary outcomes were postconcussion changes in MRI measures of cerebral blood flow (CBF), white matter mean diffusivity (MD), and fractional anisotropy (FA), evaluated using mixed models. Secondary outcomes were group differences in MRI change scores and correlations of change scores with days to RTP.

#### Results

Of the 187 athletes enrolled in the study, 25 had concussion with follow-up imaging (20.3  $\pm$  1.5 years, 56% male, 44% female) and were compared with 27 controls (19.7  $\pm$  1.8 years, 44% male, 56% female). Concussed athletes showed statistically significant changes from baseline, including decreased frontoinsular CBF (mean and 95% CI –8.97 [–12.80, –5.01] mL/100 g/minute, z = -4.53), along with increased MD (1.94  $\times$  10<sup>-5</sup> [1.26, 2.69]  $\times$  10<sup>-5</sup>, z = 5.48) and reduced FA (–7.30  $\times$  10<sup>-3</sup> [-9.80, –5.05]  $\times$  10<sup>-3</sup>, z = -6.07) in the corona radiata and internal capsule. Effects persisted beyond RTP, although only CBF changes exceeded longitudinal variability in controls. For participants with longer recovery periods, significantly greater changes in medial temporal CBF were also seen ( $\rho$  = 0.64 [0.44, 0.81], z = 6.80).

#### Discussion

This study provides direct evidence of persistent postconcussion changes in CBF and white matter at RTP and up to 1 year later. These results support incomplete recovery of brain physiology at medical clearance, with secondary analyses emphasizing the sensitivity of CBF to clinical recovery.

# Introduction

Concussion is a major health concern, characterized by acute disturbances but also long-term effects on brain function. At present, clinical determination of postconcussion recovery is based on symptom assessments, combined with brief tests of cognition, balance, and visual function. There is growing recognition, however, that physiologic brain recovery may persist beyond the resolution of clinical symptoms, with MRI studies showing disturbances months to years after clinical resolution. This raises concerns about possible long-term effects of concussion on neural physiology, the consequences of which may be exacerbated by repetitive injury. 3,4

At present, most neuroimaging evidence for persistent postconcussion brain changes is based on cross-sectional studies comparing concussed individuals with uninjured controls. This approach, however, is confounded by baseline differences in individual brain physiology. This includes demographic effects of age, sex, and concussion history,<sup>5-7</sup> but also individual-level differences in neurophysiology.8 To control these issues, prospective analyses are needed that compare baseline and postconcussion imaging within the same individuals; but, such studies are challenging and remain rare in the literature. A series of studies examined a cohort of 45 hockey players, of whom 11 were concussed and followed from 72 hours to 2 months after injury, with persistent alterations in white matter microstructure, neurometabolites, and gray matter volume identified. 9-12 To date, however, brain changes remain under-examined relative to the clinical determination of recovery. Moreover, there is a need for more prospective studies that incorporate multiple MRI measures for a more complete picture of this complex neurologic condition.13

This study addressed this gap, using MRI to image a large cohort of varsity athletes at the beginning of their athletic season; those who were later concussed were reimaged, beginning at early injury and following them up to 1 year after medical clearance to return to play (RTP). Imaging included arterial spin labeling (ASL) to quantify resting cerebral blood flow (CBF) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) to assess white matter microstructural indices of mean diffusivity (MD) and fractional anisotropy (FA). While many MRI measures of brain physiology have shown persistent post-RTP effects, ASL and DTI are of particular interest because perfusion

deficits and microstructural white matter changes are considered key mechanistic contributors to acute injury (ACU)<sup>13</sup> and long-term neuropathologic change. <sup>14,15</sup> This is supported by a substantial body of neuroimaging research, <sup>16,17</sup> showing both acute and long-term postconcussion alterations.

From these parameters, we tested the principal hypothesis that (1) concussed individuals show altered CBF, MD, and FA relative to preinjury levels, with effects that persist beyond medical clearance. Secondary hypotheses were that post-concussion brain changes (2) exceed longitudinal brain changes among uninjured controls and (3) correlate with time to medical clearance (i.e., reflecting the severity of clinical outcome).

# **Methods**

# **Study Participants**

A cohort of university-level athletes was enrolled in this study, with recruitment and data collection spanning October 2014 to November 2019. Baseline recruitment aimed for 170–200 participants, given a target of 20–24 concussed athletes, with target numbers based on previous cross-sectional work. Athletes were recruited consecutively from multiple sport teams at the University of Toronto through the institutional sport medicine clinic (eTable 1 provides numbers by sport). Participants were approached during mandatory preseason baseline testing at the clinic and enrolled in the study provided that they met eligibility criteria, which included no history of neurologic disorders, psychiatric disease, sensory/motor impairments, or MRI contraindications. Baseline neuroimaging was conducted at this time, and clinical data were collected, including tests of symptoms, balance, and cognition.

Study participants were monitored by clinic staff over the course of their varsity career. Athletes who were later concussed were approached for reimaging, after diagnosis by a staff physician trained to assess sport-related concussion and confirmation that they did not exhibit gross postconcussion neurologic impairments. Follow-up imaging was conducted at early symptomatic injury (SYM), medical clearance to RTP, 1–3 months after RTP (1-3MO), and 1 year after RTP (1 YR). Early symptomatic imaging took place a median of 5 days after the concussion event (interquartile range [3, 6] days). Clinical data were also collected at SYM and RTP. Among athletes without documented concussion, a subset

was recontacted by study personnel at the time of subsequent preseason baseline testing, with follow-up neuroimaging and clinical data collection. While the concussed group included all eligible individuals with concussion after baseline scanning, the control group was selected to match on sample size and demographics. The baseline-to-baseline interval for controls was chosen to mitigate effects of within-season training load, which may substantially alter neurophysiology. <sup>19</sup> The absence of concussion between control scans was confirmed by coaches, clinic staff, and spotters at practices and competitions, all of whom were trained to identify concussion signs and symptoms.

# Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The study was conducted in accordance with Canadian Tri-Council Policy Statement 2 and with approval of the University of Toronto and St. Michael's Hospital research ethics boards (REB# 17–228), with all study participants giving free and written informed consent. This study was written in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.<sup>20</sup>

#### **MRI**

Neuroimaging was conducted on a 3T MRI system (Magnetom Skyra, Siemens Healthineers) with a 20-channel head coil. Acquisition and preprocessing details are provided in eAppendix 1. Anatomical imaging included T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo imaging ([MPRAGE]; sagittal acquisition,  $0.9 \times 0.9$  mm inplane, 0.9-mm slice thickness), fluid-attenuated inversion recovery imaging (FLAIR; sagittal acquisition,  $0.4 \times 0.4$  mm in-plane, 0.9-mm slice thickness), and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI; oblique-axial acquisition,  $0.6 \times 0.6$  mm inplane, 1.2-mm slice thickness) sequences. Structural images were inspected by an MRI technologist and later reviewed by a neuroradiologist, with clinical reporting if abnormalities were identified. No abnormalities (white matter hyperintensities, contusions, microhemorrhage) were reported.

Resting blood flow was evaluated by 2D multislice pulsed ASL  $(4 \times 4 \times 8 \text{ mm voxels}, 45 \text{ tag-control pairs}, TI1/TI1s = 700/$ 1800 ms), producing maps of absolute CBF in mL/100 g/ minute. The maps were warped into Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) coordinate space and voxels resampled to 3-mm isotropic resolution. Afterward, the CBF maps were parcellated using the Brainnettome Atlas, which subdivides cerebral gray matter into 246 cortical and subcortical regions.<sup>21</sup> The mean CBF value was then computed for voxels in each parcel of the atlas for further analysis, reducing the number of statistical tests and improving local detection power. For 1 concussed study participant, MPRAGE data were not collected at baseline. To process these data, a modified FLAIR-based warping pipeline was used (details in eAppendix 1). White matter was evaluated using diffusion MRI  $(2 \times 2 \times 2 \text{ mm voxels}, 30 \text{ diffusion-encoding directions}, b$ = 700 seconds/mm<sup>2</sup>), producing maps of microstructural indices including MD and FA. These maps were warped into MNI coordinate space and voxels resampled to 2-mm isotropic resolution. Afterward, the MD and FA maps were parcellated using the John Hopkins University ICBM-DTI-81 white matter atlas, <sup>22</sup> which subdivides cerebral white matter into 48 regions, and the mean MD and FA values were computed for voxels within each parcel.

#### **Data Checks**

Two rounds of outlier testing were conducted to identify (a) scans showing evidence of extreme head motion and related signal effects and (b) poor fit of perfusion and diffusion models, reflected in extreme CBF, MD, or FA values (eAppendix 1 describes testing details). For CBF data, two outliers were identified (control scans, at follow-up), and for MD data, one outlier was identified (control scan, at follow-up) while no FA outliers were identified. The outlier scans were excluded from further analysis of concussion effects. Further post hoc testing of head motion found no significant confounding effects for the main study analyses (eAppendix 1 describes details).

# **Demographics and Clinical Data**

For all participants, demographic information was collected at baseline, including age, sex, sport type, history of concussion (HOC), number of prior concussions, time since last injury, and recovery time from last injury. Clinical testing was conducted using C3 Logix, an iPad-based application that assesses symptoms (Sport Concussion Assessment Tool [SCAT] evaluation scale), cognition (Standardized Assessment of Concussion [SAC]), and balance (Modified Balance Error Scoring System [M-BESS]), with data collected at preseason baseline for all athletes, along with ACU and RTP for concussed athletes. Per evolving clinical guidelines, athletes were assessed with the most recent SCAT version at the time of enrollment (SCAT3 or SCAT5). For symptoms and balance, analyses used all data, focusing on the 22-item symptom list common to both test versions. Analysis of cognitive scores was restricted to SCAT5 data, which used 10-word lists. C3 Logix also includes computerized neurocognitive testing,<sup>23</sup> including simple reaction time (SRT) and choice reaction time (CRT), processing speed (PS), and Trail Making Test A and B (TMT A and B) completion times.

# **Analysis of Demographic and Clinical Data**

Demographic data were summarized for concussed and control cohorts and, for the concussed athletes, missing scans were reported. Baseline demographics, HOC characteristics, and clinical scores were compared between groups using nonparametric 2-sample Wilcoxon tests. For clinical measures, the SCAT domain scores (number of symptoms, symptom severity, cognitive score, balance errors) of concussed athletes were compared with their own preinjury baseline, at ACU and at RTP, using paired-measures Wilcoxon tests. Similar testing compared C3 Logix neurocognitive tests of SRT and CRT, PS, and TMT A and B completion times, at ACU and RTP, relative to baseline. For all tests, *p* values and approximate z-scores were reported

and significance was determined after adjusting for multiple comparisons at a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.05.

# **Analysis of Neuroimaging Data**

To model postconcussion changes in CBF, FA, and MD relative to preinjury baseline, the effects of imaging sessions on MRI parameters were estimated through linear mixed modeling (LMM), which handles missing data by maximum likelihood estimation. The model included fixed-effect changes in MRI values at postconcussion imaging sessions (SYM, RTP, 1-3MO, 1 YR) relative to preinjury baseline, along with random-effect subject intercepts. The model is coded as

$$y \sim 1 + SYM + RTP + 1-3MO + 1YR + (1|participant)$$

and obtains fixed-effect coefficients  $b_{SYM}$ ,  $b_{RTP}$ ,  $b_{1-3MO}$ ,  $b_{IYR}$ . The models were fitted using the Matlab's R2022a fitlme package (The MathWorks, Natick, MA) with bootstrap resampling, where a resampling unit consisted of all sessions for a participant. This produced empirical distributions of fixed-effect estimates b, with corresponding p values. For each session, brain regions showing significant longitudinal change were identified at FDR = 0.05 and regional effect sizes reported as bootstrap ratio (BSR) values (a z-score statistic of effect).

For each postconcussion session, effect sizes were also summarized by averaging MRI parameter values over significant brain regions; refitting a bootstrapped linear mixed model on average values; and then reporting a summary b value, 95% CI, BSR, and p value for the appropriate postconcussion session (denoted  $b_{SYM,av}$ ,  $b_{RTP,av}$ ,  $b_{1-3MO,av}$ ,  $b_{1YR,av}$ ). For comparison, effect sizes in the control group, which had 2 beginning-of-season scans (BOS1, BOS2), were similarly summarized. After averaging connectivity values over significant connections and fitting a bootstrapped linear mixed model on  $y \sim BOS2 + (1|participant)$ , summary statistics were obtained for the fixed-effect coefficient of BOS2 (denoted  $b_{CTL,av}$ ). The effect of adding model covariates adjusting for age, sex, HOC, and collision sport participation was assessed on these summary models, by calculating the difference in session-effect coefficients b, for linear mixed models with/without the covariates included; significance was evaluated by obtaining bootstrapped p values for these differences. None of the session effects was significantly altered at p < 0.05 (uncorrected), and these covariates were omitted from the final models.

To further assess whether postconcussion changes in CBF, MD, and FA exceed uninjured brain variability, cross-sectional analyses compared the longitudinal change scores of concussed athletes and controls, focusing on brain regions altered at RTP. MRI parameter values were averaged over regions with significant  $b_{RTP}$  values based on previous LMM analyses. The average values were calculated for every participant and imaging session, and then, group differences in mean change scores were calculated (denoted  $\Delta_{SYM-CTL}$ ,  $\Delta_{RTP-CTL}$ ,

 $\Delta_{1-3MO-CTL}$ ,  $\Delta_{1YR-CTL}$ ) with bootstrapped 95% CIs in 2-sample tests. Missing participant data were accounted for using bootstrapped multiple imputation, with linear mixed models fitted on average parameter values to simulate MRI data. The impact of adding covariates of age, sex, HOC, and collision sport participation to the imputation models was assessed as previously described. None of the between-group effects was significantly altered at p < 0.05 (uncorrected), and these covariates were omitted from the final models.

Finally, characteristics of the control group were further evaluated by whole-brain analyses. Paired-measures bootstrap analyses assessed longitudinal change from BOS1 to BOS2, and two-sample bootstrap analyses assessed group differences in baseline values between concussed and control groups, with significance determined at FDR = 0.05. Detailed reporting of significant ROIs for all analyses is provided in eAppendix 2.

# **Analysis of Neuroimaging and Recovery Time**

Given the substantial variability at RTP, secondary analyses tested for associations between postconcussion changes in MRI parameter values and recovery time. This was examined for the number of days to RTP, transformed into base-10 logscale to stabilize parameter estimates in the presence of heavy distribution tails because the values were initially found to deviate substantially from normality (skew = 1.8, kurtosis = 5.2), which was corrected by the transform (skew = -0.2, kurtosis = 2.7). Pearson correlations were calculated between log-dRTP and the MRI parameter change scores at each postconcussion imaging session, with bootstrap resampling to obtain empirical p values, and with significant regions identified at FDR = 0.05. Afterward, overall effects were reported for each session, by averaging MRI values over significant brain regions and refitting a bootstrapped correlation model, producing a summary correlation value, 95% CI, p value, and BSR. Exploratory analyses also examined the relationship between MRI parameter change scores and clinical values, with results in eAppendix 3 and eFigures 1–3.

# Discussion

There is ongoing debate about the extent that clinical indices reflect injury and recovery after a concussion<sup>1,2</sup> and whether biological recovery is complete at medical clearance. This study expands on previous prospective imaging studies,<sup>9-12</sup> by assessing CBF, MD, and FA of concussed athletes at preinjury baseline, early SYM, RTP, and up to 1 year afterward. Analyses show significant, spatially extensive changes in all MRI parameters at RTP relative to baseline, with effects that persist in later imaging sessions. Comparison with uninjured controls, however, shows that postconcussion changes only exceed normal longitudinal variability for CBF. Furthermore, changes in CBF alone show significant correlations with time to RTP.

The ASL analyses found nonsignificant CBF effects at early injury, but spatially extensive changes at RTP, which diminished at 1–3 months after RTP before again increasing at 1 year after RTP. At early injury, previous cross-sectional studies have reported differing CBF responses, including significant decreases, <sup>24</sup> increases, <sup>25</sup> and nonsignificant differences. <sup>26</sup> Such initial alterations in CBF, due to disrupted autoregulation and microvascular injury, contribute to impaired neural function and subsequent biochemical cascades. <sup>13</sup> The inconsistent effects may be due to sampling variability in days after injury, <sup>26</sup> given the rapidly evolving CBF response at early injury. <sup>13</sup> For post-RTP effects, results may be compared to previous longitudinal studies with cross-sectional comparison. <sup>18,27</sup> Both studies found limited CBF

effects in the first week of injury, as in this study, but also nonsignificant effects at RTP. For this study, the increased sensitivity at RTP may be due to the within-subject design. One study with imaging at 1 year after RTP<sup>18</sup> also found significant declines in frontotemporal CBF, corroborating the results of this study. Prolonged decreases in CBF are of significant concern, likely reflecting ongoing secondary injury processes including neurometabolic impairment, neuroinflammation, and permeation of the blood-brain barrier. Such perfusion deficits can in turn exacerbate injuries, for example, through decreased protein synthesis and greater oxidative stress, 29,30 contributing to long-term gray matter declines. Nevertheless, the greatest CBF reductions in this study coincided with RTP, despite a lack of overt behavioral decrements at this time, based on clinical testing.

The DTI analyses found significant increases in MD and decreases in FA, which increased in spatial extent and magnitude at RTP before gradually declining from 1 to 3 months to 1 year after RTP. Findings at early SYM are congruent with previous DTI literature in concussion and mild traumatic injury, which typically reports increased diffusivity and lower FA.<sup>26,31-33</sup> These effects have been interpreted as signs of diffuse axonal injury<sup>34</sup> although other contributors may include cytotoxic and vasogenic edema.<sup>35</sup> The weaker early response may also be partly attributed to edema, which can cause variable changes in microstructure and resulting diffusion patterns.<sup>36</sup> Regarding post-RTP effects, results may again be compared with previous longitudinal studies with crosssectional comparisons. 32,37,38 In these studies, disturbances in FA and MD in the first week after injury were generally unresolved at follow-up, which ranged from 3 weeks to 6 months after injury, despite clinical resolution. In addition, a more recent cross-sectional analysis found increases in MD and decreases in FA from ACU to RTP, with MD effects further increasing at 1 year afterward but FA being no longer significant.18 These findings broadly align with the current prospective analyses. These persistent changes in DTI parameters are believed to reflect chronic injury-related processes including delayed inflammatory response<sup>39</sup> and axonal degeneration.<sup>40</sup> Despite these microstructural brain changes, all concussed athletes self-reported as asymptomatic at RTP and had returned to normal activities. This again suggests limited behavioral effects at this time, although the subjective nature of self-report may contribute to some underestimation of symptoms.

While analysis of CBF and white matter indices of MD and FA all showed significant longitudinal changes after RTP, the comparison of longitudinal brain changes in concussed athletes and controls showed that not all changes were concussion-specific. For CBF, uninjured controls showed modest but nonsignificant increases over time, consistent with previous literature<sup>19</sup> and likely reflecting adaptation to exertion.<sup>41</sup> Postconcussion changes proceeded in the opposite direction and differed significantly at RTP and 1 year after RTP, indicating a distinct physiologic response. These effects were mirrored in the FA analyses, where controls showed a nonsignificant decline but postconcussion changes were not significantly distinguishable from this group. These results indicate that although MD and FA are sensitive measures of postconcussion brain change, they may not differentiate from other sources of variability among athletes. This may include the cumulative effects of physical exertion, along with physiologic and psychological stress, which influence factors such as neuroinflammation and redox state <sup>42-44</sup> that alter the microstructural environment.

Correlation analyses further examined the relationship between postconcussion changes in MRI measures and time to RTP, with only CBF showing significant associations. Previous cross-sectional analyses reported positive correlations between CBF and severity of the clinical outcome. This includes correlations between symptom severity and CBF in occipital, parietal, and medial temporal regions in early injury<sup>45,46</sup>; correlations between time to medical clearance and temporal CBF at 1 year after RTP<sup>18</sup>; and enhanced global CBF among individuals with persistent postconcussion symptoms.<sup>47</sup> The current prospective analyses align with these findings, with positive associations between medial temporal CBF and time to medical clearance, seen both at early SYM and after RTP. This suggests that abnormally elevated perfusion in these regions may be a biomarker of more severe clinical outcome. This is potentially a consequence of abnormal blood flow autoregulation after injury, which in some cases enhances CBF, <sup>48</sup> although other mechanisms such as neuroinflammation may contribute. Further work is needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms, for example, by measuring physiologic changes that precede CBF effects.

Although this study provided unprecedented insights into postconcussion recovery, some limitations should be acknowledged. There was longitudinal data loss, particularly at 1 year after RTP, which is relevant to assessing long-term recovery. Nevertheless, given large effect sizes and a lack of demographic or clinical correlations with attrition, results should be given careful consideration. Similarly, some clinical data were missing (neurocognitive tests) or could not be analyzed over the full sample (different version of the SAC); given uniformly small effect sizes for these tests, however, we may infer a lack of substantial cognitive deficits in the concussed group. For cross-sectional analyses, the determination of significant CBF and nonsignificant DTI effects depends on how normal brain variability was evaluated in the control group. This was obtained by comparing preseason baseline sessions with control sessions for within-season changes associated with exertional load. 19 Nevertheless, this does not exactly reflect the trajectory of concussed athletes, in which season play is interrupted (along with school, work, and socialization), followed by gradual return to activities. The control group may also have unmodeled variability, for example, undisclosed concussions outside the sport setting. Future work should aim to prospectively recruit a more directly comparable cohort without brain injury, such as musculoskeletal injury, given similar interruptions to activities and overlapping symptom profiles.

Given the challenges of prospective study design, the sample size was reasonable, but further studies must replicate the present findings. Larger samples are needed to probe heterogeneity in clinical presentation, for example, cognitive and symptom subdomains. These studies should also, where possible, use state-of-the-art imaging. For example, the pulsed ASL sequence in this study may be replaced with pseudo-continuous ASL<sup>49</sup> to enhance the sensitivity and reliability of CBF measurements. Other important considerations in future work include incorporating signs and symptoms proximal to the concussion event<sup>50</sup> and impact-monitoring devices<sup>51</sup> to better identify concussion events and evaluate the contributing role of subconcussive blows. Finally, this work focused on sport-related concussion, where homogeneous demographics and injury biomechanics improve study power; this comes at the cost of being less representative of other cohorts and injuries. Hence, it is imperative that future prospective studies also focus on individuals outside the sporting context, to better understand how effects generalize to the broader population. 52,53

# **Conclusions**

The multiparameter MRI data provide convergent evidence that physiologic brain recovery is incomplete at medical clearance, with significant ongoing alterations in perfusion and tissue microstructure. By controlling for baseline neurophysiology, this provides one of the most accurate characterizations of these brain changes to date. Findings in the absence of persistent clinical issues suggest that changes represent a functional "new normal"; it is not clear at present whether renormalization of brain physiology occurs on sufficiently long time lines, making this an important area of further research. Nevertheless, the presence of significant, long-lasting brain changes after injury reinforces concerns about the consequences of repeated concussions and to what extent these effects accumulate over time.
