#### ABSTRACT

The pathogenetic mechanism of persistent post-concussive symptoms (PCS) following concussion remains unclear. Thalamic damage is known to play a role in PCS prolongation while the evidence and biomarkers that trigger persistent PCS have never been elucidated. We collected longitudinal neuroimaging and behavior data from patients and rodents after concussion, complemented with rodents' histological staining data, to unravel the early biomarkers of persistent PCS. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) were acquired to investigated the thalamic damage, while quantitative thalamocortical coherence was derived through resting-state functional MRI for evaluating thalamocortical functioning and predicting long-term behavioral outcome. Patients with prolonged symptoms showed abnormal DTI-derived indices at the boundaries of bilateral thalami (peri-thalamic regions). Both patients and rats with persistent symptoms demonstrated enhanced thalamocortical coherence between different thalamocortical circuits, which disrupted thalamocortical multifunctionality. In rodents, the persistent DTI abnormalities were validated in thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN) through immunohistochemistry, and correlated with enhanced thalamocortical coherence. Strong predictive power of these coherence biomarkers for long-term PCS was also validated using another patient cohort. Postconcussive events may begin with persistent TRN injury, followed by disrupted thalamocortical coherence and prolonged PCS. Functional MRI-based coherence measures can be surrogate biomarkers for early prediction of long-term PCS.

#### **1. Introduction**

Concussion, a mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) caused by a blow to the head that make the brain to move and hit the skull, typically present with neurological dysfunctions such as headache, sleep disturbances, depression, and cognitive impairment, which typically resolve within a few months (Vanderploeg et al., 2009; Veltman et al., 1996). However, a subset of patients may have persistent postconcussive symptoms (PCS) over years (Alves et al., 1993; Chen et al., 2022; Corrigan et al., 2013), but the pathogenetic mechanism remained poorly understood. There is also growing concerns regarding the potential long-term sequelae of concussion including chronic neurologic issues related to stepwise neurocognitive impairment in later life (Chen et al., 2022; Corrigan et al., 2013). Hence, clarifying the pathogenetic mechanisms underlying various persistent PCS is essential to identify early biomarkers of prolonged PCS.

On the basis of a simulation model of shearing stress distribution within the brain following concussive injury, studies have proposed that rotational acceleration produces maximum shearing forces mainly distributed in the center of the brain (Mendez et al., 2005; Ropper and Gorson, 2007), also known as the peri-thalamic region. These forces have been reported to cause thalamocortical disruptions, which a human study show could last longer even than cortical dysfunction (Moeller et al., 2011). The thalamus is a centrally located relay station with nerve fibers projecting out to the cerebral cortex in all directions for information transmission. Thalamocortical interactions include both temporal synchronization between specific thalamic subnuclei and corresponding cortical regions and desynchronization between irrelevant areas (Maxwell et al., 2004). If such interactions are out of sync, multiple neuropsychiatric symptoms, such as neuropathic pain (Jeanmonod et al., 2001), tinnitus (De Ridder et al., 2015), Parkinson's disease (Magnin et al., 2001), schizophrenia (Schulman et al., 2011), epilepsy (Martire et al., 2019), and depression (Ben-Simon et al., 2008), may arise. In addition, thalamus has also been implicated to be related to the development and recovery of PCS from prior studies (ANDERSON et al., 1996; Mofakham et al., 2022). With its global long-range connections, thalamus plays a role in the neuroplasticity mechanism in the injured brain and can facilitate the improvement of PCS (Munivenkatappa and Agrawal, 2016). One previous study has also reported that patients with multi-year coma after traumatic brain injury exhibited significant improvement after thalamic stimulation (Schiff et al., 2007). In other words, the thalamic injuries may potentially lead to the failure in postconcussive neuroplasticity mechanism, resulting in the prolonged PCS. Despite the importance of thalamocortical interaction, and the negative impacts of thalamic injury on consciousness and cognition, little is known about the mechanism that sets off such chain of events that consequently lead to multiple neurological and psychiatric symptoms.

Since the thalamus contributes to a broad range of critical functions, the side effects of thalamic and/or thalamocortical damage can vary from person to person. We first hypothesized that 1) the extent of concussion-induced thalamic damage and the disturbed thalamocortical coherence should be quantifiable, and be able to explain not only clinical between-group differences (e.g., patient vs. healthy control [HC]), but also within-group variances (e.g., symptom-prolonged patients versus symptom-resolved patients); 2) as thalamocortical coherence is restored, so should one's PCS. To this end, we longitudinally recruited 70 patients with concussion and 70 demographically-matched healthy controls (HCs), and followed the patients for 2 years on their neuroimaging and neuropsychological data.

First, we aimed to test whether peri-thalamic injury could be observed after concussion and which one of the thalamic subdivision is most sensitive to concussion-induced shearing forces. These perithalamic microstructural injuries were assessed using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI). Because the histopathology, which is considered to be the gold standard for assessing the pathophysiological changes following concussion, can be difficult to obtain since patients do not die from such mild injury, we also constructed a translational rodent closedhead injury (CHI) model to mimic the clinical status of our patients after different degrees of concussion-induced shearing forces. Then, the histological staining would be applied to the sacrificed rodents to further validate the pathological changes assessed by DTI, especially at the perithalamic regions.

Then, to investigate the dynamic configurations of thalamocortical interactions in normal and different levels of disease states, here we used functional MRI (fMRI) to dynamically record the brain activity of both humans and rodents during resting state. Specifically, we investigated the mechanism of thalamocortical functional connections at both thalamic and cortical levels, and tested whether the extent of perithalamic damage can explain the severity of thalamocortical disruption. Functional connectivity was recently used to investigate disrupted thalamocortical functional interactions (Banks et al., 2016; Sours et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2014). However, reported results are inconsistent, with some groups demonstrating a decrease in thalamocortical functional connectivity after concussion (Banks et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2022), and others an increase (Sours et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2014). One of the key contributors to these conflicting results may be the inaccurate measurements of thalamocortical connections due to the delayed interregional signaling, particularly in patients after mTBI. Functional connectivity estimated through simple correlational analyses with zero-time lag is extremely sensitive to the interregional delays (Li et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2004); this connectivity may be more pronounced when inefficient signal transmission is caused by concussion-induced axonal damage, myelin damage, or both. In this study, we proposed to use temporal coherence (Fig E1 [online]) a delay-independent estimate (Sun et al., 2004), to investigate concussion-induced thalamocortical disruptions. Then, we determined whether the disturbed thalamocortical coherence is associated with PCS prolongation by correlating the quantitative thalamocortical coherence measures assessed at baseline with the long-term PCS estimates. If so, changes in thalamocortical coherence can possibly yield reliable biomarkers that are helpful for early prediction of long-term PCS after concussion. Finally, a machine learning-based predictive model was trained using the baseline quantitative thalamocortical coherence measures and long-term PCS estimates, and validated with external cohort data to demonstrate its predictive power for persistent PCS.

Their baseline assessments were collected within the first two weeks of injury, and follow-ups were conducted at 1 and 2 years after mTBI. This study was approved by the Joint Institutional Review Board of TMUH (Nos. 201504083, N201612008, N201904032, and N202102008).

The exclusion criteria were any history of prior brain injury, coexisting or previous neurological or psychiatric illness, and contraindication for MRI. Exclusion criteria for the HCs were the same as the additional requirement of no history of concussion or brain injury.

Glasgow Outcome Scale-Extended (GOSE) and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) were administered by a clinical psychologist during baseline assessment. # *2.2. Rodent resource and research protocol*

Twenty-four adult (8-week-old) male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats weighing 250–400 g were obtained from BioLASCO Taiwan (Taipei, Taiwan). They were randomly assigned to three groups (single CHI, double CHI, and sham control) for longitudinal and cross-sectional assessment (Fig E2 [online]). In each group, one rat was sacrificed 24 h after CHI for histological staining, whereas the others were administered MRI and behavioral assessments at the designated time points to dynamically monitor post-CHI behavioral and pathological changes; they were sacrificed after 7 weeks for long-term histological staining. Note that the rats sacrificed at 24 h after CHI were treated as test animals and not included in further comparison.

The animal experiments were performed in accordance with the recommendations of National Institutes of Health Guidelines for Animal Research's *Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals* and the *Animal Research: Reporting* in vivo *Experiments Guidelines*. The research protocol was approved by the Institute Animal Care and Use Committee at Taipei Medical University (LAC-2019–0358 and LAC-2020–0437). The details of the rodent research protocol are described in Appendix E1 [online].

### *2.3. Human MRI data acquisition and experimental design*

MRI data were obtained using a 3 T MR scanner (Siemens MAGNE-TOM Prisma, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 20-channel head coil.

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) was performed through echo-planar imaging (EPI) with two *b* values (1000 and 2500 s/mm<sup>2</sup> ) along 64 noncollinear and noncoplanar diffusion directions and 10 *b*0 images. The imaging parameters were TR/TE = 7500/59 ms, flip angle = 90◦, acquisition matrix = 256 × 256 × 50 with resolution = 0.86 × 0.86 × 3 mm2 . Standard single-shot gradient-echo EPI-based fMRI (TR/TE = 2000/20 ms, flip angle = 90◦, voxel size = 3 ×3 ×3.5 mm<sup>3</sup> , matrix = 64 ×64 ×40, 190 volumes) was also performed. Participants were requested to keep their eyes closed and not engage in any thoughts while remaining awake, alert, and as motionless as possible (Li et al., 2021). Specifically, participants were advised to avoid consuming caffeine, alcohol, or other stimulants, such as energy drinks, tea, or coffee, before the scan to ensure that they were well-rested and alert (Kawagoe et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2013). Additionally, they were advised to get adequate rest and avoid intense physical activity before the scan to avoid fatigue or drowsiness that might interfere with the resting-state measurement (Lee et al., 2013; Smitha et al., 2017). We also provided participants with practice runs before the actual scan to help them become familiar with the procedures and reduce anxiety or discomfort during the actual scan (Kawagoe et al., 2018).

For the coregistration and normalization of fMRI and DTI data, threedimensional T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo images (MP-RAGE; TR/TE/TI = 2300/3.26/1030 ms, flip angle = 8◦, voxel size = 1 ×1 ×1 mm3 , matrix = 256 ×256 ×176) were obtained.

#### *2.4. DTI analysis*

DTI data were preprocessed using FSL 5.0.10 (FMRIB, Oxford, UK) and MRtrix 3.0.2 (Brain Research Institute, Melbourne, Australia). Specifically, denoising, Gibbs ringing artifact removal, and bias field correction were applied using MRtrix and then processed according to the standard FSL pipeline (ie, brain mask estimation along with eddycurrent and motion correction). Fixel-based analysis of fiber density was performed using the MRtrix multishell and multitissue constrained spherical deconvolution method (Dhollander and Connelly, 2016) and the probabilistic streamlining method (Tournier et al., 2010). DTI-derived parametric maps, including fractional anisotropy (FA), radial diffusivity (RD), and track density (TD) maps, were generated and then spatially normalized to the template space (for humans: Montreal Neurological Institute template [MNI-152]; for rats: Paxino and Waltson Stereotaxic Coordinates (Paxinos and Watson, 2006; Valdes-Hernandez et al., 2011)) by using MRtrix.

#### *2.5. fMRI preprocessing*

The fMRI data were preprocessed using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12; Wellcome Department, University College London, UK) for slice timing correction, realignment, spatial normalization to template space, and spatial smoothing with a Gaussian kernel (for humans: 5-mm full-width-at-half-maximum; for rats: 0.5-mm full-width-at-halfmaximum). Linear and quadratic trends of the fMRI time series were also removed.

# *2.6. Atlas-based functional connectivity and functional coherence computation*

The human cortical regions of interest were defined using Brodmann area (BA) (Brodmann, 1909) at the lobar level and the thalamic subnuclei were delineated using the Talairach atlas (Lancaster et al., 2000). The six head-motion parameters and averaged white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals were partially regressed out of the preprocessed fMRI time series. The within-thalamic functional connectivity was estimated by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficients between the temporal filtered (0.01–0.08 Hz) fMRI time series (Biswal et al., 1995; Li et al., 2021), followed by Fisher *Z* transform. The magnitude-squared thalamocortical functional coherence (Sun et al., 2004) was calculated

using in-house MATLAB (version R2020a; Mathworks, Sherborn, MA, USA) scripts. Note that the frequency band selection in fMRI functional connectivity and coherence analysis can have a significant impact on the results, making the determination of the disease-dominant frequency range crucial for further analysis of the connectome matrix. However, the highly idiosyncratic nature of concussion led to differences in each patient's collision angle, position, and structural damage, resulting in variations in thalamocortical injury between individuals. Selecting the specific thalamic subnuclei or cortical region for disease-dominant frequency range estimation can, therefore, be challenging. To overcome this issue and determine the disease-dominant frequency range that most accurately reflected the disturbed thalamocortical coherence, we initially performed magnitude-squared coherence analysis between the averaged time series of the voxels from the entire thalami and each cortical region in the frequency range of 0.01–0.25 Hz (Zhou et al., 2014). Subsequently, the frequency range exhibiting significant difference in coherence values between patients and HCs was regarded as the dominant frequency range and utilized for further analysis.

# *2.7. fMRI-Based functional connectome matrix and connectome biomarkers*

Two connectome matrices were generated: the within-thalamic functional connectivity matrix and the thalamocortical functional coherence matrix. Specifically, the within-thalamic functional connectivity matrix was estimated from the temporal filtered fMRI time series of each pair of thalamic subnuclei in the disease-dominant frequency range. Similarly, the thalamocortical functional coherence matrix was computed between each thalamic subnucleus and BA cortical region pair. In other word, each element in the connectome matrix represents the functional connectivity/coherence value estimated between the specific region-of-interest (ROI) pairs (Amico and Goni, ˜ 2018; Smith et al., 2013; Turk et al., 2019).

Due to the highly idiosyncratic nature of concussion, which leads to differences in each patient's collision angle, position, and structural damage, the concussion-induced thalamic and/or thalamocortical damage can vary from person to person. Therefore, to quantify the level of changes in within-thalamic functional connectivity and yield a potential personalized disease burden of thalamic dysfunction, the withinthalamic functional connectome matrix was then simply averaged at the individual level as a compromise strategy. Similarly, the thalamocortical coherence matrix was also averaged at the individual level as a compromise strategy to quantify the degree of pathologically altered thalamocortical coherence. The averaged within-thalamic functional connectivity and the averaged thalamocortical coherence measure were treated as the connectome biomarkers.

# *2.8. Statistical analyses*

A one-sample *t* test was employed to determine the within-group significance in the HC and mTBI groups, whereas a two-tailed twosample *t* test was used to determine between-group differences. A twotailed paired-sample *t* test was applied to examine the significant differences between the initial and follow-up data. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to reveal the relationships between functional coherence and neuropsychological or behavioral changes. Moreover, we evaluated the linear relationship between the proposed connectome biomarkers and neuropsychological or behavioral outcomes using a generalized linear model (GLM), while controlling for confounds such as age and sex. The statistical tests were all corrected by controlling the false discovery rate (FDR) at *q* = 0.01 to prevent errors related to multiple comparisons.

# *2.9. Individualized prediction of the prolonged PCS by connectome biomarkers*

The connectome biomarkers obtained through baseline measurement, such as the averaged within-thalamic functional connectivity and the averaged thalamocortical coherence, along with patient's age and sex were treated as the possible features to train the support vector machine regressor with leave–one–out cross–validation on each prediction. The machine learning-based predictive model was applied using in–house MATLAB (version R2020a, Mathworks, Sherborn, MA, USA) scripts.

#### **3. Results**

#### *3.1. Demographic characteristics*

In total, 70 patients with mTBI (age = 37.9 ± 12.2 y; 47 [67.1%] female) and 70 demographically matched HCs (age = 36.7 ± 12.0 y; 47 [67.1%] female) were recruited (Fig. 1). The follow-up rates at 1 and 2 years for the patients were 42.9% (n = 30) and 24.3% (n = 17), respectively (Table 1); 19 and 10 of all patients had persistent symptoms at the 1- and 2-year follow-up (Table 2).

#### *3.2. Structural injuries after concussion*

#### *3.2.1. Clinical human results*

Fig. 2 demonstrates thalamocortical tracts injury and peri-thalamic injury centered around the thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN) on DTI. At the boundaries of the bilateral thalami, where thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN) was located, we observed a significant increase in radial diffusivity (RD) and decrease in fractional anisotropy (FA) in patients compared with HCs (Table 3; Fig. 2A: yellow arrows) at baseline as well as at 1- and 2-year follow-ups. These may indicate long-lasting axonal damage, myelin damage, or both in the peri-thalamic TRN after concussion. Furthermore, a significant decrease was noted in the white matter tract density (TD) of patients (Fig. 2B) at baseline, suggesting the presence of white matter tract damage. This tract damage was resolved within 1 year after concussion, indicating that tract damage is temporary and reversible compared with the long-lasting TRN damage.

#### *3.2.2. Translational rodent results*

Similar to the clinical results of our patients, changes in RD, FA, and TD were noted in the bilateral TRN of in our rats at 1-week follow-up (Fig E3A-C [online]), which sustained for up to 7 weeks (Fig E3D-F [online]) after CHI, suggesting a long-lasting axonal and/or myelin injury in TRN after concussion. We also revealed different degrees of TRN damage after receiving single or double CHI (Fig E3 [online]). By contrast, the damage to the thalamocortical tract could be found within the first 3 weeks of CHI and disappeared afterwards (Fig E3G-I [online]). These results provided converging evidence for the notion that TRN injury may last longer than white matter tract injury.

Histological staining of the test rat in each group obtained 24 h after injury showed different levels of increased GFAP immunoreactivity in the cortex and TRN after single or double CHI, as observed through visual comparison (Fig E4A [online]). These results are consistent with their corresponding DTI on structural damages, indicating the different extents of reactive astrogliosis produced by the neuroinflammation process after injury (Acabchuk et al., 2016; Kao et al., 2019; Mierzwa et al., 2015; Mountney et al., 2017). The neuroinflammatory changes in the TRN sustained up to 7 weeks after CHI, whereas cortical damage were less pronounced and nonsignificant compared with the sham controls after 7 weeks, re-confirming the persistence of TRN injury over the 7 days.

#### *3.5.2. Translational rodent results*

In rodents, long-lasting TRN injury was confirmed through DTI and immunohistochemistry (Fig E3 and E4 [online]). The long-term behavioral deficits assessed at 7 weeks after single CHI were significantly correlated with baseline neuroimaging biomarkers (Fig E5 [online]), including the extent of TRN injury and increased within-thalamic hyperconnectivity, and thalamocortical coherence. In the double CHI group, worse behavioral outcomes such as elevated mNSS (Fig E5 [online]), prolonged beam walking duration (Fig E5B [online]), and worse recognition memory performance (Fig E5C [online]) were observed from 24 h to 7 weeks after concussion measurements. Considering that the two CHIs were administered only 1 h apart in the double CHI group, these results suggest cumulative injury after repeated impacts within a short period (Kao et al., 2019), and the injury-induced behavioral deficits can persist for ≥ 7 weeks. Our results from rats provide translational evidence for the close relationship between baseline neuroimaging biomarkers and prolonged PCS in humans.

In addition to TRN injury, thalamocortical tract injuries may play a role in PCS prolongation. However, our results demonstrated that the thalamocortical tract injury revealed through DTI were only mild at the 1-year follow-up in patients (Figs. 2) and 3 weeks after CHI in rats (Fig E3 [online]). Furthermore, Fig E7 [online] demonstrated the nonsignificant correlation of the long-term behavioral deficits assessed 7 weeks after CHI with the severity of thalamocortical tract damage between the thalamus and impacted left primary somatosensory cortex of the hindlimb region (S1HL) assessed within 1 week of CHI; this suggested that the thalamocortical tract damage is not the main contributor of PCS prolongation.

# *3.6. Predictive power and external validity of fMRI-based coherence biomarkers for long-term PCS prediction*

To test the generalizability of the fMRI-based biomarkers proposed in this study, the baseline resting-state fMRI and longitudinal PCSQ data of 10 patients with mTBI were collected from another external patient cohort from Shuang-Ho Hospital (SHH; Table 4), and the predictive power of the baseline biomarkers for PCSQ at the 1- and 2-year followups was analyzed. The results demonstrated strong correlation between the connectome biomarkers and PCS at 1- and 2-year follow-ups (Fig. 6A-D), even though MRI acquisition parameters were not entirely identical between the internal (TMUH) and external cohorts (Table 5). The GLM analyses showed a significant relationship between the severity of long-term PCS and the two baseline predictor variables (i.e., within-thalamic functional connectivity [1-year: *β* = 84.21, *p* = .049; 2 year: *β* = 10.046, *p* = .036] and thalamocortical coherence [1-year: *β* = 101.96, *p* = .009; 2-year: *β* = 43.83, *p* = .002]), by controlling for confounding factors (i.e., age [1-year: *β* = − 0.02, *p* = .708; 2-year: *β* = − 0.02, *p* = .276] and sex [1-year: *β* = 0.46, *p* = .80; 2-year: *β* = 0.55, *p* = .34]). The models explained 90.4% of the variance in PCSQ assessed at the 1-year follow-up (R2 = 0.904) and 97.1% of the variance in PCSQ obtained at the 2-year follow-up (R2 = 0.971), respectively. These results thus confirmed the external validity and stability of these two connectome biomarkers.

Both the baseline biomarkers, along with the patient age and sex, were next used to train a machine learning-based long-term PCS predictive model using TMUH data and testing by the external SHH cohort data. It yielded high predictive powers for persistent PCS in not only internal but external cohort data at the 1- and 2-year follow-ups (Fig. 6E, F), again confirming the predictive power of the baseline biomarkers for long-term PCS (1- and 2-year PCSQ score).

In addition, this machine learning-based predictive strategy was also applied to the rodent data, which could also satisfactorily predict the long-term behavioral deficits (ie, neurological function, balance function, and recognition memory; Fig E8 [online]), further providing the translational evidence that these biomarkers can facilitate early prediction of long-term PCS.

# *4.1. Possible pathogenetic mechanisms underlying peri-thalamic TRN injury and disrupted coherence after concussion*

TRN, the only GABAergic thalamic subdivision, may serve as the brain pacemaker, which modulates thalamocortical synchrony (Min, 2010). In particular, neuronal dysfunction of the TRN can result in disinhibition of thalamic relay cells (Marini et al., 2002); this can lead to the abnormal synchronization of irrelevant cortical network and thereby interfere with the information transmission between appropriately synchronized cortical neurons, eventually causing thalamocortical dysrhythmia (TCD) (Llinas ´ et al., 1999). Conventionally, electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) are most frequently used to investigate the disturbed thalamocortical oscillation mechanisms in TCD (Llinas ´ et al., 1999; Llinas ´ and Steriade, 2006). The abnormally increased within-thalamic functional connectivity and thalamocortical coherence can nevertheless also be investigated through fMRI—as indicated by our results. This enhancement in within-thalamic functional connectivity and thalamocortical coherence has also been previously noted in patients with schizophrenia (Baran et al., 2019), Parkinson disease (Owens-Walton et al., 2019) and acute mTBI (Sours et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2014); it can be attributed to the pathological rhythmic coupling from thalamic loops with different functions (Llinas ´ et al., 1999). Our findings can also be compared with previous studies stating that the wide spread low-frequency (theta wave observed by EEG and/or MEG) thalamic rhythmicity can disrupt thalamocortical functional interactions, thereby producing negative symptoms noted in TCD such as sleep disorder, attentional deficits, fatigue, and depression (Llinas ´ et al., 1999; Llinas ´ and Steriade, 2006). Further, our observed positive correlation between elevated within-thalamic functional connectivity and thalamocortical coherence seem to provide additional evidence for Llin´ as and colleagues' (2005) notion of a predominant, pathological low-frequency thalamic rhythmicity in TCD patients coupling with the thalamocortical oscillatory system, which we think is responsible for the fMRI results in the present study. This predominant slow rhythmicity then leads to, and shows positive correlations with, symptoms such as sleep quality, PCS, and depression (Llinas ´ et al., 1999; Llinas ´ and Steriade, 2006)—consistent with our results. However, the relationship between the role of the fMRI-based biomarkers noted in our study and TCD biomarkers proposed by previous EEG and/or MEG studies remains unclear because our fMRI sampling rate may be too broad (TR = 2 s) and the neurovascular-coupled responses may wrap the neuronal oscillations into the low-frequency band (Lin et al., 2018). Thus, we emphasize that the pathologically enhanced thalamocortical coherence delineated through fMRI in this study may not be directly translated to the electrophysiological phenomena and neurobiological mechanisms. Further investigation using simultaneous EEG-fMRI to link EEG biomarkers with fMRI biomarkers in mTBI is needed to help unravel the complete mechanism underlying TCD and more precisely predict disease-related symptom progression.

# *4.2. Functional connectivity and temporal coherence of thalamocortical rhythmicity in mTBI*

The difference between functional connectivity and functional coherence can be observed in terms of signal transmission latency and hemodynamic response shape (Sun et al., 2004). In particular, functional connectivity estimated through simple correlational analyses with zero-time lag is sensitive to interregional signal transmission latencies and hemodynamic response shapes (Li et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2004), but temporal coherence is not. As such, if two brain regions demonstrate identical underlying neural activities—but with distinct hemodynamic responses, different signal transmission latencies, or both—their time series can reflect correlation differences but with high coherence (Sun et al., 2004). Therefore, on the basis of our findings, we suggest that thalamocortical network disruption in mTBI results from a combined effect of 1) inefficient signal transmission due to axonal damage, myelin loss, or both in the thalamocortical tracts and 2) pathologically enhanced thalamocortical coherence. This may also be the reason for the somewhat conflicting results noted in previous studies: a study reported a decrease in the thalamocortical functional connectivity of patients with mTBI (Banks et al., 2016), whereas two other studies reported an increase in temporal coherence or latency corrected-functional connectivity in patients with mTBI (Sours et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2014). The specific reasons for the differences in these findings may be related to the analytical approaches used in the studies, which may have probed different mechanisms of the disturbed thalamocortical coherence system after concussion.

#### *4.3. Limitations*

#### *4.3.1. Dropouts in longitudinal data*

The early biomarkers proposed in our study and the PCS did not improve significantly at the 1- and 2-year follow-ups, confirming that even mTBI can cause widespread, potentially long-lasting effects (Moeller et al., 2011). However, the considerable dropouts in our longitudinal data should also be considered, so the results can also be explained by attrition bias, meaning that perhaps only patients who still felt uncomfortable after 1 or 2 years continued participating in this study. Although the outcomes of every lost patient or why they left the trial are impossible to know, we could at least confirm that a subset of patients may experience persistent PCS that may be associated with peri-thalamic TRN injury and disrupted thalamocortical coherence.

#### *4.3.2. Control the consciousness state during resting-state fMRI scan*

Resting start fMRI is highly susceptible to participant-induced variability. In human, we used several pre-scan instructions to help ensure that the participants were in a relaxed, stable, and alert state during the scan. Specifically, participants were advised to get adequate rest and avoid intense physical activity before the scan to avoid fatigue or drowsiness that might interfere with the resting-state measurement (Lee et al., 2013; Smitha et al., 2017). However, we did not monitor or control the consciousness state of the participants during the scan. It is possible that increased drowsiness in patients with PCS may have contributed to our findings. For the rodents, we used a previously published protocol to prepare the animals (Hsu et al., 2016). However, it is important to note that the protocol only minimizes, but cannot entirely eliminate the interference of anesthesia. Prior to scanning, the animals received a 0.03 mg/kg, subcutaneous, bolus injection of dexmedetomidine, followed by continuous infusion at 0.015 mg/kg/h, while the level of isoflurane was gradually reduced to 0.5% (Hsu et al., 2016). Resting-state fMRI data were collected approximately 90 min after the initiation of dexmedetomidine infusion, at which time the resting-state fMRI signal was maximally stable (Hsu et al., 2016; Lu et al., 2012). Future studies need to carefully control the state of consciousness in both participants and rodents to enhance the accuracy and stability of resting-state fMRI measurements.

# *4.3.3. The cross*–*species differences and generalizability of animal model to mimic clinical status after concussion*

Here, we used a translational rodent CHI model, which could produce peri-thalamic TRN injury from impact-acceleration shearing forces similar to that in patients. However, even though the general trends regarding postconcussive alterations were similar, the process of the concussive injury and the results might not entirely identical to that noted in human pathology. Specifically, we used a fixed target impact region in our weight-drop model to control the experimental conditions, and two repetitive impacts apart with a 1-hour interval to modulate the

impact severity and avoid focal hemorrhage or contusion. The experimental conditions included here may be considerably different from clinical scenarios in which variations in the impacted regions often occur and could be essential for specific symptomatic and behavioral changes. In addition, only male rats were included in our rodent study. Previous studies have suggested that the male rats were used as the standard because the researchers believed that hormonal changes throughout the female cycle caused female rats to react differently to the same stimuli (Beery and Zucker, 2011; Wildner, 2019). This made their responses unpredictable and led to variable results during repeated experiments (Beery and Zucker, 2011; Wildner, 2019). Therefore, we used only male rats to reduce the variability caused by hormone fluctuation and increase the reproducibility of the findings. However, we acknowledge that excluding female rats may limit our understanding of how certain pathogenetic mechanisms affect both sexes. Further studies are needed to replicate these experiments using female rats to investigate the potential sex differences in this pathogenetic mechanism. Nevertheless, our rodent model could recapitulate our human study in that the persistent damage to peri-thalamic TRN, even in a very mild degree of injury, triggering the thalamocortical coherence disruptions, ultimately inducing prolonged PCS.

*4.3.4. Age and sex vulnerability to thalamic dysfunction after concussion*  Recent studies have indicated the importance of age and sex vulnerability in the context of mTBI because older people and women are particularly predisposed to persistent PCS (Bazarian et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2016; Levin et al., 2021). This is also true for our data, where the symptom-resolved and symptom-prolonged groups demonstrated differences in terms of age and sex (Table 2)—possibly connoting the age and sexual vulnerability factor related to acute peri-thalamic injury. However, the predominance of thalamocortical coherence in the symptom-prolonged group can be explained by not only severe thalamic dysfunction but also age-related changes in the hemodynamic response shape, which slows down the neurovascular-coupled fMRI signals (Chen et al., 2015; West et al., 2019), particularly in older people with cerebral vascular diseases (Carusone et al., 2002; Li et al., 2021). Further investigation corroborating these findings and identifying the effects of age and sex on thalamic dysfunction are warranted.
