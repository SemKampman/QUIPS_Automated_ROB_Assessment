#### **INTRODUCTION**

It is estimated that up to 3.8 million sports-related head injuries occur annually, the majority of which are concussions [Langlois et al., 2006]. Due to this high incidence, there is great interest in developing physiological-based biomarkers to aid in clinical management of concussion, which is largely dependent on clinical judgment [Giza et al., 2013]. However, basic knowledge gaps regarding the exact physiological consequences of sports-related concussion (SRC) have hampered biomarker development to date. Thus, there is a pressing need to characterize the acute neurophysiological effects of concussion, the time frame of recovery for these neurophysiological effects, and the relationship between neurophysiological recovery and typical symptom resolution.

One potential biomarker that has shown promise in mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) is diffusion tensor imaging (DTI). Evidence from work in animals [Mac Donald et al., 2007; Spain et al., 2010] and humans [Dodd et al., 2014; Shenton et al., 2012] demonstrates that white matter abnormalities are a common consequence of mTBI. One commonly assessed DTI metric is fractional anisotropy (FA), which measures the direction of water diffusion along white matter tracts and is thought to reflect axonal microstructure [Beaulieu, 2002]. While much of the work investigating FA abnormalities following mTBI has focused on emergency room cohorts with heterogeneous mechanisms of injuries (e.g., falls, motor-vehicle accidents, assault), interest in white matter abnormalities associated with SRC has increased in recent years [Gardner et al., 2012]. increased FA has been reported following SRC at the acute (i.e., days) and subacute phase (one-month) [Borich et al., 2013; Pasternak et al., 2014; Virji-Babul et al., 2013]. For example, increased FA was observed in the corticospinal tract (CST) and corpus callosum (CC) acutely and persisted at 6 months post-concussion relative to controls [Henry et al., 2010]. However, other studies have found no evidence of FA differences in the acute and sub-acute time periods post-concussion [Zhang et al., 2010; Zhu et al., 2015], while others have observed reduced FA [Murugavel et al., 2014]. Large-scale, prospective studies are needed to better capture acute changes and subsequent recovery of white matter abnormalities following SRC.

In addition to neuroimaging markers, there is also great interest in identifying fluid biomarkers for the clinical management of concussion [Zetterberg et al., 2013]. One potential marker that may reflect axonal injury and has recently shown prognostic value is tau, a microtubuleassociated protein that is specific to the central nervous system and found predominately in neuronal axons [Trojanowski et al., 1989]. Post-injury increases in tau have been documented in cerebral spinal fluid following severe [Franz et al., 2003] and mild traumatic brain injury [Neselius et al., 2012]. Recent studies have also observed increased tau in blood following mTBI. For example, increased tau has been observed in boxers following a fight compared with controls [Neselius et al., 2013] and following concussion in professional ice hockey players [Shahim et al., 2014], with acute tau levels predicting symptom resolution in the latter study. To our knowledge, the relationship between post-concussion changes in tau concentration and neuroimaging markers such as FA has not been established even though they both putatively measure similar pathology (i.e., axonal injury).

In this study, we investigated the recovery of FA across the acute to sub-acute phase post-concussion, the time period at which large-scale behavioral studies have documented the resolution of typical post-concussion symptoms [McCrea et al., 2003]. Serial DTI was collected from a sample of concussed collegiate athletes with a wellmatched group of healthy, contact-sport collegiate athletes serving as controls for potential confounds such as subconcussive head injuries and other non-specific factors (e.g., stressors, diet, physical fitness levels) associated with

collegiate athletics. Because we have previously reported increased FA in three independent samples of emergency room mTBI patients [Ling et al., 2012b; Mayer et al., 2010; Mayer et al., 2012], presumably reflecting cytotoxic edema [Bazarian et al., 2007; Povlishock and Katz, 2005; Wilde et al., 2008], we hypothesized that concussed athletes would exhibit increased FA relative to healthy athletes with evidence of partial recovery by the sub-acute time period post-concussion. Finally, given a recent report of increased peripheral levels of tau protein following SRC, putatively thought to reflect axonal injury [Shahim et al., 2014], we also conducted an exploratory analysis of plasma tau concentration in a subset of participants.

# **MATERIALS AND METHODS**

## **Participants**

NCAA Division I student-athletes (N 5 86) provided written informed consent and clinical data for this study following referral by sports medicine professionals. Institutional Review Boards approved all aspects of this study, and all participants self-reported no past or current mood disorders, anxiety disorders, alcohol abuse, or substance abuse. Behavioral data from a subset of athletes included in this study have been reported elsewhere [Meier et al., 2015; Singh et al., in press]. Forty concussed athletes participated in at least one of three visits occurring approximately one day (T1: 1.64 6 0.78 days post-concussion; N 5 33), one week (T2: 8.33 6 2.23 days post; N 5 30) and one month (T3: 32.15 6 4.75 days post; N 5 26) following SRC. Physicians trained in sports medicine diagnosed concussions independently of the study at the time of injury following recommended guidelines [McCrory et al., 2013] based on a clinical exam assessing symptoms, a cranial nerve check, manual muscle testing for strength deficits, the Rhomberg's test for balance deficits, on-field cognitive testing developed by the UPMC Center for Sports Medicine, and the King-Devick test. Seventeen concussed athletes participated in all three visits and 32 participated in at least two visits. Forty-six healthy, collegiate contactsport athletes (HA) participated in this study and served as a control group. Thirty-three percent (15 out of 46) of HA had a previous concussion, with the most recent occurring on average 36.3 months prior to study date (range 5–134 months, S.D. 5 39 months).

## **Behavioral Data**

Structured interviews for the Hamilton Depression and Anxiety Rating Scales served as the primary behavioral measures. The Automated Neuropsychological Assessment Metrics 4 Sports Medicine Battery (ANAM) was used to collect secondary measures of interest, including computerized assessments of self-reported concussion symptoms, mood scales, and cognitive tests [Cernich et al., 2007]. To

limit the number of comparisons, and because of the high correlation among many of the ANAM measures, composite measures of ANAM scores were calculated by comparing the correlation structure of these measures across healthy athletes. Overall symptom frequency, overall symptom severity, concussion symptom inventory frequency, and concussion symptom inventory severity were strongly correlated (mean r 5 0.91, range 0.85–0.94; see Supporting Information Table I), thus only the overall symptom frequency was used. ANAM mood scores were not assessed because the primary behavioral measures were structured interviews for mood symptoms. ANAM cognitive tasks include measures of simple processing speed (two simple reaction time tasks), information processing speed (code substitution task, procedural reaction time task), visual-spatial processing (spatial processing task), working memory (matching-to-sample task, memory search task, mathematical processing), memory (delayed code substitution task), and response inhibition (go-no-go task). Throughput, which is the number of correct responses per minute, was used to measure performance on each cognitive task [Thorne, 2006]. Scores on the cognitive battery were converted to standard scores by using a gender and age matched sample of collegiate athletes available in ANAM. Reference group scores for the go-nogo and the mathematical processing tests were not available and these measures were not investigated. All remaining cognitive measures were positively correlated (mean correlation r 5 0.44, range 0.24–0.82; see Supporting Information Table I), and averaged to create a single cognitive composite measure to reduce the likelihood of Type I error. Analyses of individual ANAM measures can be found in the Supplement to ensure that data reduction did not result in a loss of critical information. The ANAM Validity Indicator Report was used to flag cognitive batteries with questionable effort or scores on individual cognitive modules indicative of misunderstanding subtest instructions [Roebuck-Spencer et al., 2013]. These measures were excluded prior to calculation of composite scores. Likewise, athletes reporting zeros on all self-reported symptoms and mood scale questions (including positive measures) were excluded from analyses. Furthermore, not every participant completed ANAM testing. The final number of athletes included in the behavioral analyses is listed in Table I.

Finally, an independently determined, real-world measure of outcome was defined as the number of days that athletes were withheld from competition (i.e., return-toplay decision) by clinicians trained in sports medicine following recommended guidelines [McCrory et al., 2013].

## **Imaging Parameters, Processing, and Analysis**

MRI was performed using a General Electric Healthcare Discovery MR750 3-Tesla whole body MRI scanner and brain-dedicated receive-only 32-element coil array optimized for parallel imaging (Nova Medical, Inc.). Diffusion tensor imaging was performed with the following parameters: 30 non-collinear directions, b-value 5 1,000 s/mm2 , TR 5 8,800 ms, TE 5 minimum (average 78.5 ms, range 76.8–84.9 ms; see Supporting Information), acquisition matrix 5 96 3 96 interpolated to 256 3 256, field of view525.6 3 25.6 cm, slice thickness 5 2 mm, inter-slice spacing 0.2 mm, slices 5 69, acceleration factor R 5 2 in the phase encoding direction, original voxel size 2.7 3 2.7 3 2.2 mm. For anatomical reference, T1-weighted anatomical images were collected to using a parallelized magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo sequence (MPRAGE) with sensitivity encoding (FOV 5 240 mm, 130 axial slices, slice thickness 5 1.1 mm, image matrix 5 256 3 256, TR/TE 5 5/1.948 ms, acceleration factor R 5 2 in the phase encoding direction, flip angle 5 8 degrees, TI 5 725 ms, sampling bandwidth 5 31.25 kHz, voxel size 5 0.9375 3 0.9375 3 1.1 mm). DTI and anatomical imaging data were collected on 46/46 HA, 29/33 T1, 28/30 T2 and 24/ 26 T3 participants.

A previously described binary classification scheme was used to flag individual gradients with excessive motion or zipper artifacts [Ling et al., 2012a]. Participants that had more than 3 flagged gradients were excluded from DTI analyses (Table I for final n). Head motion during DTI scan acquisition was calculated as the Euclidean norm of motion parameters estimated using a 6-parameter affine registration of gradient images to each participant's b0 image [Jones et al., 2010].

The AFNI analysis suite was used for DTI preprocessing [Cox, 1996]. Gradients images were co-registered to the b0 volume using a 12-parameter affine alignment to correct for subject motion and eddy currents. The resultant rotational parameters were also applied to the gradient table. A 3.5 mm Gaussian full width at half maximum smoothing kernel was then applied to each diffusion-weighted image to reduce effects of Gibb's ringing artifact [Turken et al., 2009]. A non-linear algorithm was used to calculate diffusion tensors, followed by scalar measures of axial diffusivity (AD), radial diffusivity (RD), and fractional anisotropy (FA). Separate 12-parameter affine alignments were calculated to register each participant's b0 volume to their anatomical T1 image in native space and the native T1 to the N27 brain template in Talairach space [Talairach and Tournoux, 1988]. These alignment matrices were subsequently concatenated to create one b0 to standard space normalization matrix for each participant.

A priori region-of-interest (ROI) analyses included the following tracts from the Johns Hopkins University atlas [Mori and van Zijl, 2007]: the genu, body, and splenium of the corpus callosum (CC), and the left and right uncinate fasciculus (UF), superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF), anterior corona radiata, (ACR), superior corona radiata (SCR), combined anterior and posterior internal capsule (IC), and the cerebellar peduncles (CP). The inverse of the b0 to standard template normalization matrix was used to transform ROIs to each participant's native b0 image. Analyses were restricted to white matter by applying a binary mask created from the intersection between each ROI and a binary FA map (FA > 0.25) individually created for each participant. Voxel-wise analyses were also performed to examine FA in all white matter (restricted to voxels with FA > 0.25 from standard template). Non-linear registration was performed to align each participant's FA image to standard template space (FMRIB58\_FA) at 1 mm isotropic resolution using FNIRT from the FSL package [Smith et al., 2004].

Finally, a subject-specific analysis was performed to capture the number and volume of clusters with abnormally increased or decreased white matter FA in each individual participant [Mayer et al., 2014]. The mean and standard deviation of FA at each voxel was first calculated across the HA group using FA images normalized to the FMRIB58\_FA template. Next, each participant's normalized data were transformed to a z-score on a voxel-wise basis using the mean and standard deviation from the HA group. The z-scores were transformed to a distributioncorrected z-score (DisCo-Z) based on the number of participants included in the control group to control for known bias. Clusters with abnormally increased or decreased FA (volume and total number) were calculated for every HA and concussed athlete at T1, T2, and T3, restricted to white matter (standard FA template > 0.25). A minimum cluster size of 160 ll was used to reduce false positives based on cluster-level family-wise error correction (see below).

## **Statistics**

Count data (flagged DTI gradients, the number of prior concussions, number and volume of abnormal FA clusters) were square root transformed following addition of a constant prior to statistical analyses. Linear mixed-effects (LME) models with time as a fixed factor were used to investigate changes in time as a function of recovery. The AFNI program 3dLME was used to perform voxel-wise analyses on FA data [Chen et al., 2013]. For ROI analyses, three LME models (corpus callosum, left hemisphere, right hemisphere) were performed with time, ROI, and the interaction between time and ROI as fixed effects. For all LME, a random intercept was used to account for missing data with compound symmetry as the covariance structure. Monte Carlo simulations (10,000 iterations in resample voxel space) determined the necessary correction for family-wise error rate at P < 0.05 for all voxel-wise analyses (P 5 0.005, minimum cluster volume 5 160 ll) based on the average smoothness of (4.15 mm) of the detrended FA data. An identical volume threshold was used for subjectspecific analyses. Two-tailed independent sample t-tests characterized cross-sectional differences against HA. Multivariate analyses of variance compared FA in HA and concussed athletes at T1 in left hemisphere, right hemisphere, and corpus callosum ROIs.

## **Demographics and Behavioral Analyses**

Two-tailed independent t-tests showed that concussed athletes did not differ from HA in age or education (P's > 0.10). However, concussed athletes had a trend for more self-reported prior concussions relative to HA (t(84)51.76, P 5 0.082, d 5 0.38).

## **Exploratory Tau Analyses**

The effect of time post-concussion on plasma tau levels trended toward significance (F(2,26)53.18, P 5 0.058; Fig. 5). Follow-up tests indicated that T1 tau was significantly lower than T2 (P 5 0.019), while tau levels at T3 did not differ from T1 or T2 (P's > 0.10). Cross-sectional analyses indicated a trend for higher tau levels in HA relative to concussed athletes at T1 (t(35) 5 2.01, P 5 0.052, d 5 0.66), while tau levels in concussed athletes at T2 and T3 did not differ from HA (P's > 0.10).

# **Relationship Between FA, Behavior, and Outcome**

Exploratory Spearman correlations examined whether FA metrics at each time point post-concussion were associated with the primary behavioral measures, concussion outcome, or tau levels. FA in regions identified in the voxel-wise analysis were averaged to reduce the number of comparisons. The number of days that concussed athletes were withheld from competition was positively correlated with FA in the left SLF ROI at T1 (q 5 0.44, P 5 0.02) and T3 (q 5 0.52, P 5 0.01). None of the other correlations between behavioral outcomes or tau levels with metrics of increased FA were significant (P's > 0.10).

#### **DISCUSSION**

White matter abnormalities are commonly observed following TBI regardless of severity [Johnson et al., 2013], and represent a potential physiological target for the development of objective biomarkers for the clinical management of SRC. In this study, concussed collegiate athletes had increased FA at one day, one week, and one month post-concussion relative to a demographically matched sample of healthy collegiate athletes. Importantly, we observed no longitudinal evidence of recovery of FA in concussed athletes, suggesting that white matter abnormalities persisted beyond the time frame of typical behavioral symptom recovery commonly reported in the literature [McCrea et al., 2003] and observed in the current sample. Furthermore, increased FA in the left SLF was associated with an independent, real-world outcome measure of concussion severity at both one day and one month postconcussion.

Contrary to our hypothesis, we observed no evidence of longitudinal changes (i.e., recovery) in FA for concussed athletes across the acute (i.e., days) to sub-acute post-concussion (i.e., weeks to one month post-injury) phase. Rather, both voxel-wise and ROI analyses indicated that concussed athletes had increased FA at all three time points relative to controls. Importantly, increased FA in the left SLF was positively correlated with the number of days that athletes were withheld from competition at multiple time points, as determined by physicians trained in sports medicine. This supports the assumption that the cross-sectional FA differences reflect pathology associated with SRC and highlights the potential utility of DTI as an objective, physiological-based biomarker.

Previous meta-analyses have demonstrated that the location of white matter abnormalities following concussion varies across studies, though several of the regions observed in the current data are commonly observed, such as the CC, IC, and SLF [Dodd et al., 2014; Eierud et al., 2014; Gardner et al., 2012]. These inconsistencies between studies are likely due to the heterogeneity of mTBI in terms of initial injury conditions (i.e., fall versus motor vehicle accident versus assault), which motivated our use of methods that identify subject-specific abnormalities. Importantly, these analyses do not rely on the assumption of spatial homogeneity of injuries and thus are similar to measures of lesion load [Mayer et al., 2014]. In the current study, concussed athletes had a greater number and larger total volume of clusters with increased FA at one day and one week post-concussion relative to healthy athletes, with common regions of subject-specific clusters including bilateral SLC, IC, right sagittal stratum and CP. Although common regions were observed across athletes, subject-specific analyses may ultimately provide the most useful information for developing individualized treatment plans for concussed athletes given the heterogeneity of SRC.

The increased FA observed in concussed athletes was driven largely by a decrease in RD. Previous studies have hypothesized that this pattern may reflect cytotoxic edema because of the stretching of axons and neuroglia that occurs during mTBI [Bazarian et al., 2007; Ling et al., 2012b; Povlishock and Katz, 2005; Wilde et al., 2008]. This mechanical force potentially alters the function of gated ion channels, causing a decrease in extracellular water and an increase in intracellular water, which would lead to increased FA and decreased RD [Rosenblum, 2007]. Alternatively, gliosis has been associated with increased FA in gray matter following experimental brain injury [Budde et al., 2011; Xu et al., 2011], and could conceivably explain the increased FA in the current study.

Prolonged differences in white matter abnormalities, and specifically increased FA, have been previously reported following mTBI. For example, increased FA in the CST and CC was observed 6 months post-injury [Henry et al., 2010], and we have previously observed evidence of only partial normalization of increased FA in the CC and corona radiata (CR) at 4–5 months post-injury in emergency room samples [Ling et al., 2012b; Mayer et al., 2010]. Furthermore, cross-sectional studies have reported increased whole-brain FA [Virji-Babul et al., 2013] and increased FA in the CC and CR at approximately onemonth following SRC relative to controls [Borich et al., 2013]. However, the direction of FA differences following mTBI has been inconsistent [Dodd et al., 2014; Eierud et al., 2014]. The explanation for this inconsistency could be a result of numerous factors including sample characteristics and DTI imaging parameters, such as the number of diffusion weighted images [Dodd et al., 2014].

The fact that some neurophysiological consequences of concussion may persist beyond typical symptom recovery has important implications for the clinical management of concussion, as premature return to play decisions may put athletes at additional risk for subsequent, more severe injuries. For example, there is preclinical evidence that a subsequent temporally proximate injury may result in significantly greater axonal injury and behavioral deficits compared to a single injury alone [Longhi et al., 2005; Prins et al., 2010]. However, recovery of the neurometabolic sequelae of mTBI has been documented in the time frame used in the current study [Vagnozzi et al., 2010]. Similarly, we have previously reported that abnormalities in cerebral blood flow also recover within one month postinjury [Meier et al., 2015]. Therefore, prospective, largescale multi-modal studies are needed to track the various neurophysiological recovery profiles of the brain following SRC and to document its relationship with common postconcussion symptoms.

An exploratory goal of the current study was to explore plasma levels of tau in a subset of concussed and healthy athletes. Recently, increased peripheral levels of tau, putatively thought to reflect axonal injury, have been observed following an Olympic boxing bout and following concussion in ice hockey players relative to preseason levels [Neselius et al., 2013; Shahim et al., 2014]. In contrast, our data suggest that tau plasma levels were suppressed acutely post-concussion, with return to normal levels at one week post-injury. An initial decrease in both plasma and brain levels of tau has previously been observed at 6 hours following experimental blast TBI, with subsequent increase in tau levels at 24 hours post-injury [Arun et al., 2013]. It is unclear how these observed time-dependencies of tau translate to humans, though the aforementioned increase of plasma tau in hockey players was evident up to 6 days post-concussion relative to pre-season samples [Shahim et al., 2014]. Numerous factors could explain the inconsistency with these earlier reports, including sample characteristics, timing of blood draw, time since injury, and injury severity. Nevertheless, these results highlight the need for additional, well-controlled studies to validate the potential of tau as a biomarker for SRC.

## **Limitations**

Concussed athletes in the current study had more prior concussions than healthy athletes. This could partially explain the cross-sectional differences observed in the current study, as previous work has shown that a history of prior concussions is a risk factor for additional, more severe concussions [Guskiewicz et al., 2003]. Second, although all primary analyses were corrected for multiple comparisons, exploratory analyses were also performed examining relationships between FA and concussion outcome. A strict family-wise error correction would be overly conservative for these exploratory analyses due to the fact that many of the investigated variables were not independent. Nevertheless, we cannot rule out of the possibility of Type I error for these analyses. Furthermore, the primary real-world outcome measure used (return-to-play decision) may be partially dependent on the competitive schedule of the athlete (i.e., temporal relationship between injury date and the next game or end of season). Thus, this measure should be considered a general approximation rather than an exact assessment of outcome. While the current work focused on FA as the main measure of white matter integrity, future studies linking FA with alternative measures of white matter abnormalities, such FLAIR or SWI abnormalities, are needed to further elucidate the effects of SRC on white matter structure. Finally, although a well-matched sample of healthy contact athletes was included as a control group, baseline scans were not available for athletes that suffered a concussion. Future studies longitudinally tracking athletes pre-injury and at multiple time points post-injury are necessary to fully document

any changes from baseline. In addition, given the complexity and heterogeneity of mTBI, future studies with a priori hypotheses regarding associations between specific neurobehavioral symptoms (e.g., processing speed) with white matter abnormalities are merited.

#### **CONCLUSION**

Current results provide cross-sectional and longitudinal evidence of increased FA in the acute and sub-acute phase post-concussion in a large sample of demographically matched collegiate athletes. Increased FA was shown to have real-word validity for predicting concussion outcome, highlighting the potential of DTI as an objective biomarker for SRC. Importantly, there was no evidence of recovery in FA one month post-injury, suggesting that additional studies are required to determine when individuals have truly recovered from concussion.
